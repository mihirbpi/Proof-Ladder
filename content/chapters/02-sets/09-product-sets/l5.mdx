An **ordered pair** $(s, t)$ is two objects in a fixed order. The defining property is
$$
(a, b) = (c, d) \iff a = c \ \wedge\ b = d,
$$
**equality coordinate by coordinate.** That single line is the only thing you ever need to know about pairs.

The **product set** of $S$ and $T$ is the set of all such pairs:
$$
S \times T = \{\, (s, t) \mid s \in S,\ t \in T \,\}.
$$

## The example you already know

$$
\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^2
$$

*In words: pairs of real numbers are exactly the points of the coordinate plane.*

is the coordinate plane. Every point is an ordered pair of reals; the plane *is* a product set, and has been all along.

This makes several familiar facts into statements about products:

- A graph is a subset of $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ — the set of pairs satisfying an equation.
- $(2,3) \neq (3,2)$ because equality is coordinate-wise, which is exactly why axes must be labelled.
- The graph of a function is a subset of $\mathbb{R}^2$ passing the vertical line test, and Chapter 3 makes that precise.

## Order matters

$$
S \times T \neq T \times S \quad\text{in general.}
$$

With $S = \{1, 2\}$ and $T = \{a\}$:
$$
S \times T = \{(1,a), (2,a)\}, \qquad T \times S = \{(a,1), (a,2)\}.
$$

Different sets — no element of one is an element of the other, since $(1,a) = (a,1)$ would require $1 = a$.

This is a real difference from $\cup$ and $\cap$, which do not care about order. **The product is the first operation in this chapter that is not symmetric.**

<Warning title="Sets are unordered; pairs are ordered">
Module 2.1 said $\{a, b\} = \{b, a\}$. This module says $(a,b) \neq (b,a)$.

Both are correct, and they are statements about different objects. The braces build a set, where order is not data. The parentheses build a pair, where order is the whole point.

The notational collision is unfortunate and standard. When you see $(2,5)$, decide from context whether it is a pair or the interval of module 2.2 — nothing in the symbols will tell you.
</Warning>

## The empty case

$$
S \times \emptyset = \emptyset \quad\text{for every } S.
$$

A pair in $S \times \emptyset$ would need a second coordinate drawn from $\emptyset$, and there are none. So no pairs can be formed, and the product is empty.

The finite count says the same thing: if $S$ has $m$ elements and $T$ has $n$, then $S \times T$ has $mn$, and $m \cdot 0 = 0$.

## Products and intersection

> **Proposition.** $(S \times T) \cap (A \times B) = (S \cap A) \times (T \cap B)$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Elements of both sides are <em>pairs</em>. Membership in a product unfolds into two separate conditions, one per coordinate.</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion.</div>
  <div slot="how">The essential habit: as soon as an arbitrary element of a product appears, <strong>name it $(x,y)$ immediately.</strong> Until you do, there is nothing to work with; once you do, both sides unfold into conditions on $x$ and on $y$ that can be compared directly.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $p \in (S \times T) \cap (A \times B)$ be arbitrary. Since $p$ lies in a product, $p = (x,y)$ for some $x$ and $y$.

From $(x,y) \in S \times T$: $x \in S$ and $y \in T$.
From $(x,y) \in A \times B$: $x \in A$ and $y \in B$.

So $x \in S \cap A$ and $y \in T \cap B$, giving $(x,y) \in (S \cap A) \times (T \cap B)$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $p \in (S \cap A) \times (T \cap B)$ be arbitrary, so $p = (x,y)$ with $x \in S \cap A$ and $y \in T \cap B$.

Then $x \in S$ and $y \in T$, so $(x,y) \in S \times T$. Also $x \in A$ and $y \in B$, so $(x,y) \in A \times B$. Hence $p$ lies in the intersection.

Both inclusions hold.
</Proof>

**Naming the pair was the whole proof.** Every subsequent line is bookkeeping: unfold each membership into its two coordinate conditions, then regroup. Try it without writing $p = (x,y)$ and you will find there is no first step.

<Warning title="Union does not behave this way">
$$
(S \times T) \cup (A \times B) \neq (S \cup A) \times (T \cup B) \quad\text{in general.}
$$

Take $S = T = \{1\}$ and $A = B = \{2\}$. The left side is $\{(1,1), (2,2)\}$. The right side is $\{1,2\} \times \{1,2\}$, which also contains $(1,2)$ and $(2,1)$.

The right side is strictly larger, because it permits mixing a first coordinate from one product with a second from the other. Intersection has no such freedom — it demands both conditions at once — which is why that case worked and this one does not.

**One law holding is not evidence for its partner.** Test the variant before assuming it.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Products are how mathematics builds things with several inputs.

A function of two variables has a product as its domain: $f : \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ takes one argument, which happens to be a pair. That is why Chapter 3's whole theory applies to multi-variable functions without any changes — nothing there assumes the domain is a set of numbers.

The construction also iterates. $\mathbb{R}^3$ is the set of ordered triples, and $\mathbb{R}^n$ the set of $n$-tuples. Every coordinate system you will ever use is a product set.
</Aside>
