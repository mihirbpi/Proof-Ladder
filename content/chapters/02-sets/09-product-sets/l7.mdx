## Ordered pairs

The ordered pair is characterised by a single property:
$$
(a,b) = (c,d) \iff a = c \wedge b = d. \tag{1}
$$

Anything satisfying (1) will do; the standard implementation is Kuratowski's,
$$
(a,b) := \{\{a\}, \{a,b\}\},
$$
from which (1) is a short exercise in being fixed by its members. The implementation is deliberately forgettable — what matters is (1), and a different encoding satisfying it would serve equally well. This is the first appearance of a pattern that dominates modern mathematics: objects are characterised by the properties they satisfy, not by what they are made of.

## The cartesian product

$$
S \times T = \{(s,t) \mid s \in S \wedge t \in T\},
\qquad
(x,y) \in S \times T \iff x \in S \wedge y \in T. \tag{2}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\wedge$ is "and", the bar $\mid$ is "such that", $\times$ pairs things up. So the line reads: the pairs with first entry from $S$ and second from $T$ — and a pair belongs exactly when each entry belongs where it should.*

Basic facts, all immediate:

- $|S \times T| = |S|\,|T|$ for finite sets — multiplication as the size of a grid.
- $S \times T \neq T \times S$ unless $S = T$ or one is empty; (1) is what obstructs the identification. There is nevertheless a standard matching $S \times T \to T \times S$, and much of the time that is all one needs.
- $S \times \emptyset = \emptyset$: the second coordinate has nothing to be.
- $\times$ is not associative on the nose either — $(a,(b,c))$, $((a,b),c)$ and $(a,b,c)$ are formally distinct — but the standard matchings between them are so routine that everyone writes $S \times T \times R$ and moves on.

<Warning title="Name an arbitrary element as a pair, immediately">
Every proof about products opens the same way:

> Let $z \in A \times C$ be arbitrary. Then $z = (x,y)$ with $x \in A$ and $y \in C$.

Until $z$ is decomposed, (2) cannot be applied and the argument has nothing to work with. Failing to decompose is the characteristic error in this module, as failing to unfold a definition is in the previous ones.
</Warning>

## Interaction with intersection

> **Proposition.** $(A \cap B) \times (C \cap D) = (A \times C) \cap (B \times D)$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Both sides unfold, via (2) and the definition of $\cap$, to a conjunction of the same four atomic statements.</div>
  <div slot="want">Set equality.</div>
  <div slot="how">Decompose an arbitrary element into coordinates, unfold both sides, and observe that they differ only by the order of conjuncts. Commutativity and associativity of $\wedge$ finish it, and since every step is biconditional the two inclusions come together.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Every element of either side is an ordered pair, so let $(x,y)$ be arbitrary. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
(x,y) \in (A\cap B)\times(C\cap D)
&\iff (x \in A \wedge x \in B)\wedge(y \in C \wedge y \in D)\\
&\iff (x \in A \wedge y \in C)\wedge(x \in B \wedge y \in D)\\
&\iff (x,y) \in (A\times C)\cap(B\times D),
\end{aligned}
$$
the middle step by commutativity and associativity of $\wedge$.
</Proof>

The theorem is commutativity of conjunction in costume. Recognising that is more valuable than the theorem.

## Why the union analogue fails

$$
(A \cup B)\times(C \cup D) \;\supsetneq\; (A \times C)\cup(B\times D) \quad\text{in general.}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\times$ pairs things up. So the line reads: pairing two pooled collections gives strictly more pairs than pooling the two pairings; the mixed pairs are the extras.*

With $A = C = \{1\}$, $B = D = \{2\}$: the left contains $(1,2)$; the right is $\{(1,1),(2,2)\}$. The containment $\supseteq$ always holds, and the failure of $\subseteq$ is exactly that a pair may draw its coordinates from different summands. The correct identity is
$$
(A\cup B)\times(C\cup D) = (A\times C)\cup(A\times D)\cup(B\times C)\cup(B\times D),
$$
which distributes properly — and whose four terms are the cross-terms the naive version omits. Meanwhile $A \times (C \cup D) = (A\times C)\cup(A\times D)$ does hold, so $\times$ distributes over $\cup$ in each variable separately; it is only the simultaneous version that fails.

## Relations

A **relation** from $S$ to $T$ is by definition any subset $R \subseteq S \times T$, with $sRt$ meaning $(s,t) \in R$. This is the definition that makes the rest of the course possible:

- **Functions** (Chapter 3) are relations $f \subseteq S \times T$ such that each $s \in S$ occurs as the first coordinate of exactly one pair. A function *is* its graph; there is no separate notion.
- **Orders** are relations on $S \times S$ that are reflexive, such that mutual containment forces equality, transitive — $\subseteq$ from §2.3 being the example at hand.
- **Equivalence relations** are reflexive, symmetric, transitive relations, and their quotients construct $\mathbb{Z}$ from $\mathbb{N}$, $\mathbb{Q}$ from $\mathbb{Z}$, and $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ from $\mathbb{Z}$.

So everything from here forward is a subset of a product set with a condition attached.

$\mathbb{R}^2 = \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R}$ makes this concrete: a line is $\{(x,y) \mid y = mx+c\}$, a circle is $\{(x,y) \mid x^2+y^2 = r^2\}$. Coordinate geometry is set-builder notation applied to a product.

<Aside>
Infinite products require care. For a family $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$, $\prod_{i \in I} A_i$ is the set of functions $f$ on $I$ with $f(i) \in A_i$ — note the definition uses functions, so the order of development in this course is a pedagogical convenience rather than a logical necessity. Whether this product is nonempty whenever every $A_i$ is nonempty is a genuine assumption in its own right, independent of the other axioms of set theory.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Products are the sets both courses ahead actually work in.

**$\mathbb{R}^n$ is a product.** $\mathbb{R}^2 = \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{R}^3 = \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$, and a vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ *is* an ordered $n$-tuple. The defining property of ordered pairs — equal exactly when the entries match — is what makes coordinates well defined, and it is why $\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ unpacks into $n$ separate scalar equations.

**In calculus, a function of two variables has a product for its domain.** $f(x,y)$ is a function $D \to \mathbb{R}$ with $D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^2$, and the graph is a subset of $\mathbb{R}^3 = \mathbb{R}^2\times\mathbb{R}$. Level curves are pre-images (§3.3); partial derivatives are ordinary derivatives of restrictions along the two factors.

Even in one variable the product is present: the graph of $f$ is $\{(x, f(x)) : x \in \operatorname{dom} f\}\subseteq \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$, which is §3.1's definition of a function as a set of pairs. **The picture you have been drawing since algebra is a subset of a product**, and the vertical line test is the single-valuedness condition read off that subset.

</WhereThisGoes>
