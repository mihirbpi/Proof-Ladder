Module 2.1 established that sets forget order: $\{3, 5\} = \{5, 3\}$.

Coordinate geometry cannot afford that. The point $(3,5)$ is not the point $(5,3)$, and every theorem you have proved with coordinates depends on the difference. So we need a second kind of object — one that remembers.

## Ordered pairs

An **ordered pair** $(a, b)$ has a first entry and a second entry. Its defining property is:

$$
(a,b) = (c,d) \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a = c \ \text{ and } \ b = d.
$$

That biconditional is the whole definition, and it is exactly what your coordinate work assumes. When you prove two points coincide, you prove their $x$-coordinates agree **and** their $y$-coordinates agree — two obligations, because the definition has two conjuncts.

<Warning title="Braces forget, parentheses remember">
$$\{3,5\} = \{5,3\} \qquad\text{but}\qquad (3,5) \neq (5,3).$$

$$\{3,3\} = \{3\} \qquad\text{but}\qquad (3,3) \text{ has two entries.}$$

A set is decided by *what* is in it. An ordered pair is decided by what is in it *and where*. The vertex set $\{A,B,C\}$ of a triangle and the congruence correspondence $A\leftrightarrow D$, $B \leftrightarrow E$, $C\leftrightarrow F$ are the two objects side by side — one forgetting order, one keeping it.
</Warning>

## The product set

$$
S \times T = \{\,(s,t) : s \in S \ \text{ and }\ t \in T\,\}.
$$

Every first entry from $S$, every second from $T$, in every combination.

$$\{1,2,3\}\times\{a,b\} = \{(1,a),(1,b),(2,a),(2,b),(3,a),(3,b)\}.$$

Six pairs, from a $3$-element set and a $2$-element set. In general
$$|S \times T| = |S|\cdot|T|,$$
which is where the *product* in the name comes from — and which is the same multiplication principle you use for counting outcomes.

Note that $S\times T \neq T\times S$ in general: $(1,a)$ is in one and not the other. Order matters at both levels.

## The coordinate plane is a product

$$
\mathbb{R}^2 = \mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R} = \{\,(x,y) : x \in \mathbb{R}, \ y \in \mathbb{R}\,\}.
$$

This is not an analogy. **The plane, for the purposes of coordinate geometry, is literally this set of ordered pairs**, and every figure you graph is a subset of it:

$$\text{line} = \{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : y = mx+b\}$$
$$\text{circle} = \{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^2 : (x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2\}$$

Two coordinates because the plane is a product of two copies of $\mathbb{R}$; three for space, $\mathbb{R}^3$. The dimension is the number of factors.

Descartes' insight in 1637 was exactly this identification, and it is what makes a coordinate proof possible at all: geometric statements become algebraic ones because points *are* pairs of numbers.

<Aside>
Products give you a clean way to talk about things geometry usually leaves informal.

A **congruence** $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF$ asserts a correspondence — a set of ordered pairs
$$\{(A,D),(B,E),(C,F)\} \subseteq \{A,B,C\}\times\{D,E,F\},$$
together with the claim that corresponding parts are congruent. A *relation* between two sets is by definition a subset of their product, and Chapter 3 builds functions out of exactly this idea.

The rigid motions your course uses — translations, rotations, reflections — are functions from $\mathbb{R}^2$ to $\mathbb{R}^2$, which is to say subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2\times\mathbb{R}^2$ satisfying a condition. Nothing new; just the same machinery.
</Aside>

## Products of regions

For $A = [0,3]$ and $B = [0,2]$ as intervals in $\mathbb{R}$,
$$A \times B = \{(x,y) : 0\le x\le 3,\ 0 \le y \le 2\}$$
is a $3$-by-$2$ rectangle in the plane, sides included.

Products of intervals are rectangles. This is why area is a product of lengths — the set-theoretic operation and the measurement agree, and the rectangle-area formula is the two-dimensional shadow of $|S\times T| = |S||T|$.

Not every region is such a product. A disk is not: its horizontal cross-sections have different lengths, so it cannot be written as (an interval) $\times$ (an interval). The regions that *are* products are exactly the rectangles with sides parallel to the axes.

## A product identity

> **Proposition.** $(A\cap B)\times(C\cap D) = (A\times C)\cap(B\times D)$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Membership in a product is a claim about the two entries separately; membership in an intersection is a conjunction.</div>
  <div slot="want">Both containments.</div>
  <div slot="how">Both sides unwind to the same four conditions on $x$ and $y$; the proof is bookkeeping about which conjunction is written outermost. Take an arbitrary element — and note it must first be shown to *be* an ordered pair.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $p \in (A\cap B)\times(C\cap D)$. Then $p = (x,y)$ with $x \in A \cap B$ and $y \in C\cap D$; that is, $x \in A$, $x \in B$, $y \in C$, $y \in D$.

From $x \in A$ and $y \in C$ we get $(x,y) \in A\times C$. From $x \in B$ and $y \in D$ we get $(x,y)\in B\times D$. Hence $p \in (A\times C)\cap(B\times D)$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $p \in (A\times C)\cap(B\times D)$. From $p \in A\times C$, $p = (x,y)$ with $x \in A$ and $y \in C$. From $p \in B\times D$, also $x \in B$ and $y \in D$ — the entries are the same $x$ and $y$, since an ordered pair determines its entries.

So $x \in A\cap B$ and $y \in C\cap D$, giving $p \in (A\cap B)\times(C\cap D)$.

Therefore the sets are equal.
</Proof>

The second half quietly used the defining property of ordered pairs: the pair could not have different first entries in the two readings. Small steps like that are the ones a proof must not skip.

<Warning title="Products do not distribute over unions">
$$(A\cup B)\times(C\cup D) \neq (A\times C)\cup(B\times D).$$

Take $A = \{1\}$, $B=\{2\}$, $C=\{a\}$, $D=\{b\}$. The left side has four pairs; the right has two, missing $(1,b)$ and $(2,a)$.

Geometrically: the left side is the bounding rectangle of two smaller rectangles, and the right side is the two rectangles themselves. The corners are what differ.

The lesson is the one that closes the chapter. Identities that hold for $\cap$ and $\cup$ do not automatically hold when a new operation joins them, and the only way to find out is to check — with an element chase if it is true, with two small sets if it is not.
</Warning>
