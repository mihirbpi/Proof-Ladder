## Ordered pairs

An **ordered pair** $(a,b)$ is pinned down completely by one rule:

> **(1)** Two pairs are equal exactly when their first entries match **and** their second entries match.

That is the whole difference between a pair and a set. The set $\{a,b\}$ is the same as $\{b,a\}$, always. The pair $(a,b)$ equals $(b,a)$ only when $a$ and $b$ are the same thing. Sets forget order; pairs remember it, and rule (1) is what "remember" means.

## The cartesian product

$S \times T$, said "$S$ cross $T$", is the collection of **every** pair whose first entry comes from $S$ and whose second comes from $T$.

The working form of that — the line every proof below reaches for — is:

> **(2)** A pair $(x,y)$ is in $S \times T$ exactly when $x$ is in $S$ **and** $y$ is in $T$.

With $S = \{1,2\}$, $T = \{a,b,c\}$:
$$
S \times T = \{(1,a),(1,b),(1,c),(2,a),(2,b),(2,c)\}.
$$

For finite sets $|S \times T| = |S| \cdot |T|$ — the grid has $|S|$ rows and $|T|$ columns. This is the combinatorial content of multiplication.

## Non-commutativity and the empty set

$S \times T \neq T \times S$ in general, since $(1,a) \neq (a,1)$ by (1). They are equal only when $S = T$, or when one of them is empty.

**Crossing anything with the empty set gives the empty set.**

Why: a member of $S \times \{\,\}$ would be a pair whose second entry comes from the empty set — and there is nothing there to take. So there are no such pairs. (Or: the grid has zero columns.)

<Warning title="An element of a product is a pair — name it as one">
The single most useful habit in this module: whenever you chase one element out of a product set, immediately write it as a pair.

> Take anything in $A \times C$. It is a pair — call it $(x,y)$, with $x$ from $A$ and $y$ from $C$.

Trying to reason about it without splitting it into two coordinates is where these proofs stall. Once it is a pair, rule (2) turns one membership statement into two ordinary ones, and Chapter 1 takes over.
</Warning>

## Products interact with intersection

> **Proposition.** For all sets $A, B, C, D$:
> $$(A \cap B) \times (C \cap D) = (A \times C) \cap (B \times D).$$

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Rule (2), and what an overlap means: being in the overlap of two collections is being in each of them.</div>
  <div slot="want">The two collections have exactly the same members.</div>
  <div slot="how">Unfold both sides into plain statements about $x$ and $y$. Each side turns into the same four requirements joined by *and*, listed in a different order — and the order of *and*s never matters.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Anything in either collection is a pair, so call it $(x,y)$ and unfold each side in turn.

**Left side.** Being in $(A \cap B) \times (C \cap D)$ means, by rule (2), that $x$ is in the overlap of $A$ and $B$, and $y$ is in the overlap of $C$ and $D$. Written out, that is four requirements at once:

> $x$ is in $A$, and $x$ is in $B$, and $y$ is in $C$, and $y$ is in $D$.

**Right side.** Being in the overlap of $A \times C$ and $B \times D$ means being in both of those. By rule (2) again, the first says $x$ is in $A$ and $y$ is in $C$; the second says $x$ is in $B$ and $y$ is in $D$. Written out:

> $x$ is in $A$, and $y$ is in $C$, and $x$ is in $B$, and $y$ is in $D$.

**The same four requirements, in a different order.** Since the order of *and*s never matters, each side holds exactly when the other does — so the two collections have the same members.
</Proof>

Notice what actually happened: the entire theorem was *the order of **and**s does not matter*. That is the Chapter 1 dictionary doing the work again.

## The corresponding union statement is false

Replace both overlaps above by pools and the statement becomes **false**. Crossing two pooled collections gives *more* pairs than pooling the two crossings, and the extras are the mixed pairs.

Take $A = C = \{1\}$ and $B = D = \{2\}$. The left side contains the pair $(1,2)$, since $1$ is in the first pool and $2$ is in the second. The right side is $\{(1,1)\}$ pooled with $\{(2,2)\}$ — no mixed pairs at all. One counterexample settles it (module 1.11).

The asymmetry is worth understanding rather than memorizing. The proof above worked by rearranging four *and*s, which is always allowed. Rearranging *or*s the same way is not.

## The coordinate plane and the whole-number grid

Cross the real numbers with themselves and you get every pair of coordinates — which is to say, **the coordinate plane**. Cross the integers with themselves and you get the grid points on it.

So coordinate geometry is the study of collections of pairs. The graph of $y = 3x$ is the collection of pairs whose second entry is three times the first. A circle of radius $r$ about the origin is the collection of pairs at distance $r$ from $(0,0)$. Every graph you have ever drawn was a collection of pairs picked out by a rule.

Crossing does not stop at two. Cross three collections and members are triples; three copies of the real numbers give space; and $n$ copies — lists of $n$ coordinates — is where most mathematics after high school is done.

<Aside>
This module is the last piece of scaffolding before Chapter 3. A **function** from $S$ to $T$ will be defined as a particular collection of pairs inside $S \times T$: the pairs whose first entry is an input and whose second is the output it gets sent to. That set of pairs is exactly what you would plot as its graph.

So functions are not new objects. They are crossings with a rule attached, and the rule is that each member of $S$ turns up in exactly one pair.
</Aside>
