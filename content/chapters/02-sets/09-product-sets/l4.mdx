## Ordered pairs

An **ordered pair** $(a,b)$ is characterised entirely by one property:
$$
(a,b) = (c,d) \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a = c \;\wedge\; b = d. \tag{1}
$$

That is what distinguishes pairs from sets: $\{a,b\} = \{b,a\}$ always, while $(a,b) = (b,a)$ only when $a = b$. Sets forget order; pairs remember it, and (1) is the formal content of "remember".

## The cartesian product

$$
S \times T = \{\, (s,t) \mid s \in S \wedge t \in T \,\},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\wedge$ is "and", the bar $\mid$ is "such that", $\times$ pairs things up. So the line reads: $S \times T$ is the collection of all pairs whose first entry comes from $S$ **and** whose second comes from $T$. The bar is "such that"; $\in$ is "is in".*
so the working form is
$$
(x,y) \in S \times T \iff x \in S \wedge y \in T. \tag{2}
$$

With $S = \{1,2\}$, $T = \{a,b,c\}$:
$$
S \times T = \{(1,a),(1,b),(1,c),(2,a),(2,b),(2,c)\}.
$$

For finite sets $|S \times T| = |S| \cdot |T|$ — the grid has $|S|$ rows and $|T|$ columns. This is the combinatorial content of multiplication.

## Non-commutativity and the empty set

$S \times T \neq T \times S$ in general, since $(1,a) \neq (a,1)$ by (1). They are equal only when $S = T$, or when one of them is empty.

$$
S \times \emptyset = \emptyset \times S = \emptyset.
$$

Proof: an element of $S \times \emptyset$ would be a pair $(s,t)$ with $t \in \emptyset$, and $\emptyset$ has no elements, so no such pair exists. (Equivalently: the grid has zero columns.)

<Warning title="An element of a product is a pair — name it as one">
The single most useful habit in this module: whenever you chase an arbitrary element out of a product set, immediately write it as a pair.

> Let $z \in A \times C$. Then $z = (x,y)$ for some $x \in A$, $y \in C$.

Trying to reason about $z$ without splitting it into coordinates is where these proofs stall. Once it is $(x,y)$, condition (2) converts membership into two ordinary membership statements and Chapter 1 takes over.
</Warning>

## Products interact with intersection

> **Proposition.** For all sets $A, B, C, D$:
> $$(A \cap B) \times (C \cap D) = (A \times C) \cap (B \times D).$$

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$(x,y) \in (A\cap B)\times(C\cap D) \iff (x \in A \wedge x \in B) \wedge (y \in C \wedge y \in D)$. And $(x,y) \in (A\times C)\cap(B\times D) \iff (x \in A \wedge y \in C) \wedge (x \in B \wedge y \in D)$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion.</div>
  <div slot="how">Both sides unfold to the same four conjuncts in a different order. Since $\wedge$ is commutative and associative, the two are equivalent — so the proof is a chain of biconditionals once every element is named as a pair.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $z$ be arbitrary. Any element of either side is an ordered pair, so write $z = (x,y)$.

Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
(x,y) \in (A \cap B) \times (C \cap D)
&\iff (x \in A \cap B) \wedge (y \in C \cap D) \\
&\iff (x \in A \wedge x \in B) \wedge (y \in C \wedge y \in D) \\
&\iff (x \in A \wedge y \in C) \wedge (x \in B \wedge y \in D) \\
&\iff \big((x,y) \in A \times C\big) \wedge \big((x,y) \in B \times D\big) \\
&\iff (x,y) \in (A \times C) \cap (B \times D).
\end{aligned}
$$
The third step is a rearrangement, valid since $\wedge$ is commutative and associative. Every step is a biconditional, so both inclusions hold and the sets are equal.
</Proof>

Notice what actually happened: the whole theorem is commutativity of $\wedge$. That is the Chapter 1 dictionary doing the work again.

## The corresponding union statement is false

$$(A \cup B) \times (C \cup D) \neq (A \times C) \cup (B \times D) \text{ in general.}$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\emptyset$ is the empty collection, $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\wedge$ is "and", $\cup$ is "pooled with". So the line reads: pairing up two pooled collections gives more pairs than pooling the two pairings — the mixed pairs are the extras.*

Take $A = C = \{1\}$, $B = D = \{2\}$. The left side contains $(1,2)$, since $1 \in A \cup B$ and $2 \in C \cup D$. The right side is $\{(1,1)\} \cup \{(2,2)\}$, which does not. One counterexample settles it (module 1.11).

The asymmetry is instructive: products distribute over $\cap$ but not over $\cup$, because the rearrangement step above needs the conjunctions to be independent, and disjunctions do not rearrange the same way.

## The coordinate plane and the whole-number grid

$$
\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} = \mathbb{R}^2, \qquad
\mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} = \text{the integer lattice.}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\times$ pairs things up. So the line reads: pairs of reals are the points of the plane; pairs of integers are the grid points on it.*

Coordinate geometry *is* the study of subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$: the graph of a proportional relationship is $\{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid y = 3x\}$, a circle of radius $r$ about the origin is $\{(x,y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 \mid x^2 + y^2 = r^2\}$. Every graph you have ever drawn was a set-builder subset of a product set.

Products extend: $S \times T \times R$ has ordered triples, $\mathbb{R}^3$ is space, and $\mathbb{R}^n$ — lists of $n$ coordinates — is where most of the mathematics after high school is done.

<Aside>
This module is the last piece of scaffolding before Chapter 3. A **function** from $S$ to $T$ will be defined as a particular subset of $S \times T$: the set of pairs whose first entry is an input and whose second is the output it gets sent to. That set of pairs is exactly what you would plot as its graph.

So functions are not new objects. They are products with a condition attached, and the condition is that each element of $S$ appears in exactly one pair.
</Aside>
