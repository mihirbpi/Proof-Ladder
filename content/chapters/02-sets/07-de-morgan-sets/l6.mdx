$$
\overline{S \cap T} = \overline{S} \cup \overline{T}
\qquad\qquad
\overline{S \cup T} = \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

You have seen these before. In module 1.4 they were about $\wedge$ and $\vee$; here they are about $\cap$ and $\cup$. Nothing new is being discovered — a translation is being performed, and this module is where you watch a Chapter 1 proof become a Chapter 2 proof line by line.

The diagrams make the laws believable. They do not prove them — a two-circle picture shows one arrangement, and a proof must cover all of them. Diagrams are for finding the statement; element chasing is for establishing it.

## Proof

> **Theorem.** $\overline{S \cap T} = \overline{S} \cup \overline{T}$.

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in \overline{S\cap T}$. Then $x \in U$ and $x \notin S \cap T$.

Suppose $x \in S$ and $x \in T$. Then $x \in S\cap T$, contradicting $x \notin S \cap T$. So it is not the case that both hold, and therefore $x \notin S$ or $x \notin T$.

So $x \in \overline S$ or $x \in \overline T$, that is, $x \in \overline S \cup \overline T$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in \overline S \cup \overline T$. Then $x \in \overline S$ or $x \in \overline T$; we take cases.

*Case 1: $x \in \overline S$,* so $x \notin S$. Then $x \notin S \cap T$, since membership in the intersection would require $x \in S$.

*Case 2: $x \in \overline T$,* so $x \notin T$. Then $x \notin S \cap T$ for the same reason.

The cases are exhaustive, so in both $x \in \overline{S \cap T}$.

Therefore $\overline{S\cap T} = \overline S \cup \overline T$.
</Proof>

Two features are worth naming, because they recur:

**The $\subseteq$ half used contradiction.** The hypothesis was a negation — $x \notin S \cap T$ — and negations give you nothing to build with, so "suppose not" supplied something (module 2.5).

**The $\supseteq$ half used cases.** The hypothesis was a $\cup$, and you were not told which side. Module 1.12's rule again.

This is the same pairing as the last module's, and once you have seen it twice you can predict the structure of the next such proof before writing a word.

## The second law

$\overline{S \cup T} = \overline S \cap \overline T$ can be proved the same way, but there is a better route: **derive it from the first.**

Apply the first law to $\overline S$ and $\overline T$ in place of $S$ and $T$:
$$
\overline{\overline S \cap \overline T} = \overline{\overline S} \cup \overline{\overline T} = S \cup T,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

using $\overline{\overline S} = S$. Now complement both sides:
$$
\overline S \cap \overline T = \overline{S \cup T}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

**One law implies the other.** Complementation is a mirror that exchanges $\cap$ and $\cup$, and each law is the other seen in it. Deriving is better than re-proving here — it is shorter, and it shows *why* the two laws come as a pair rather than by coincidence.

## The dictionary

| Logic (Ch. 1) | Sets (Ch. 2) |
|---|---|
| $\wedge$ | $\cap$ |
| $\vee$ | $\cup$ |
| $\neg$ | complement |
| $\Rightarrow$ | $\subseteq$ |
| $\equiv$ | $=$ |
| tautology | $U$ |
| contradiction | $\emptyset$ |

Every row is a genuine correspondence, and the table is worth memorizing because it converts work you have already done into results you would otherwise prove again. The absorption law, commutativity, associativity, distributivity — all transfer.

<Warning title="What the diagrams cannot do">
A two-circle Venn diagram assumes $S$ and $T$ overlap, with each region non-empty. Real pairs of sets might be disjoint, or nested, or equal.

For these particular laws the picture happens to generalize. For other claims it does not, and a diagram is exactly the wrong instrument: three sets need eight regions and a carefully drawn figure, four sets cannot be drawn with circles at all.

Element chasing has none of these limits, which is the practical argument for learning it.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The same laws hold for quantifiers, and by now the pattern should be predictable:
$$\overline{S_1 \cap \cdots \cap S_n} = \overline{S_1} \cup \cdots \cup \overline{S_n}, \qquad \overline{S_1 \cup \cdots \cup S_n} = \overline{S_1} \cap \cdots \cap \overline{S_n}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

Since an intersection is a *for all* and a union is a *there exists*, this is module 1.11 written a third time.

Three appearances — joining words, sets, quantifiers — of one law. That kind of recurrence is what mathematicians mean when they call a result *fundamental*: it is not that the law is difficult, but that it keeps being the same law in every new setting.
</Aside>

## A geometric reading

Take $U$ to be the plane, $S$ the interior of one circle and $T$ the interior of another.

$\overline{S \cap T}$ is everything outside the lens where the disks overlap — including points inside one disk. $\overline S \cup \overline T$ is the set of points outside at least one disk. Convince yourself those are the same description, and you have the first law in a form you can see.

The lesson your book keeps applying: the negation of *both* is *at least one fails*, and the region that fails to be in both is much larger than the region outside everything.
