Fix a universe $U$; all sets are subsets of it.

$$
\overline{S \cap T} = \overline S \cup \overline T,
\qquad
\overline{S \cup T} = \overline S \cap \overline T.
$$

These are §1.4's laws under the dictionary $\neg \leftrightarrow \overline{\phantom{x}}$, $\wedge \leftrightarrow \cap$, $\vee \leftrightarrow \cup$. This is the first module in which Chapter 1 functions as machinery rather than as content: the pivot of the proof is a quoted logical equivalence.

## Proof

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in \overline A \iff x \in U \wedge x \notin A$; $x \in S \cup T \iff (x\in S)\vee(x\in T)$; and $\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion (§2.6).</div>
  <div slot="how">Unfold membership into logic, apply the logic law, refold. Because the logic law is an equivalence rather than an implication, both inclusions are the same computation read in opposite directions — so the equality can be presented as a chain of biconditionals.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $x \in U$ be arbitrary. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
x \in \overline{S \cup T}
&\iff \neg\big(x \in S \cup T\big) \\
&\iff \neg\big((x \in S) \vee (x \in T)\big) \\
&\iff (x \notin S) \wedge (x \notin T) &&\text{(De Morgan, §1.4)}\\
&\iff (x \in \overline S) \wedge (x \in \overline T) \\
&\iff x \in \overline S \cap \overline T .
\end{aligned}
$$
Every step is a biconditional, so both inclusions are established simultaneously. Since $x \in U$ was arbitrary, the sets are equal.
</Proof>

The chain form is legitimate here precisely because each link is a definitional unfolding or a logical equivalence — the condition §2.6 warned about. Written as two separate inclusions the proof is twice as long and no more convincing.

The first law follows either by the same argument with $\neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q$, or from the second by substituting $\overline S, \overline T$ and complementing, using $\overline{\overline A} = A$.

## The translation is structural

| Connective | Set operation |
|---|---|
| $\neg$ | complement |
| $\wedge$ | $\cap$ |
| $\vee$ | $\cup$ |
| $\Rightarrow$ (universally) | $\subseteq$ |
| $\Leftrightarrow$ (universally) | $=$ |
| $\exists i$ | $\bigcup_i$ |
| $\forall i$ | $\bigcap_i$ |

The reason this dictionary is exact: the map sending a predicate $p$ to its truth set $\{x \in U \mid p(x)\}$ turns $\wedge$ into $\cap$, $\vee$ into $\cup$, and $\neg$ into complement — it preserves all three operations. Every equational identity therefore transfers in both directions. It is not that the two subjects are similar; up to isomorphism they are the same algebra.

## Indexed form

For a family $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$:
$$
\overline{\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i} = \bigcap_{i \in I} \overline{A_i},
\qquad
\overline{\bigcap_{i \in I} A_i} = \bigcup_{i \in I} \overline{A_i}.
$$

The proof is the same three moves, with $\neg\exists \equiv \forall\neg$ (§1.11) at the pivot instead of $\neg\vee \equiv \wedge\neg$:
$$
x \in \overline{\textstyle\bigcup_i A_i}
\iff \neg \exists i,\, x \in A_i
\iff \forall i,\, x \notin A_i
\iff x \in \textstyle\bigcap_i \overline{A_i}.
$$
No induction is needed and $I$ may be uncountable — which is the point of proving it this way rather than by iterating the binary case.

<Warning title="The connective must swap">
In $U = \{1,\ldots,6\}$ with $S = \{1,2,3\}$, $T = \{3,4\}$:
$$\overline{S \cap T} = \{1,2,4,5,6\}, \qquad \overline S \cap \overline T = \{5,6\}.$$
A single counterexample settles that $\overline{S \cap T} \neq \overline S \cap \overline T$. In practice the error appears not as a bare identity but inside a proof, when a negated hypothesis about a union or intersection is expanded carelessly.
</Warning>

## Duality

De Morgan says complementation is an **order-reversing involution** on $\mathcal{P}(U)$ exchanging $\cap$ and $\cup$ (and, by §2.5, exchanging $\subseteq$ with $\supseteq$, $\emptyset$ with $U$). Consequently every theorem about subsets has a **dual**, obtained by swapping $\cap \leftrightarrow \cup$, $\subseteq \leftrightarrow \supseteq$, $\emptyset \leftrightarrow U$ — and the dual is automatically true, with the dual proof.

This halves the work in the next module: the two distributive laws of §2.8 are duals, so proving one proves both. The habit of noticing duality before starting a proof is worth acquiring now.

<Aside>
$\cup$ and $\cap$ are recoverable from each other via complement — $S \cap T = \overline{\overline S \cup \overline T}$ — so $\{\neg, \vee\}$ suffices to express everything, mirroring the functional completeness of $\{\neg, \vee\}$ for connectives. In circuit terms this is why NOR gates alone can implement any Boolean function.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
The set form of De Morgan is what lets you compute a complicated solution set by computing its complement instead.

**Solving inequalities.** To find $\{x : |x-3|\geq 2 \ \text{or}\ x^2 < 1\}$ directly means assembling a union of pieces. Complementing turns it into an intersection of two simpler conditions, and complementing back at the end is often less error-prone than the direct assembly. The tactic is standard in a first calculus course whenever a sign chart has several factors.

**Where a function is undefined.** The domain of $f$ is the complement of the set where the formula fails, and when the formula fails for several reasons at once — a zero denominator **or** a negative radicand — the excluded set is a union, so the domain is an intersection of complements. That is one law of this module, applied to every domain computation you have ever done.

**In linear algebra the duality shows up in dimension.** For subspaces $U, W$ of $V$,
$$\dim(U+W) + \dim(U\cap W) = \dim U + \dim W,$$
which is the same trade as the counting formula $|S\cup T| + |S\cap T| = |S|+|T|$ from §2.4. Sum and intersection exchange roles the way union and intersection do, and the orthogonal complement $W^\perp$ behaves like a complement in the exact sense De Morgan requires:
$$(U + W)^\perp = U^\perp \cap W^\perp, \qquad (U\cap W)^\perp = U^\perp + W^\perp.$$

Those two identities are usually presented as separate theorems. They are one law in two costumes, and recognising that is worth more than memorising either.

**The habit is to expect the swap.** When you complement a compound condition, the connective changes; when you take orthogonal complements of subspaces, sum and intersection change places. Predicting that before computing is what this module buys you.

*You have matrices, determinants and systems; linear algebra builds its vocabulary on top of them. Above: **subspace** — a set of vectors closed under adding and scaling.*
</WhereThisGoes>
