Fix a universe $U$; all sets are subsets of it.

$$
\overline{S \cap T} = \overline S \cup \overline T,
\qquad
\overline{S \cup T} = \overline S \cap \overline T.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: outside an overlap is outside one pooled with outside the other; outside a pooled pair is the overlap of the two outsides.*

These are §1.4's laws under the dictionary $\neg \leftrightarrow \overline{\phantom{x}}$, $\wedge \leftrightarrow \cap$, $\vee \leftrightarrow \cup$. This is the first module in which Chapter 1 functions as toolkit rather than as content: the pivot of the proof is a quoted logical equivalence.

## Proof

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Being outside $A$ means being in $U$ but not in $A$; being in $S \cup T$ means being in one or the other; and De Morgan for joining words says that failing an *or* means failing both parts.</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion (§2.6).</div>
  <div slot="how">Unfold membership into logic, apply the logic law, refold. Because the logic law is an equivalence rather than an implication, both inclusions are the same computation read in opposite directions — so the equality can be presented as a chain of if-and-only-if statements.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>

*The symbols in this proof: $\in$ is "is in", $\notin$ is "is not in", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\iff$ is "exactly when", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when". In words, what follows shows: each step rewrites "outside the pool" until it reads "outside both", one equivalence at a time.*

Let $x \in U$ be arbitrary. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
x \in \overline{S \cup T}
&\iff \neg\big(x \in S \cup T\big) \\
&\iff \neg\big((x \in S) \vee (x \in T)\big) \\
&\iff (x \notin S) \wedge (x \notin T) &&\text{(De Morgan, module 1.4)}\\
&\iff (x \in \overline S) \wedge (x \in \overline T) \\
&\iff x \in \overline S \cap \overline T .
\end{aligned}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and".*

Every step is an if-and-only-if statement, so both inclusions are proved simultaneously. Since $x \in U$ was arbitrary, the sets are equal.
</Proof>

The chain form is legitimate here exactly because each link is a definitional unfolding or a logical equivalence — the condition §2.6 warned about. Written as two separate inclusions the proof is twice as long and no more convincing.

The first law follows either by the same argument with $\neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q$ — *denying that both hold is saying at least one of them fails* — or from the second by substituting $\overline S, \overline T$ and complementing, using $\overline{\overline A} = A$.

## The translation is structural

| Joining word | Set operation |
|---|---|
| $\neg$ | complement |
| $\wedge$ | $\cap$ |
| $\vee$ | $\cup$ |
| $\Rightarrow$ (universally) | $\subseteq$ |
| $\Leftrightarrow$ (universally) | $=$ |
| *there exists an $i$* | $A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n$ |
| *for every $i$* | $A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n$ |

The reason this dictionary is exact: sending each condition to the set of things satisfying it turns *and* into intersection, *or* into union, and *not* into complement — all three operations are preserved. Every equational identity therefore transfers in both directions. It is not that the two subjects are similar; up to a renaming that preserves all the structure they are the same algebra.

## Indexed form

For a family $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$:
$$
\overline{A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots \cup A_n} = \overline{A_1} \cap \cdots \cap \overline{A_n},
\qquad
\overline{A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_n} = \overline{A_1} \cup \cdots \cup \overline{A_n}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

The proof is the same three moves, with the quantifier rule of §1.11 at the pivot instead of the joining word rule:
$$
x \in \overline{A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n}
\iff \neg \exists i,\, x \in A_i
\iff \forall i,\, x \notin A_i
\iff x \in \textstyle\overline{A_1} \cap \cdots \cap \overline{A_n}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when".*

No induction (§6.1: prove the first case, then that each case forces the next) is needed, and the family may be larger than any list — which is the point of proving it this way rather than by repeating the two-set case over and over.

<Warning title="The joining word must swap">
In $U = \{1,\ldots,6\}$ with $S = \{1,2,3\}$, $T = \{3,4\}$:
$$\overline{S \cap T} = \{1,2,4,5,6\}, \qquad \overline S \cap \overline T = \{5,6\}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

A single counterexample settles that $\overline{S \cap T} \neq \overline S \cap \overline T$. In practice the error appears not as a bare identity but inside a proof, when a negated hypothesis about a union or intersection is expanded carelessly.
</Warning>

## Each law is the other in a mirror

De Morgan says complementation undoes itself, turns every containment around, and exchanges $\cap$ and $\cup$ (and, by §2.5, exchanging $\subseteq$ with $\supseteq$, $\emptyset$ with $U$). Consequently every theorem about subsets has a **dual**, which you get by swapping $\cap \leftrightarrow \cup$, $\subseteq \leftrightarrow \supseteq$, $\emptyset \leftrightarrow U$ — and the dual is automatically true, with the dual proof.

This halves the work in the next module: the two distributive laws of §2.8 are mirror images of each other, so proving one proves both. The habit of checking whether a law has a mirror image before starting a proof is worth acquiring now.

<Aside>
$\cup$ and $\cap$ are recoverable from each other via complement — $S \cap T = \overline{\overline S \cup \overline T}$ — so *not* and *or* between them can express everything, exactly as they can for the joining words of Chapter 1. The same fact is why a single kind of logic gate, wired up enough times, can compute anything a circuit can.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

The set form of De Morgan is what lets you compute a complicated solution set by computing its complement instead.

**Solving inequalities.** To find $\{x : |x-3|\geq 2 \ \text{or}\ x^2 < 1\}$ directly means assembling a union of pieces. Complementing turns it into an intersection of two simpler conditions, and complementing back at the end is often less error-prone than the direct assembly. The tactic is standard in a first calculus course whenever a sign chart has several factors.

**Where a function is undefined.** The domain of $f$ is the complement of the set where the formula fails, and when the formula fails for several reasons at once — a zero denominator **or** a negative radicand — the excluded set is a union, so the domain is an intersection of complements. That is one law of this module, applied to every domain computation you have ever done.

Those two identities are usually presented as separate theorems. They are one law in two costumes, and recognizing that is worth more than memorizing either.

</WhereThisGoes>
