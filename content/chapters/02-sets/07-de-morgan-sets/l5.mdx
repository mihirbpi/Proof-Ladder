Working inside a universe $U$:

$$
\overline{S \cap T} = \overline S \cup \overline T \tag{DM1}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: **outside the overlap** is the same as **outside $S$ pooled with outside $T$**; and outside a union is the overlap of the two outsides.*

$$
\overline{S \cup T} = \overline S \cap \overline T \tag{DM2}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

**Taking the outside swaps intersection and union.** Compare module 1.4, where denying an *and* gave an *or*, and denying an *or* gave an *and*.

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

These are the same two laws. Under the dictionary of module 2.4 — *not* becomes complement, *and* becomes intersection, *or* becomes union — each logic law turns into a set law, and you should expect the proofs to correspond too.

## Reading DM2 in words

*Outside both* is *outside the first and outside the second.*

Say it about something concrete. A student is not taking chemistry or physics exactly when they are not taking chemistry **and** not taking physics. Obvious in English, and the obviousness is the point: the law is a fact about ordinary reasoning that happens to be provable.

## Proof of DM2

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Being outside the union means it is not the case that you are in $S$ or in $T$. Being in the overlap of the two outsides means you are outside $S$ **and** outside $T$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion (module 2.6).</div>
  <div slot="how">Unfold both sides into logic, then apply the logic law of module 1.4 at the pivot. Every other step is bookkeeping.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in \overline{S \cup T}$ be arbitrary. Then $x \in U$ and $x \notin S \cup T$, so it is not the case that $x \in S$ or $x \in T$.

If $x$ were in $S$, then $x$ would be in $S \cup T$ — it is not, so $x \notin S$, giving $x \in \overline S$. The same argument with $T$ gives $x \in \overline T$.

Hence $x \in \overline S \cap \overline T$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in \overline S \cap \overline T$ be arbitrary. Then $x \in U$, $x \notin S$, and $x \notin T$.

Suppose $x \in S \cup T$. Then $x \in S$ or $x \in T$, and both have been ruled out — impossible. So $x \notin S \cup T$, and $x \in \overline{S \cup T}$.

Both inclusions hold, so $\overline{S \cup T} = \overline S \cap \overline T$.
</Proof>

The backward direction used **suppose not** (module 2.5) in miniature: the goal was a negative one — *not* in the union — so assuming the positive and finding it impossible was the direct route.

## DM1, for free

You could prove DM1 the same way, and there is a shorter path.

<Proof>
Apply DM2 to the sets $\overline S$ and $\overline T$:
$$
\overline{\overline S \cup \overline T} = \overline{\overline S} \cap \overline{\overline T} = S \cap T,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

using that complementing twice returns the original set. Now complement both sides:
$$
\overline S \cup \overline T = \overline{S \cap T},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

which is DM1.
</Proof>

**One law was proved and the other was derived**, using nothing but double complementation. Module 2.8 shows this is not luck: the mirroring is general, and every identity about $\cap$ and $\cup$ comes with a partner obtained by swapping the two.

Note the tool that made it work: the outside of the outside is the original set. That is the set version of denying a denial from module 1.2, which looked at the time like a fact too small to bother naming.

<Warning title="The complement of an intersection is not the intersection of complements">
The outside of an intersection is **not** the intersection of the outsides.

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: the outside of an overlap is **not** the overlap of the outsides — the operation has to swap.*

The operation swaps; it does not pass through unchanged. Test it. In $U = \{1,2,3\}$ with $S = \{1\}$ and $T = \{2\}$:

- $S$ and $T$ share nothing, so the outside of their intersection is everything: $\{1,2,3\}$.
- But the outside of $S$ is $\{2,3\}$ and the outside of $T$ is $\{1,3\}$, and those overlap in just $\{3\}$.

Not equal. This is module 1.4's error transplanted, and it appears most often when negating a hypothesis with two conditions: the negation of *$x$ is positive and rational* is *$x$ is not positive **or** not rational*.
</Warning>

<Aside>
De Morgan's laws hold in three places you have now met — logic, sets, and (in Chapter 1) truth tables — and the reason is that all three are the same structure described differently.

The practical payoff is in solving inequalities. The set of $x$ failing *both* $x > 1$ and $x < 5$ is not the set failing "$1 < x < 5$" in some vague sense; it is precisely $\overline{(1,5)} = (-\infty, 1] \cup [5, \infty)$, a **union**, and the switch from *and* to *or* is DM2 doing its job.

Students who get these problems wrong almost always kept the joining word instead of swapping it.
</Aside>
