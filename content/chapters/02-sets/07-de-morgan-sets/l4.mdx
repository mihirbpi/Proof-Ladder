Fix a universe $U$, and let $S$ and $T$ be collections inside it.

$$
\overline{S \cap T} = \overline{S} \cup \overline{T},
\qquad
\overline{S \cup T} = \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: outside an overlap is the same as outside one pooled with outside the other; outside a pooled pair is the overlap of the two outsides.*

This is the first proof in the course where **Chapter 1 is used as a tool** rather than as subject matter. The pivot step of the argument below is a logical equivalence, quoted and applied.

## Proof of the second law

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Being outside $A$ means being in $U$ and not in $A$. Being in $S \cup T$ means being in $S$ or in $T$. And De Morgan for joining words (module 1.4): failing an *or* means failing both parts.</div>
  <div slot="want">$\overline{S \cup T} \subseteq \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$ and the reverse.</div>
  <div slot="how">Chase one element, assuming nothing about it. Unfold the set notation into logic, apply the logic law, and fold back up. Both directions are the same unfolding read in opposite orders — because the logic law is an equivalence, not an implication.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Take any $x \in \overline{S \cup T}$. Then $x \in U$, and $x$ fails to be in $S$ *or* $T$.

By De Morgan's logic law, failing an *or* means failing both parts: $x \notin S$ and $x \notin T$.

Since also $x \in U$, we get $x \in \overline{S}$ and $x \in \overline{T}$, so $x \in \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$.

**($\supseteq$)** Take any $x \in \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$. Then $x \in U$, $x \notin S$, and $x \notin T$. By the same logic law, failing both parts is the same as failing the *or*, so $x \notin S \cup T$. With $x \in U$, this gives $x \in \overline{S \cup T}$.

Both inclusions hold, so $\overline{S \cup T} = \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$.
</Proof>

The first law is proved identically, using the other De Morgan law — failing an *and* means failing at least one part — instead. Alternatively it follows from the second by substituting $\overline S, \overline T$ for $S, T$ and complementing both sides, using $\overline{\overline A} = A$.

## Why the two chapters keep matching

The correspondence is not luck. Sets in this course are specified by conditions (module 2.2), and a set operation is defined *as* an operation on conditions:

| Joining word | Set operation | What membership means |
|---|---|---|
| and | intersection | in both sets |
| or | union | in at least one of them |
| not | complement | in the universe but not in the set |
| if–then | $\subseteq$ | being in the first forces being in the second |
| if and only if | $=$ | the two have exactly the same members |

So every logical identity produces a set identity by mechanical translation, and the proof is always the same three moves: unfold, apply the logic law, fold. Once you have seen it twice, the remaining laws of this chapter are exercises.

<Warning title="The joining word must swap">
The outside of an overlap is **not** the overlap of the outsides. Concretely, in $U = \{1,\ldots,6\}$ with $S = \{1,2,3\}$, $T = \{3,4\}$:
$$\overline{S \cap T} = \overline{\{3\}} = \{1,2,4,5,6\},$$
$$\overline{S} \cap \overline{T} = \{4,5,6\} \cap \{1,2,5,6\} = \{5,6\}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: the outside of the overlap of $S$ and $T$ is everything except $3$.*So the line reads: the outside of $S$, overlapped with the outside of $T$, leaves only $5$ and $6$.*

Different sets. Keeping the joining word is the same over-negation error as in module 1.4, and one counterexample settles it.
</Warning>

## Where you will use it

Whenever a proof needs the outside of something built by pooling or overlapping — which is often, since any hypothesis phrased negatively produces one. Two immediate consequences worth recording:

$$
S \setminus (T \cup R) = (S \setminus T) \cap (S \setminus R),
\qquad
S \setminus (T \cap R) = (S \setminus T) \cup (S \setminus R),
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: taking away a pool means taking away each of them in turn, and taking away an overlap means taking away one **or** the other.*

obtained by taking $U = S$. These are the forms you actually meet in practice, since set difference is more common than absolute complement.

<Aside>
The laws extend to any number of sets at once. For a family $A_1, \ldots, A_n$,
$$\overline{A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n} = \overline{A_1} \cap \cdots \cap \overline{A_n},$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: the outside of a pool of many collections is the overlap of all their outsides.*

provable by induction (Chapter 6). Push it to infinitely many collections and the joining words turn into quantifiers, and the law becomes *denying a there-is gives a for-every* — module 1.11 again. One law, three levels of generality.
</Aside>
