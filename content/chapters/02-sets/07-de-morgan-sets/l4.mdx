Fix a universe $U$ and let $S, T \subseteq U$.

$$
\overline{S \cap T} = \overline{S} \cup \overline{T},
\qquad
\overline{S \cup T} = \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}.
$$

This is the first proof in the course where **Chapter 1 is used as a tool** rather than as subject matter. The pivot step of the argument below is a logical equivalence, quoted and applied.

## Proof of the second law

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Being outside $A$ means being in $U$ and not in $A$. Being in $S \cup T$ means being in $S$ or in $T$. And De Morgan for connectives (module 1.4): failing an *or* means failing both parts.</div>
  <div slot="want">$\overline{S \cup T} \subseteq \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$ and the reverse.</div>
  <div slot="how">Chase an arbitrary element. Unfold the set notation into logic, apply the logic law, and fold back up. Both directions are the same unfolding read in opposite orders — because the logic law is an equivalence, not an implication.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in \overline{S \cup T}$ be arbitrary. Then $x \in U$, and $x$ fails to be in $S$ *or* $T$.

By De Morgan's logic law, failing an *or* means failing both parts: $x \notin S$ and $x \notin T$.

Since also $x \in U$, we get $x \in \overline{S}$ and $x \in \overline{T}$, so $x \in \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$ be arbitrary. Then $x \in U$, $x \notin S$, and $x \notin T$. By the same logic law, failing both parts is the same as failing the *or*, so $x \notin S \cup T$. With $x \in U$, this gives $x \in \overline{S \cup T}$.

Both inclusions hold, so $\overline{S \cup T} = \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$.
</Proof>

The first law is proved identically, using the other De Morgan law — failing an *and* means failing at least one part — instead. Alternatively it follows from the second by substituting $\overline S, \overline T$ for $S, T$ and complementing both sides, using $\overline{\overline A} = A$.

## Why the two chapters keep matching

The correspondence is not luck. Sets in this course are specified by conditions (module 2.2), and a set operation is defined *as* an operation on conditions:

| Connective | Set operation | What membership means |
|---|---|---|
| and | $\cap$ | in both sets |
| or | $\cup$ | in at least one of them |
| not | $\overline{\phantom{S}}$ | in the universe but not in the set |
| if–then | $\subseteq$ | being in the first forces being in the second |
| if and only if | $=$ | the two have exactly the same members |

So every logical identity produces a set identity by mechanical translation, and the proof is always the same three moves: unfold, apply the logic law, fold. Once you have seen it twice, the remaining laws of this chapter are exercises.

<Warning title="The connective must swap">
$\overline{S \cap T} \neq \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$ in general. Concretely, in $U = \{1,\ldots,6\}$ with $S = \{1,2,3\}$, $T = \{3,4\}$:
$$\overline{S \cap T} = \overline{\{3\}} = \{1,2,4,5,6\},$$
$$\overline{S} \cap \overline{T} = \{4,5,6\} \cap \{1,2,5,6\} = \{5,6\}.$$
Different sets. Keeping the connective is the same over-negation error as in module 1.4, and one counterexample settles it.
</Warning>

## Where you will use it

Whenever a proof needs the complement of something built from $\cup$ or $\cap$ — which is often, since complements arise from any hypothesis phrased negatively. Two immediate consequences worth recording:

$$
S \setminus (T \cup R) = (S \setminus T) \cap (S \setminus R),
\qquad
S \setminus (T \cap R) = (S \setminus T) \cup (S \setminus R),
$$

obtained by taking $U = S$. These are the forms you actually meet in practice, since set difference is more common than absolute complement.

<Aside>
The laws extend to arbitrarily many sets. For a family $A_1, \ldots, A_n$,
$$\overline{A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n} = \overline{A_1} \cap \cdots \cap \overline{A_n},$$
provable by induction (Chapter 6). Pushed to infinite families, the connectives become quantifiers and this becomes $\neg \exists \equiv \forall \neg$ — module 1.11 again. One law, three levels of generality.
</Aside>
