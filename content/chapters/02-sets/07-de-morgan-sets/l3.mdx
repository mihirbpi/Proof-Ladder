Module 1.4 gave you a rule about words: saying *not* to a joined statement swaps *and* with *or*. This module says the same thing about sets, because the overlap *is* and, the pool *is* or, and the outside *is* not.

## The two laws

Fix a universe. For sets $S$ and $T$:

$$
\text{outside(overlap of } S,T) = \text{pool of outside } S \text{ and outside } T
\qquad\qquad
\text{outside(pool of } S,T) = \text{overlap of outside } S \text{ and outside } T
$$

In words:

- The outside of the overlap is the union of the outsides.
- The outside of the whole thing is the overlap of the outsides.

Look at that shaded border and ask: what is true of a point sitting there? It is not in $S$. It is also not in $T$. Both at once. So it belongs to the outside of $S$ **and** the outside of $T$ — that is, to the overlap of the two outsides.

For the other law, imagine shading everything *except* the middle lens. A point out there is missing at least one of the two circles — it is not in $S$, **or** it is not in $T$. So it belongs to the pool of the two outsides.

## Checking with numbers

Let the universe be the whole numbers from $1$ to $12$. Let
$$
S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\} \quad(\text{divisors of } 12),
\qquad
T = \{2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12\} \quad(\text{even numbers}).
$$

Then the overlap of $S$ and $T$ is $\{2, 4, 6, 12\}$, so
$$
\text{outside that overlap} = \{1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11\}.
$$

Now the other side. $\overline{S} = \{5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11\}$ and $\overline{T} = \{1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11\}$, so
$$
\text{pool of the two outsides} = \{1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11\}.
$$

The same set. That is not a proof — it is one example — but it is a good sanity check, and if the two sides had *differed*, you would have found a counterexample and known something was wrong.

## The dictionary

Here is the table that makes this module almost free.

| Chapter 1 (words) | Chapter 2 (sets) |
|---|---|
| not | complement |
| and | overlap |
| or | pool |
| if–then, checked for every member | sits inside |
| if and only if, checked for every element | $=$ |

Read De Morgan's logic laws through this table and you get De Morgan's set laws. Nothing new happened. The same fact was written in a different alphabet.

<Warning title="Do not forget to swap">
The outside of an overlap is **not** the overlap of the outsides. Check it against the numbers above: the overlap of the two outsides is $\{5,7,9,11\}$, which is much smaller than the outside of the overlap, $\{1,3,5,7,8,9,10,11\}$. Keeping the joining word is the same over-negation mistake you met in module 1.4, and the picture makes it obvious once you look.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Why does this correspondence exist at all? Because sets are *built* from conditions (module 2.2). A set operation is just what happens to the condition. When you complement a set you negate its condition, and when you negate a condition you must apply the logic law. So the set law is the logic law, forced through the definition.
</Aside>
