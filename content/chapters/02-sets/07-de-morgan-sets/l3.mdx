Module 1.4 gave you a rule about words: saying *not* to a joined statement swaps *and* with *or*. This module says the same thing about sets, because $\cap$ *is* and, $\cup$ *is* or, and complement *is* not.

## The two laws

Fix a universe. For sets $S$ and $T$:

$$
\overline{S \cap T} = \overline{S} \cup \overline{T}
\qquad\qquad
\overline{S \cup T} = \overline{S} \cap \overline{T}
$$

In words:

- The outside of the overlap is the union of the outsides.
- The outside of the whole thing is the overlap of the outsides.

Look at that shaded border and ask: what is true of a point sitting there? It is not in $S$. It is also not in $T$. Both at once. So it belongs to $\overline{S}$ **and** to $\overline{T}$ — that is, to $\overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$.

For the other law, imagine shading everything *except* the middle lens. A point out there is missing at least one of the two circles — it is not in $S$, **or** it is not in $T$. So it belongs to $\overline{S} \cup \overline{T}$.

## Checking with numbers

Let the universe be the whole numbers from $1$ to $12$. Let
$$
S = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12\} \quad(\text{divisors of } 12),
\qquad
T = \{2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12\} \quad(\text{even numbers}).
$$

Then $S \cap T = \{2, 4, 6, 12\}$, so
$$
\overline{S \cap T} = \{1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11\}.
$$

Now the other side. $\overline{S} = \{5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11\}$ and $\overline{T} = \{1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11\}$, so
$$
\overline{S} \cup \overline{T} = \{1, 3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11\}.
$$

The same set. That is not a proof — it is one example — but it is a good sanity check, and if the two sides had *differed*, you would have found a counterexample and known something was wrong.

## The dictionary

Here is the table that makes this module almost free.

| Chapter 1 (words) | Chapter 2 (sets) |
|---|---|
| not | complement |
| and | $\cap$ |
| or | $\cup$ |
| if–then, checked for every element | $\subseteq$ |
| if and only if, checked for every element | $=$ |

Read De Morgan's logic laws through this table and you get De Morgan's set laws. Nothing new happened. The same fact was written in a different alphabet.

<Warning title="Do not forget to swap">
$\overline{S \cap T}$ is **not** $\overline{S} \cap \overline{T}$. Check with the numbers above: $\overline{S} \cap \overline{T} = \{5,7,9,11\}$, which is much smaller than $\overline{S \cap T}$. Keeping the connective is the same over-negation mistake you met in module 1.4, and the picture makes it obvious once you look.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Why does this correspondence exist at all? Because sets are *built* from conditions (module 2.2). A set operation is just what happens to the condition. When you complement a set you negate its condition, and when you negate a condition you must apply the logic law. So the set law is the logic law, forced through the definition.
</Aside>
