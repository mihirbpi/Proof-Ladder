Within a fixed set $U$ — the **universe** for the discussion — the **complement** of $A$ is everything in $U$ outside $A$:
$$
\overline{A} = \{\, x \in U \mid x \notin A \,\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

  <VennTwo labelS="A" labelT="B" shade={["outside", "T-only"]} universe="U" />
</Figure>

Complement is $\neg$ in the dictionary of module 2.4, and like $\neg$ it splits everything into two parts with no overlap and no gap:
$$
A \cap \overline{A} = \emptyset, \qquad A \cup \overline{A} = U.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\emptyset$ is the empty collection, $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

<Warning title="The universe is part of the answer">
$\overline{A}$ is meaningless until $U$ is fixed, and changing $U$ changes the answer.

Let $A$ be the set of even integers.

- In $U = \mathbb{Z}$: $\overline{A}$ is the odd integers.
- In $U = \mathbb{R}$: $\overline{A}$ contains the odd integers **and** $\tfrac12$, and $\pi$, and every non-integer.

Two different sets. When a problem says "the complement", find out what the universe is before answering.
</Warning>

## Suppose not

The complement is what makes a proof technique available. To prove a statement $p$, you may instead show that $\neg p$ is impossible.

> **Proof by contradiction.** To prove $p$: assume $\neg p$, derive a statement that is false, and conclude $p$.

The named move is **suppose not**. It is legitimate because module 1.2 guarantees exactly one of $p$, $\neg p$ is true — so ruling out one leaves the other, with nowhere else to go.

The template:

> 1. State clearly: *Suppose, for contradiction, that $\neg p$.*
> 2. Reason forward, using $\neg p$ as an ordinary hypothesis.
> 3. Reach a contradiction — a statement and its negation, or something known false.
> 4. Conclude: *This is impossible, so $p$.*

Step 1 is not decoration. A reader who does not know an assumption is temporary will think you are asserting it.

## A worked contradiction

> **Proposition.** There is no smallest positive rational number.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The claim is a negative one — *there is no…* — and there is nothing to compute with. Module 1.8's advice: negative goals are where indirect methods pay.</div>
  <div slot="want">A contradiction from assuming such a number exists.</div>
  <div slot="how">Assume $q$ is the smallest. Then produce something smaller that is still positive and still rational. Halving does both.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose, for contradiction, that there is a smallest positive rational $q$.

Consider $q/2$. Since $q > 0$, we have $q/2 > 0$. Since $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under division by $2$, we have $q/2 \in \mathbb{Q}$. So $q/2$ is a positive rational.

But $q/2 < q$, since $q > 0$.

So $q/2$ is a positive rational smaller than $q$, contradicting the assumption that $q$ was the smallest.

This is impossible, so no smallest positive rational exists.
</Proof>

Notice what the assumption bought. Before it, the claim mentioned no particular number and there was nothing to manipulate. Afterwards there was a $q$ on the page, and $q$ could be halved. **The contradiction hypothesis is often the only concrete object in the proof**, and that is exactly why the method works on negative claims.

## When not to use it

<Warning title="Contradiction is over-used">
A very common pattern in student work:

> Suppose $n$ is even and $n^2$ is odd. Since $n$ is even, $n = 2k$, so $n^2 = 2(2k^2)$ is even. But we assumed $n^2$ is odd — contradiction.

The proof is correct, and the contradiction is doing nothing. Delete the first and last sentences and you have a direct proof that is shorter and clearer.

**The test:** if you never actually use the assumption $\neg p$ to get anywhere — if the contradiction arrives just by proving $p$ directly and then noticing the clash — you wanted a direct proof.

Reach for contradiction when the goal is a negative or a non-existence claim, where a direct approach has nothing to start from.
</Warning>

## The two indirect methods are different

Both assume something negative, and they are not the same move.

| | Assume | Derive | Concludes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contrapositive (1.8) | $\neg q$ | $\neg p$ | $p \Rightarrow q$ |
| Contradiction | $p \wedge \neg q$ | anything false | $p \Rightarrow q$ |

Contradiction gives you **more** to work with: both the hypothesis and the negated conclusion. That extra hypothesis is why it succeeds on problems the contrapositive cannot reach — and it is also why the resulting proofs are harder to read, since you cannot tell which statements on the page are true until the end.

Prefer direct. Then contrapositive. Then contradiction.

<Aside>
The most famous proof in this course is a contradiction, and you will meet it in module 4.7: **$\sqrt2$ is irrational.**

You already know the fact. The proof assumes $\sqrt2 = a/b$ in lowest terms, and derives that $a$ and $b$ are both even — contradicting *lowest terms*.

The shape is exactly the one above. *Irrational* means *not rational*, a negative claim with nothing to compute with; assuming the negation puts an actual fraction on the page; and the fraction can be manipulated.
</Aside>
