## Complement

Fix a **universe** $S$ — the collection everything in the discussion is drawn from. For a subset $A$ of it, the **complement** of $A$ is everything in $S$ that is *not* in $A$. It is written with a bar on top:

$$\overline{A}$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: $\overline{A}$ is everything in the universe that is not in $A$.*

The working form, the one every proof below uses:

> Something is in $\overline A$ exactly when it is in the universe **and** not in $A$.

Complement completes the dictionary of module 2.4: overlap is *and*, pool is *or*, and now outside is **not**.

## The universe must be named

Let $A = \{0, 1\}$.

- among the counting numbers, the outside of $A$ is $\{2, 3, 4, \ldots\}$;
- among the integers, it also picks up every negative number;
- among all the numbers on the line, it is everything but two points.

Three different answers to "what is outside $A$?", and the only thing that changed was the universe. **The bar is meaningless until the universe is named.**

When the universe is not obvious, write $S \setminus A$ instead — read "$S$ take away $A$". It names both collections, so it cannot be misread, and it means exactly the same thing.

The most important case in this course: the **irrational** numbers are the real numbers take away the fractions. That $\sqrt2$ belongs to this set is module 4.7, and its proof is the flagship application of the technique below.

## Basic identities

Each of these is the definition plus one fact from Chapter 1, and each is worth reading as a sentence rather than a formula:

| The identity | Why it holds |
|---|---|
| the outside of the outside of $A$ is $A$ again | denying a denial (module 1.2) |
| nothing at all is in both $A$ and its outside | nothing is both true and false |
| everything in the universe is in $A$ or its outside | every statement is true or false |
| the outside of the whole universe is empty | there is nothing left over |
| the outside of the empty set is the whole universe | nothing was taken away |

The middle two together say that $A$ and its outside split the universe cleanly: **every member of the universe is in exactly one of them.** That is what "every statement is either true or false" looks like once it is about collections instead of sentences.

## Proof by contradiction

Statements about outsides are negative ones — saying a thing is in $\overline A$ is saying it is **not** in $A$ — and a negative goal gives you nothing to build with. The technique for those is **proof by contradiction**, named move **suppose not**.

> To prove a claim: assume it is false, follow that assumption until you reach something that contradicts itself, and conclude the claim was true after all.

The reason it works is module 1.3. If assuming the claim false leads to an impossibility, then it cannot be false — and exactly one of *true* and *false* is available.

## The proposition

> **Proposition.** Let $A, B \subseteq S$. If $A \subseteq B$, then $\overline{B} \subseteq \overline{A}$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Every member of $A$ is a member of $B$. To show one collection sits inside another, follow a single member through, assuming nothing about it.</div>
  <div slot="want">Anything outside $B$ is outside $A$.</div>
  <div slot="how">The goal is negative — *not in $A$* — so there is nothing to build with directly. Suppose not: assume the thing **is** in $A$, and use the hypothesis to put it in $B$, which is exactly what it was not.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Take anything outside $B$. It is in the universe, and it is not in $B$. We have to show it is not in $A$ either.

Suppose not — suppose it **is** in $A$. Every member of $A$ is a member of $B$, so it is in $B$. But we started by saying it is not in $B$. It cannot be both.

So the supposition fails: the thing is not in $A$, which is to say it is outside $A$. Nothing was assumed about which thing we took, so everything outside $B$ is outside $A$.
</Proof>

Notice that the containment **turns around**: $A$ inside $B$ became the outside of $B$ inside the outside of $A$.

That is the contrapositive of module 1.7, wearing set clothing. *In $A$ forces in $B$* and *not in $B$ forces not in $A$* are the same statement, and this proposition is that fact translated.

## The shape of a contradiction proof

1. State the goal, then write "suppose not" and the exact opposite of it. Getting that opposite right needs module 1.11 — assume the wrong thing and you prove nothing at all.
2. Reason forward, using the false assumption exactly like any other hypothesis.
3. Arrive at an explicit contradiction and say what it is.
4. Conclude.

<Warning title="Contradiction is over-used">
Many student proofs open with "suppose not", never use the contradictory assumption, and end by deriving the conclusion directly. That is a direct proof wearing a costume — delete the costume.

A related case: if you assume an if–then is false — so the *if* holds and the *then* fails — and end up denying the *if*, you have written a contrapositive proof (module 1.8). It is cleaner stated that way, because the reader does not have to hold a false assumption for the length of the argument.

Reach for contradiction when the goal says *nonexistence* or *impossibility*, where there is genuinely nothing to construct.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The greatest hits of this technique are all nonexistence claims: there is no rational whose square is $2$ (module 4.7); there is no largest prime (Euclid); there is no way to pair the counting numbers off against all the numbers on the line (Cantor). Each begins by assuming the object exists and extracting an impossibility from it, and in each case no direct construction was available, because the theorem says nothing is there to construct.
</Aside>
