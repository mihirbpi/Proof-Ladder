## Complement

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\subseteq$ reads "is a subset of".*

Fix a **universe** $S$. For $A \subseteq S$, the **complement** of $A$ in $S$ is
$$
\overline{A} = \{\, x \in S \mid x \notin A \,\},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

so that
$$
x \in \overline{A} \iff x \in S \wedge x \notin A.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\wedge$ is "and", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

Complement completes the dictionary: $\cap \leftrightarrow \wedge$, $\cup \leftrightarrow \vee$, $\overline{\phantom{A}} \leftrightarrow \neg$.

## The universe must be named

Let $A = \{0, 1\}$.

- In $\mathbb{N}$: $\overline{A} = \{2, 3, 4, \ldots\}$.
- In $\mathbb{Z}$: $\overline{A} = \{\ldots, -2, -1\} \cup \{2, 3, \ldots\}$.
- In $\mathbb{R}$: $\overline{A} = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0,1\}$, which is everything on the line but two points.

The notation $\overline{A}$ is meaningless without $S$ fixed. When the universe is not clear from context, use the **set difference** notation instead:
$$
S \setminus A = \{x \in S \mid x \notin A\},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

which names both sets and cannot be misread. Note $\overline{A} = S \setminus A$ by definition.

The most important instance in this course: the **irrationals** are $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$, the complement of $\mathbb{Q}$ *in $\mathbb{R}$*. That $\sqrt2$ belongs to this set is module 4.7, and its proof is the flagship application of the technique below.

## Basic identities

All immediate from the definition plus the corresponding logical fact:

$$
\overline{\overline{A}} = A \quad (\neg\neg p \equiv p), \qquad
A \cap \overline{A} = \emptyset \quad (p \wedge \neg p), \qquad
A \cup \overline{A} = S \quad (p \vee \neg p),
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\emptyset$ is the empty collection, $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\cup$ is "pooled with".*

$$
\overline{S} = \emptyset, \qquad \overline{\emptyset} = S.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\emptyset$ is the empty collection, the bar on top is "everything outside".*

The pair $A \cap \overline A = \emptyset$, $A \cup \overline A = S$ says that $A$ and $\overline A$ *partition* $S$: every element of the universe is in exactly one. That is the set-theoretic content of excluded middle.

## Proof by contradiction

Complement statements are negative — $x \in \overline{A}$ asserts $x \notin A$ — and negative goals resist direct attack. The technique for them is **proof by contradiction**, named move **suppose not**.

> To prove $p$: assume $\neg p$; derive a statement of the form $r \wedge \neg r$; conclude $p$.

The justification is module 1.3. If $\neg p$ leads to a contradiction, $\neg p$ cannot be true; since exactly one of $p, \neg p$ is true, $p$ holds.

## The proposition

> **Proposition.** Let $A, B \subseteq S$. If $A \subseteq B$, then $\overline{B} \subseteq \overline{A}$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$A \subseteq B$, i.e. every element of $A$ lies in $B$. To show a subset relation we must chase an arbitrary element of $\overline B$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$x \in \overline{B} \Rightarrow x \in \overline{A}$; that is, $x \notin B \Rightarrow x \notin A$.</div>
  <div slot="how">The goal $x \notin A$ is negative, so a direct derivation has nothing to work with. Suppose not: assume $x \in A$ and use the hypothesis to reach $x \in B$, contradicting $x \notin B$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $x \in \overline{B}$ be arbitrary, so $x \in S$ and $x \notin B$. We must show $x \notin A$.

Suppose not: suppose $x \in A$. Since $A \subseteq B$, it follows that $x \in B$. But $x \notin B$. So $x \in B$ and $x \notin B$ both hold, which is a contradiction.

Hence the supposition fails, so $x \notin A$, i.e. $x \in \overline{A}$. As $x$ was arbitrary, $\overline{B} \subseteq \overline{A}$.
</Proof>

Notice that the containment **reverses**. Complementation is order-reversing, and this is the same phenomenon as the contrapositive (module 1.7): $A \subseteq B$ says $x \in A \Rightarrow x \in B$, and $\overline B \subseteq \overline A$ says $x \notin B \Rightarrow x \notin A$. The proposition *is* contraposition, translated.

## The shape of a contradiction proof

1. State the goal, then write "suppose not" and the precise negation. Getting the negation right requires module 1.11 — a mis-negated assumption produces a proof of nothing.
2. Reason forward, using the false assumption exactly like any other hypothesis.
3. Arrive at an explicit contradiction and say what it is.
4. Conclude.

<Warning title="Contradiction is over-used">
Many student proofs open with "suppose not", never use the contradictory assumption, and end by deriving the conclusion directly. That is a direct proof wearing a costume — delete the costume.

A related case: if you assume $\neg(p \Rightarrow q)$, i.e. $p \wedge \neg q$, and derive $\neg p$, you have written a contrapositive proof (module 1.8). It is cleaner stated that way, because the reader does not have to hold a false assumption for the length of the argument.

Reach for contradiction when the goal asserts *nonexistence* or *impossibility*, where there is genuinely nothing to construct.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The greatest hits of this technique are all nonexistence claims: there is no rational whose square is $2$ (module 4.7); there is no largest prime (Euclid); there is no bijection from $\mathbb{N}$ to $\mathbb{R}$ (Cantor). Each begins by assuming the object exists and extracting an impossibility from it, and in each case no direct construction was available, because the theorem says nothing is there to construct.
</Aside>
