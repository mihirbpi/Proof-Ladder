Your course calls it *indirect proof*: assume the opposite, follow it until something impossible appears, conclude the opposite was wrong. This module names the operation that produces "the opposite", and then justifies the technique — which your textbook uses without ever quite explaining why it is allowed.

## Complement

Fix a collection to draw from $U$. For $S \subseteq U$, the **complement** is

$$
\overline{S} = \{\,x \in U : x \notin S\,\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: the complement is everything in the surrounding collection that is not in $S$.*

Complement is $\neg$, exactly as $\cap$ was $\wedge$.

</Figure>

<Warning title="The surrounding collection decides the answer">
Let $S$ be the points of a circle.

- In the **plane**, $\overline{S}$ is the interior together with the exterior.
- In **space**, $\overline{S}$ additionally contains every point off the plane.

Same $S$, different complements. A complement without a stated $U$ is not a set — it is an unfinished description, and the dashed box in the figure is drawn exactly to keep $U$ visible.
</Warning>

Two facts, both immediate:

$$\overline{\overline{S}} = S, \qquad S \cap \overline{S} = \emptyset, \qquad S \cup \overline{S} = U.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\emptyset$ is the empty collection, $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: complementing twice returns you to the start; a set and its complement share nothing and together fill everything.*

The first is $\neg\neg p \equiv p$. The second is that $p \wedge \neg p$ is a contradiction. The third is that $p \vee \neg p$ is a tautology — the law of the excluded middle, and the license for every case split you have ever written.

## Why indirect proof works

Here is the argument your textbook leaves implicit.

You want to prove $q$. Suppose instead $\neg q$, and derive a contradiction — some statement $r \wedge \neg r$.

A contradiction is false in every row of its truth table. If $\neg q$ implied it, then $\neg q$ cannot be true, since a true statement never implies a false one. So $\neg q$ is false, and by $\neg\neg q \equiv q$, $q$ is true.

**The named move is suppose not.** In set language: you show that $\overline{Q}$ is empty, and therefore $Q$ is everything.

## The standard shape

> **Suppose not.** Suppose $\neg q$.
>
> … derivation, using $\neg q$ freely, and the hypotheses too …
>
> This contradicts [some proved fact]. So $q$.

Three obligations, and all three are places students lose marks:

1. **State the assumption explicitly.** A reader must know which sentences are being made under a false supposition.
2. **Name the contradiction.** Not "this is impossible" — *"this contradicts the Triangle Inequality"*, or *"this contradicts our assumption that $\ell_1 \neq \ell_2$."*
3. **Discharge it.** The final line must return to $q$. A proof that stops at the contradiction has not stated its conclusion.

## A geometry example

> **Proposition.** In a plane, if $\ell_1 \parallel \ell_2$ and $\ell_2 \parallel \ell_3$, then $\ell_1 \parallel \ell_3$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Parallel means coplanar and non-intersecting. Available: through a point not on a given line there is exactly one parallel to it (the Parallel Postulate).</div>
  <div slot="want">$\ell_1 \parallel \ell_3$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Directly, we would have to show no point of $\ell_1$ lies on $\ell_3$ — infinitely many points to check, and no way to start. Suppose not: they meet somewhere, and that gives us a *specific point* to reason about. Uniqueness in the Parallel Postulate is what will break.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose not: suppose $\ell_1$ and $\ell_3$ are not parallel. Being coplanar, they meet at some point $P$.

Then $P$ lies on $\ell_1$, which is parallel to $\ell_2$, and $P$ lies on $\ell_3$, which is also parallel to $\ell_2$.

So $\ell_1$ and $\ell_3$ are two distinct lines through $P$, each parallel to $\ell_2$. This contradicts the Parallel Postulate, which gives exactly one such line.

So $\ell_1 \parallel \ell_3$.
</Proof>

Notice what "suppose not" bought. Directly, the goal was a claim about infinitely many non-intersections. Negated, it produced a single named point $P$ — something to work with. **That trade is the reason for the technique**, and it is worth watching for: when your goal is a negative claim, its negation is usually a positive one.

## A set proposition

> **Proposition.** If $A \subseteq B$, then $\overline{B} \subseteq \overline{A}$.

Complementation reverses inclusion. This is contraposition (module 1.8) in set clothing.

<Proof>
Let $x$ be an arbitrary element of $\overline{B}$, so $x \in U$ and $x \notin B$.

Suppose, for contradiction, that $x \in A$. Since $A \subseteq B$, so $x \in B$ — contradicting $x \notin B$.

So $x \notin A$, and therefore $x \in \overline{A}$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $\overline{B}\subseteq\overline{A}$.
</Proof>

<Aside>
That proof was a contradiction wrapped around a contrapositive — exactly the pattern module 1.8 warned about. The inner argument is just: $x \in A \Rightarrow x \in B$, contraposed.

Written directly: *let $x \in \overline B$. Since $A \subseteq B$ gives $x \in A \Rightarrow x \in B$, the contrapositive gives $x \notin B \Rightarrow x \notin A$. So $x \in \overline A$.* Shorter, and no false assumption to carry.

Run the self-check on your own indirect proofs. If you never used the contradiction — if you only derived $\neg p$ from $\neg q$ — remove the wrapper.
</Aside>

## When to reach for it

Contradiction earns its cost when the goal is:

- **a negation** — "no triangle has two right angles"
- **a uniqueness claim** — "suppose two, and they must coincide"
- **an impossibility** — "$\sqrt2$ is not rational" (Chapter 4)
- **a claim about infinitely many cases**, like the parallel proposition above

In each, the negation supplies a concrete object where the original supplied none.

<Warning title="Contradiction is not a default">
The genuine cost is that every intermediate line is stated under a false assumption, so nothing you derive is true. A reader must hold that in mind throughout, and a long contradiction proof is genuinely harder to check than a long direct one.

There is also a failure mode: deriving a contradiction from a mistake of your own rather than from the assumption. When a contradiction appears, look at whether the assumption really caused it — because if the error is yours, the proof proves nothing.
</Warning>
