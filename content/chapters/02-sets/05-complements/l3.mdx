Union and intersection came from *and* and *or*. There is one joining word left over from Chapter 1 — *not* — and it gives the third set operation.

## The complement

Fix a set $S$ to be the whole world for the moment. We call it the **universe**. For a set $A$ inside it, the **complement** of $A$ is everything in $S$ that is not in $A$:
$$
\overline{A} = \{\, x \in S \mid x \notin A \,\}.
$$

## The universe is not optional

This is the point of the module, so here it is twice.

Take $A = \{0, 1\}$.

- Inside the whole numbers, $\overline{A} = \{2, 3, 4, 5, \ldots\}$.
- Inside all the numbers on the number line, $\overline{A}$ is enormous: it contains $\tfrac12$, $7$, $\pi$, everything except two points.

Same $A$. Completely different complements. So writing $\overline{A}$ without saying what the universe is means nothing at all.

A useful check: $\overline{A}$ always depends on two things, not one.

## A famous complement

Inside the real numbers, the complement of the fractions is called the **irrational numbers**. So $\pi$ is irrational, and so is the number that multiplies by itself to give $2$ — a fact that takes real work to prove, and module 4.7 does it.

## Suppose not

Complements are naturally *negative*: to say $x \in \overline{A}$ is to say $x$ is **not** something. Negative goals are awkward to attack head-on, because "not" gives you nothing to substitute.

So here is the technique that handles them, and it is one of the great ones. It is called **proof by contradiction**, and the named move is **suppose not**.

> To prove a claim: assume the claim is false. Reason carefully. If you arrive at something impossible, then the assumption must have been wrong — so the claim is true.

## A worked example

> **Claim.** Let $S$ be the whole numbers, and let $E$ be the even ones. Then no element of $\overline{E}$ is a multiple of $4$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$\overline{E}$ is the odd numbers — the whole numbers not in $E$. And every multiple of $4$ is a pile of $4$s.</div>
  <div slot="want">Nothing in $\overline{E}$ is a multiple of $4$.</div>
  <div slot="how">The goal is a *nothing* claim, which is hard to attack directly. So suppose not: pretend some element of $\overline{E}$ *is* a multiple of $4$, and see what breaks.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose not. That is, suppose some number in $\overline{E}$ is also a multiple of $4$.

Being a multiple of $4$ means it is a pile of $4$s. Split every $4$ into $2 + 2$: nothing is added and nothing thrown away, and now the pile is made of $2$s.

So the number is a multiple of $2$ — that is, it is even, and it belongs to $E$.

Now $n$ is in $\overline{E}$ and also in $E$. But $\overline{E}$ is exactly the numbers *not* in $E$. So $n$ is both in $E$ and not in $E$, which is impossible.

Our supposition led to something impossible, so it was false. No element of $\overline{E}$ is a multiple of $4$.
</Proof>

## What made that work

Every *suppose not* proof has the same three beats.

1. **Assume the opposite of the goal.** Say so out loud: "suppose not."
2. **Reason honestly.** You are allowed to use the false assumption exactly like any other fact.
3. **Hit a wall.** Something has to come out impossible — usually a statement and its own opposite, both true at once (a contradiction, module 1.3).

Then you conclude that the assumption was to blame, because everything else you did was correct.

<Warning title="Do not reach for it too fast">
Contradiction is powerful, so it is tempting to use it everywhere. Resist. A direct proof is easier to read and easier to check, because the reader does not have to carry a false assumption in their head. Save *suppose not* for goals that are negative or that claim something does not exist.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Two facts worth noticing about complements, both easy to see in the picture: $\overline{\overline{A}} = A$ — the outside of the outside is the inside — and $A \cap \overline{A} = \{\,\}$, since nothing can be both in and out. These are the set versions of two things you already know: saying *not* twice gets you back where you started, and nothing can be both true and not true at once.
</Aside>
