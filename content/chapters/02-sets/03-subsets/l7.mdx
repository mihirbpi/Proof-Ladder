$$
A \subseteq S \quad:\Leftrightarrow\quad \forall x\;(x \in A \Rightarrow x \in S).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces". So the line reads: $A$ sits inside $S$ exactly when being in $A$ forces being in $S$, for every $x$.*

The definition is a if–then claim made about every element, and everything in this module is a consequence of that single observation. §1.12 fixed the proof template for such statements; applied to sets it gets the name **element chasing**, and it is the technique on which essentially all of naive set theory runs.

## The template

> Let $x \in A$ be arbitrary. [Use only what $x \in A$ gives you.] Therefore $x \in S$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $A \subseteq S$.

The discipline of §1.12 applies verbatim: the element is chosen at the start and never changed, and any assumption about $x$ beyond membership in $A$ invalidates the generalization.

> **Proposition.** $S = (-3,5) \subseteq T = (-6,5]$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in S \iff -3 < x < 5$; $x \in T \iff -6 < x \leq 5$. Available: transitivity of $<$, and that $a < b$ implies $a \leq b$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$-6 < x$ and $x \leq 5$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Both halves of the goal follow from the two halves of the hypothesis by a single step each. Note $T$ is the weaker constraint on both ends, so the containment should be immediate — and it should be *proper*, witnessed by the endpoint $5$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $x \in S$ be arbitrary; then $-3 < x < 5$.

From $-6 < -3$ and $-3 < x$, transitivity gives $-6 < x$. From $x < 5$ we obtain $x \leq 5$.

So $-6 < x \leq 5$, i.e. $x \in T$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $S \subseteq T$.
</Proof>

The containment is proper: $5 \in T \setminus S$. Properness is a separate claim requiring a separate witness (§1.12), and a subset proof never proves it.

## Refutation

$\neg(A \subseteq S)$ is, by §1.11,
$$
\exists x\;(x \in A \wedge x \notin S),
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\wedge$ is "and".*

so refuting a containment means exhibiting one element of $A$ outside $S$ — and nothing less. Here $5 \in T$, $5 \notin S$ refutes $T \subseteq S$.

<Warning title="Membership and inclusion are different relations">
$\in$ relates an object to a set; $\subseteq$ relates two sets. With $A = \{1, \{2,3\}, \emptyset\}$:

$1 \in A$ ✓ · $\{2,3\} \in A$ ✓ · $2 \in A$ ✗ · $\emptyset \in A$ ✓ (it was listed) · $\emptyset \subseteq A$ ✓ (always) · $\{1\} \subseteq A$ ✓ · $\{2,3\} \subseteq A$ ✗ (since $2 \notin A$).

Note that $\emptyset \in A$ and $\emptyset \subseteq A$ are both true here for entirely different reasons. Note also that $\in$ is *not* transitive: $2 \in \{2,3\}$ and $\{2,3\} \in A$, but $2 \notin A$. Inclusion is transitive; membership is not, and conflating them is the standard source of nonsense in early set-theoretic writing.
</Warning>

## True because there is nothing to check

> **Proposition.** $\emptyset \subseteq S$ for every set $S$.

<Proof>
Pick any $x$ you like. The hypothesis of $x \in \emptyset \Rightarrow x \in S$ is false, so the conditional is true (§1.5). Since $x$ was arbitrary, $\forall x\,(x \in \emptyset \Rightarrow x \in S)$, i.e. $\emptyset \subseteq S$.
</Proof>

This is vacuous truth carrying structural weight. Every distributive, De Morgan, and product identity in this chapter holds without exception for $\emptyset$ exactly because of it; a convention that made $\emptyset \subseteq S$ fail would introduce a special case into every theorem that follows.

## Containment behaves like $\leq$

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\cap$ reads "intersection".*

Three properties:

- **Reflexive.** $S \subseteq S$, since $x \in S \Rightarrow x \in S$.
- **Transitive.** If $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq C$ then $A \subseteq C$: chase $x \in A$ into $B$, then into $C$.
- **Antisymmetric.** If $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq A$ then $A = B$ — this is being fixed by its members, and it is the content of §2.6.

Those are the three properties $\leq$ has on the numbers, so containment orders the subsets of $X$ much as $\leq$ orders numbers — with one difference. Some pairs simply cannot be compared: $\{1,2\}$ and $\{2,3\}$ are incomparable. That failure of comparability is not a defect — it is the reason set inclusion carries more information than a linear ordering, and it is why the language of *greatest* and *least* ($\cap$ as the largest set inside both, $\cup$ as the smallest containing both) is the right one for §2.4.

## The power set

$$
\mathcal{P}(X) = \{\, A \mid A \subseteq X \,\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: the power set of $X$ is the collection of all collections sitting inside $X$.*

For $X = \{a,b\}$: $\mathcal{P}(X) = \{\emptyset, \{a\}, \{b\}, \{a,b\}\}$. For finite $X$, $|\mathcal{P}(X)| = 2^{|X|}$ — each element is independently in or out, giving $2^{|X|}$ characteriztic functions $X \to \{0,1\}$, so the notation $2^X$. §6.3 proves the count by induction.

Two remarks worth carrying. First, $\mathcal{P}(X)$ is a set whose elements are sets, and keeping track of which level you are on is a real skill: $a \in X$, $\{a\} \in \mathcal{P}(X)$, $\{\{a\}\} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$. Second, no function from $X$ to $\mathcal{P}(X)$ can hit everything, so $\mathcal{P}(X)$ is always strictly bigger than $X$ — for every set, finite or infinite. Module 6.7 states that and proves it. Two things follow, and both are worth carrying: there is no largest infinity, and there is no set containing everything.

<Aside>
The relation $\subseteq$ is definable from $\in$ alone, which is why axiomatic set theory needs only one undefined relation. Everything in this chapter — union, intersection, complement, product, function — is likewise built from $\in$ by explicit definition. That economy is the point of taking sets as the foundation.
</Aside>

> Let $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W$ be arbitrary and let $c$ be a scalar. … Therefore $\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\in W$ and $c\mathbf{u}\in W$.

Take any one — or here, any two — and follow them. Nothing about the specific vectors may be used beyond membership in $W$, which is what makes the argument cover all of them.

**In calculus the containments are between domains and intervals.** A theorem may hold on the inside of a stretch while the endpoints need a separate argument — the statement is trapped between an open interval and the closed one, and the two endpoints are exactly what is left undecided. That is why endpoints get checked on their own, every time.

And the vacuous containment $\emptyset\subseteq S$ is not a curiosity there either: the set where a function fails a condition is often empty, and "every point of that set has property $P$" is then true by the argument given above.
