$$
A \subseteq S \quad\text{means}\quad \forall x\ (x \in A \Rightarrow x \in S).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

$A$ is a **subset** of $S$ when every element of $A$ is an element of $S$.

Read that definition carefully, because it is a *if–then claim made about every element* — the two constructions of modules 1.5 and 1.10 stacked. And the shape of the definition dictates the shape of every proof about it, with no choices left to make:

> **To prove $A \subseteq S$: take any element of $A$, assume nothing else about it, and follow it until you reach $S$.**

That is the named move, **take any one and follow it**. It is Template 1 of module 1.12 with the details filled in.

## A first proof

> **Proposition.** $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$.

Most books say this. It is provable, and the proof is short.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">A rational number is one that *can be written* as a whole number over a non-zero whole number — being expressible that way is the definition, not being written that way already.</div>
  <div slot="want">An arbitrary integer, exhibited in that form.</div>
  <div slot="how">Give the ratio. The only obligation is checking the denominator is nonzero.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $n$ be any integer. Then
$$
n = \frac{n}{1},
$$
and $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, $1 \in \mathbb{Z}$, $1 \neq 0$. Then $n$ is a ratio of integers with nonzero denominator, so $n \in \mathbb{Q}$.

As $n$ was arbitrary, $\mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Q}$.
</Proof>

The content is the observation that being rational is a matter of being *expressible* as a ratio. $7$ does not look like a fraction, and it is one.

## Two special cases

**Every set is a subset of itself.** Take any element of $S$; it is in $S$. That is the whole proof. It is not a trick, it is what the definition says, and it will matter in module 2.6.

**The empty set is a subset of everything.**

<Warning title="The empty set sits inside everything by vacuous truth, not by decree">
To prove it, you have to show that every element of the empty set is in $S$.

Take an element of the empty set. There are none — so the *if* part is false whatever you hold up, and an if–then with a false *if* part is true (module 1.5). Every case is vacuously satisfied because there are no cases.

Students often want this to be a convention adopted for tidiness. It is not. It is forced by the definition of *subset* together with the truth table for if–then, and any other answer would contradict one of them.
</Warning>

## Proper subsets

$A \subsetneq S$ means $A \subseteq S$ **and** $A \neq S$ — a **proper subset**, one that leaves something out.

$$
\mathbb{Z} \subsetneq \mathbb{Q}, \qquad \text{since } \tfrac12 \in \mathbb{Q} \text{ and } \tfrac12 \notin \mathbb{Z}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\subsetneq$ is "sits strictly inside", $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in".*

Proving properness is a second job: containment, plus **one witness** in $S \setminus A$. One element is enough, because $A \neq S$ is the negation of a set equality, and module 1.11 says a single failure refutes a universal claim.

Some authors write $\subset$ for proper containment and others for containment in general, so $\subset$ is genuinely ambiguous in the literature. This course uses $\subseteq$ and $\subsetneq$, which are never ambiguous.

## Two structural facts

**Reflexive.** $S \subseteq S$, shown above.

**Transitive.** If $A$ sits inside $B$ and $B$ sits inside $C$, then $A$ sits inside $C$.

<Proof>
Assume $A \subseteq B$ and $B \subseteq C$. Let $x \in A$ be arbitrary.

Since $A \subseteq B$ and $x \in A$, we get $x \in B$.

Since $B \subseteq C$ and $x \in B$, we get $x \in C$.

As $x$ was arbitrary, $A \subseteq C$.
</Proof>

Two applications of a conditional, in sequence — the *dominoes* pattern of module 1.6, at its shortest.

Transitivity is what makes a chain like
$$
\mathbb{N} \subseteq \mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{R}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers, $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\subseteq$ is "sits inside".*

mean what you expect — the counting numbers sit inside the reals, with no separate proof needed.

<Warning title="Being an element and being a subset are different relations">
Membership relates an object to a set. Being a subset relates a set to a set. Mixing them is the most common error in this chapter.

Let $S = \{1, 2\}$. Then:

- $1$ is an **element** of $S$ ✓, but $1$ is not a **subset** of it ✗ — $1$ is not a set at all.
- $\{1\}$ is a **subset** of $S$ ✓, but not an **element** of it ✗ — the elements of $S$ are $1$ and $2$, not the set $\{1\}$.

The test is one question: *is the thing on the left an element of $S$, or a collection of them?*
</Warning>

<Aside>
Being a subset behaves like $\leq$, and the resemblance is not accidental. Both are reflexive and transitive, and in both, comparison running two ways forces equality — $a \leq b$ and $b \leq a$ give $a = b$, and module 2.6 proves the same for $\subseteq$.

One difference matters. Any two real numbers compare: either $a \leq b$ or $b \leq a$. Sets need not. Take $A = \{1,2\}$ and $B = \{2,3\}$: neither contains the other. **Sets can be genuinely incomparable**, and that is why you cannot line all subsets of a set up in a row the way you can line up numbers.
</Aside>
