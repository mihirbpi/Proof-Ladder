$$
S \cap (T \cup R) = (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R), \tag{D1}
$$
$$
S \cup (T \cap R) = (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R). \tag{D2}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: each operation spreads across the other, which arithmetic manages in only one direction.*

Both are transcriptions of logical equivalences,
$$
p \wedge (q \vee r) \equiv (p \wedge q) \vee (p \wedge r),
\qquad
p \vee (q \wedge r) \equiv (p \vee q) \wedge (p \vee r),
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

each checkable on eight rows. The mathematically notable feature is that **both** hold. In a ring, $\times$ distributes over $+$ and not conversely; in $\mathcal{P}(X)$ the two operations are on equal footing, and neither is the "additive" one.

## Proof of (D2)

(D1) is the routine one; (D2)'s reverse inclusion contains the only step worth slowing down for.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in (S\cup T) \cap (S \cup R)$ gives two disjunctions simultaneously.</div>
  <div slot="want">$x \in S$ or $x \in T \cap R$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Nothing in the hypothesis suggests a case split, so split on excluded middle (§1.3): either $x \in S$ or not. If not, disjunctive syllogism — from *p or q* and *not p*, infer *q* — extracts $T$ from the first disjunction and $R$ from the second.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
($\subseteq$) Let $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$.

*Case $x \in S$:* then $x \in S \cup T$ and $x \in S \cup R$, hence $x$ lies in their intersection.
*Case $x \in T \cap R$:* then $x \in T \subseteq S \cup T$ and $x \in R \subseteq S \cup R$, same conclusion.

($\supseteq$) Let $x \in (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R)$, so $x \in S \cup T$ and $x \in S \cup R$. Either $x \in S$ or $x \notin S$.

*Case $x \in S$:* then $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$ at once.
*Case $x \notin S$:* from $x \in S \cup T$ and $x \notin S$, disjunctive syllogism gives $x \in T$; likewise $x \in R$. Hence $x \in T \cap R$, so $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$.

Both inclusions hold.
</Proof>

Two techniques are worth extracting. **Splitting on excluded middle** ($x \in S$ or not) is always available when the hypothesis offers no natural case division, and it is the standard opening for reverse inclusions of this shape. **Disjunctive syllogism** — from $p \vee q$ and $\neg p$ infer $q$ — is how a union hypothesis is converted into usable information once one side is excluded.

## Duality makes one proof do

Complementation is an involution exchanging $\cap$ and $\cup$ (§2.7). Complementing (D1) and applying De Morgan throughout:
$$
\overline{S \cap (T \cup R)} = \overline S \cup (\overline T \cap \overline R),
\qquad
\overline{(S\cap T) \cup (S \cap R)} = (\overline S \cup \overline T) \cap (\overline S \cup \overline R),
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

so (D1) applied to arbitrary $S,T,R$ yields (D2) for $\overline S, \overline T, \overline R$ — and since complementation is a bijection on $\mathcal{P}(X)$, that is (D2) in full generality. Proving one law proves both.

This is worth internalising as a habit: in any structure with an order-reversing involution, half the theorems are free.

## What distributivity is doing

The two laws hold together or not at all — each implies the other. And they are worth proving carefully rather than assuming, because the most important system you will meet next term **fails** them.

The subspaces of a vector space have their own versions of $\cap$ and $\cup$: intersection $U \cap W$, and the **sum** $U + W$, the smallest subspace containing both. (The union of two subspaces is usually not a subspace, which is why the sum has to be invented.) Take three distinct lines $A, B, C$ through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^2$. Then
$$
A \cap (B + C) = A \cap \mathbb{R}^2 = A,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

but
$$
(A \cap B) + (A \cap C) = \{0\} + \{0\} = \{0\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

So $A \cap (B+C) = A$ while $(A\cap B) + (A\cap C) = \{0\}$. **Distributivity fails, and fails badly** — the two sides are as far apart as they could be.

This is the practical warning to carry into your later matrix work. Subspaces look like sets and behave like sets under $\cap$; they do **not** behave like sets under $+$, and an argument that quietly assumes the distributive law for subspaces is wrong. The counterexample above is two lines long and worth remembering.

<Warning title="The ring analogy has limits">
$\cap \leftrightarrow \times$, $\cup \leftrightarrow +$ is a useful mnemonic for (D1) and a false friend elsewhere. Set operations are **idempotent** — repeating one changes nothing ($S \cup S = S$) and **have no inverses** ($S \cup T = S \cup R$ does not force $T = R$), so $(\mathcal{P}(X), \cup)$ has no inverses and $\mathcal{P}(X)$ is not a ring under these operations.

There *is* a repair: swapping $\cup$ for symmetric difference (in-one-but-not-both) restores the missing inverses, since every set is its own inverse under it. The resulting structure does obey the ring laws, and it is the version algebraists reach for.
</Warning>

## More than two sets

Both laws extend to any number of sets:
$$
S \cap (A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) = (S \cap A_1) \cup \cdots \cup (S \cap A_n),
$$
$$
S \cup (A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) = (S \cup A_1) \cap \cdots \cap (S \cup A_n).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

The proofs are the same element chases, with the case split now running over $n$ possibilities instead of two. Nothing about the argument changes; only the bookkeeping grows.

<Aside>
(D1) and (D2) are equivalent, and the duality argument above is the quickest route — but it leans on complements. A system with $\cap$ and $\cup$ but no complement still has the equivalence, by a short direct computation instead.

That matters because the subspace example has no complements either: there is no single subspace playing the role of "everything not in $U$". Which is a second reason its failure of distributivity cannot be diagnosed by the methods of this chapter.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
**In calculus the distributive law is used and rarely named.** Differentiation and integration are linear — $\frac{d}{dx}(af+bg) = af'+bg'$ — and that is a distributive law over sums. It holds for finite sums always, and for endless ones only under conditions — precisely the finite/infinite asymmetry §2.4 flagged. Doing it to an endless sum is legitimate in the right circumstances and needs a theorem to say when.

**The general lesson closes the chapter.** An identity proved for $\cap$ and $\cup$ is a theorem about those operations, not a law of thought. When a new pair of operations appears, the identity must be re-proved or refuted — and refuting takes one small example, as it did here.

</WhereThisGoes>
