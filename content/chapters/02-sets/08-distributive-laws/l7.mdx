$$
S \cap (T \cup R) = (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R), \tag{D1}
$$

*So the line reads: being in $S$ and in at least one of $T$, $R$ is the same as being in both $S$ and $T$, or in both $S$ and $R$.*

$$
S \cup (T \cap R) = (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R). \tag{D2}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: each operation spreads across the other, which arithmetic manages in only one direction.*

Both are transcriptions of logical equivalences,
$$
p \wedge (q \vee r) \equiv (p \wedge q) \vee (p \wedge r),
\qquad
p \vee (q \wedge r) \equiv (p \vee q) \wedge (p \vee r),
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

each checkable on eight rows. The mathematically notable feature is that **both** hold. In arithmetic, multiplication spreads across addition and not the other way round; here the two operations are on equal footing, and neither one is playing the part of addition.

## Proof of (D2)

(D1) is the routine one; (D2)'s reverse inclusion contains the only step worth slowing down for.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in (S\cup T) \cap (S \cup R)$ gives two disjunctions simultaneously.</div>
  <div slot="want">$x \in S$ or $x \in T \cap R$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Nothing in the hypothesis suggests a case split, so split on excluded middle (§1.3): either $x \in S$ or not. If not, then from *p or q* together with *not p* we may infer *q*, which extracts $T$ from the first disjunction and $R$ from the second.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
($\subseteq$) Let $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$.

*Case $x \in S$:* then $x \in S \cup T$ and $x \in S \cup R$, so $x$ lies in their intersection.
*Case $x \in T \cap R$:* then $x \in T \subseteq S \cup T$ and $x \in R \subseteq S \cup R$, same conclusion.

($\supseteq$) Let $x \in (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R)$, so $x \in S \cup T$ and $x \in S \cup R$. Either $x \in S$ or $x \notin S$.

*Case $x \in S$:* then $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$ at once.
*Case $x \notin S$:* from $x \in S \cup T$ and $x \notin S$, the *or* leaves only one option, so $x \in T$; likewise $x \in R$. Then $x \in T \cap R$, so $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$.

Both inclusions hold.
</Proof>

Two techniques are worth extracting. **Splitting on excluded middle** — every statement is either true or false (§1.2), so either $x \in S$ or it is not — is always available when the hypothesis offers no natural case division, and it is the standard opening for reverse inclusions of this shape. **Ruling out one half of an *or*** — from $p \vee q$ and $\neg p$, infer $q$ — is how a union hypothesis is converted into usable information once one side is excluded.

## The mirror makes one proof do

Complementation undoes itself and exchanges $\cap$ and $\cup$ (§2.7). Complementing (D1) and applying De Morgan throughout:
$$
\overline{S \cap (T \cup R)} = \overline S \cup (\overline T \cap \overline R),
\qquad
\overline{(S\cap T) \cup (S \cap R)} = (\overline S \cup \overline T) \cap (\overline S \cup \overline R),
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

so (D1) applied to arbitrary $S,T,R$ gives (D2) for $\overline S, \overline T, \overline R$ — and since complementation is a bijection (§3.8: a perfect one-to-one matching) on $\mathcal{P}(X)$, that is (D2) in full generality. Proving one law proves both.

This is worth internalizing as a habit: wherever an operation undoes itself and turns the ordering around, half the theorems are free.

## What distributivity is doing

The two laws hold together or not at all — each implies the other. And they are worth proving carefully rather than assuming, because the most important system you will meet next term **fails** them.

The same pair of laws turns up wherever two ways of combining things sit side by side — in logic as *and* and *or*, in sets as overlap and pooling, and in several structures you will meet later. Recognizing the pattern once means recognizing it every time.

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

but
$$
(A \cap B) + (A \cap C) = \{0\} + \{0\} = \{0\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

So $A \cap (B+C) = A$ while $(A\cap B) + (A\cap C) = \{0\}$. **Distributivity fails, and fails badly** — the two sides are as far apart as they could be.

The practical warning is general: when a new kind of object arrives with operations that *look* like $\cap$ and $\cup$, you have to check the distributive laws rather than assume them. Plenty of systems satisfy one and fail the other. The counterexample above is two lines long and worth remembering.

<Warning title="The arithmetic analogy has limits">
$\cap \leftrightarrow \times$, $\cup \leftrightarrow +$ is a useful mnemonic for (D1) and a false friend elsewhere. Set operations **repeat harmlessly** — repeating one changes nothing ($S \cup S = S$) and **have no inverses** ($S \cup T = S \cup R$ does not force $T = R$), so pooling has no undo, and the analogy with arithmetic stops here.

There *is* a repair: swapping $\cup$ for symmetric difference (in-one-but-not-both) restores the missing inverses, since every set is its own inverse under it. The resulting structure does obey the arithmetic laws, and it is the version algebraists reach for.
</Warning>

## More than two sets

Both laws extend to any number of sets:
$$
S \cap (A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n) = (S \cap A_1) \cup \cdots \cup (S \cap A_n),
$$

*So the line reads: the same swap works however many sets you spread across, not just two.*

$$
S \cup (A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n) = (S \cup A_1) \cap \cdots \cap (S \cup A_n).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

The proofs are the same element chases, with the case split now running over $n$ possibilities instead of two. Nothing about the argument changes; only the bookkeeping grows.

<Aside>
(D1) and (D2) are equivalent, and the mirror argument above is the quickest route — but it leans on complements. A system with $\cap$ and $\cup$ but no complement still has the equivalence, by a short direct computation instead.

That matters because the failing example above has no complements either — there is nothing playing the role of "everything not in $U$" — which is a second reason its failure cannot be diagnosed by the methods of this chapter.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
**In calculus the distributive law is used and rarely named.** Differentiation — the operation that measures how steeply a graph is rising — and integration — adding up thin strips to get a total — are linear — $\frac{d}{dx}(af+bg) = af'+bg'$ — and that is a distributive law over sums. It holds for finite sums always, and for endless ones only under conditions — exactly the finite/infinite asymmetry §2.4 flagged. Doing it to an endless sum is legitimate in the right circumstances and needs a theorem to say when.

**The general lesson closes the chapter.** An identity proved for $\cap$ and $\cup$ is a theorem about those operations, not a law of thought. When a new pair of operations appears, the identity must be re-proved or refuted — and refuting takes one small example, as it did here.

</WhereThisGoes>
