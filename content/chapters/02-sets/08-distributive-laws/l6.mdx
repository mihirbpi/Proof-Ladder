$$
S \cap (T \cup V) = (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap V)
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: meeting one collection against a pooled pair is the same as meeting it against each and then pooling — and the same works with the two operations swapped.*

$$
S \cup (T \cap V) = (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup V)
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

Two laws, and the second one has no arithmetic analogue. That asymmetry is what makes this module worth a page.

## Compare with arithmetic

In arithmetic, multiplication distributes over addition:
$$a(b+c) = ab + ac.$$

Addition does **not** distribute over multiplication:
$$a + bc \neq (a+b)(a+c)$$
— take $a=1, b=1, c=1$, giving $2$ on the left and $4$ on the right.

**For sets, both directions hold.** $\cap$ and $\cup$ are symmetric partners in a way that $\times$ and $+$ are not, and every identity in this chapter comes in a matched pair as a result: two De Morgan laws, two absorption laws, two distributive laws.

You can already predict the reason. $\cap$ is $\wedge$ and $\cup$ is $\vee$, and the truth tables of $\wedge$ and $\vee$ are mirror images — swap every T for an F and the two tables trade places. Nothing similar relates $\times$ and $+$.

## Proof of the first law

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in S\cap(T\cup V)$ means $x \in S$ and ($x\in T$ or $x \in V$).</div>
  <div slot="want">Both containments.</div>
  <div slot="how">Each direction has a hypothesis containing a $\cup$, so expect cases in both — unlike the last two modules, where only one half branched. The forward direction distributes the $\wedge$ over the $\vee$ by hand; the reverse collects.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cap (T \cup V)$. Then $x \in S$, and $x \in T$ or $x \in V$.

*Case 1: $x \in T$.* With $x \in S$, this gives $x \in S \cap T$, hence $x \in (S\cap T)\cup(S\cap V)$.

*Case 2: $x \in V$.* With $x \in S$, this gives $x \in S\cap V$, hence again $x \in (S\cap T)\cup(S\cap V)$.

The cases are exhaustive, so the containment holds.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in (S\cap T)\cup(S\cap V)$. Then $x \in S \cap T$ or $x \in S \cap V$.

*Case 1: $x \in S\cap T$.* Then $x \in S$, and $x \in T$ so $x \in T \cup V$.

*Case 2: $x \in S \cap V$.* Then $x \in S$, and $x \in V$ so $x \in T \cup V$.

In both cases $x \in S$ and $x \in T \cup V$, so $x \in S\cap(T\cup V)$.

Therefore the two sets are equal.
</Proof>

**Both halves branched**, and both branches did the same work with the letters exchanged. That repetition is a signal: whenever two cases differ only by a renaming, published proofs write *"the case $x \in V$ is identical with $T$ and $V$ interchanged."* Doing that is not laziness, provided the symmetry is genuine — but you must check that it is.

<Warning title="Parentheses are load-bearing">
$$S \cap T \cup V$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

is meaningless. Unlike $\times$ and $+$, the operations $\cap$ and $\cup$ have **no** precedence convention, and

$$(S\cap T)\cup V \quad\text{and}\quad S\cap(T\cup V)$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

*These two differ: the brackets decide which operation happens first, exactly as in arithmetic.*

are genuinely different sets. Take $S = \emptyset$ and $V$ non-empty: the first is $V$, the second is $\emptyset$.

Arithmetic lets you drop parentheses in $ab+c$ because $\times$ binds tighter than $+$. Nothing here does. Write them.
</Warning>

## The second law

$S \cup (T \cap V) = (S\cup T)\cap(S \cup V)$ can be proved directly, but the shortcut from module 2.7 is better.

Complement the first law and apply De Morgan repeatedly:
$$
\overline{S \cap (T\cup V)} = \overline S \cup \overline{T \cup V} = \overline S \cup (\overline T \cap \overline V),
$$
$$
\overline{(S\cap T)\cup(S\cap V)} = \overline{S\cap T} \cap \overline{S \cap V} = (\overline S \cup \overline T)\cap(\overline S \cup \overline V).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

The two left sides are equal by the first law, so the right sides are too — which is the second law, written in $\overline S, \overline T, \overline V$. Since every set is the complement of *something*, that establishes it in general.

**This is the mirroring doing real work.** Complementation exchanges $\cap$ and $\cup$, so it converts any true identity into another true identity. Every law in this chapter has a dual partner, and half the proofs are therefore free.

## Where it shows up

**Loci.** Let $S$ be the interior of a circle, $T$ a half-plane, $V$ another half-plane. Then $S \cap (T \cup V)$ is the part of the disk lying in at least one half-plane, and the law says you may equally take the two pieces separately and combine them. Constructions use this whenever a region is described in two ways.

**Probability.** With events instead of sets, the same identity is the reason
$$P\big(A \cap (B \cup C)\big)$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

can be computed by splitting into $A\cap B$ and $A \cap C$ and correcting for the overlap. The inclusion–exclusion rule is distributivity plus counting.

**Number theory.** For divisor sets, $\cap$ is gcd and $\cup$ is lcm, and the distributive laws become
$$\gcd(a, \operatorname{lcm}(b,c)) = \operatorname{lcm}(\gcd(a,b), \gcd(a,c)).$$
That identity looks arbitrary in a number theory book. Here it is the same law you just proved.

<Aside>
Two operations obeying commutativity, associativity, absorption and both distributive laws form a pattern that recurs all over mathematics, and adding complements gives the version that describes both sets and logic.

Three systems you have met obey exactly this pattern: the statements of Chapter 1 under *and*, *or* and *not*; the subsets of a fixed set under overlap, pooling and outside; and the two values $\{0,1\}$ under AND, OR and NOT that every digital circuit is built from. One set of laws, three subjects — which is why a fact proved in any one of them transfers to the other two.

Because they satisfy the same laws, a theorem proved once holds in all three. The identity you proved above about circles and half-planes is also a theorem about logic gates.
</Aside>

## Reading the structure first

By this point in the chapter you can predict a proof's shape before starting:

- **$\cup$ in the hypothesis** — cases, because you are not told which side.
- **$\cup$ in the goal** — easy, land in either part.
- **$\cap$ in the hypothesis** — easy, you get both facts.
- **$\cap$ in the goal** — two things to establish.
- **complement in the hypothesis** — likely "suppose not."

Reading the statement this way and sketching the skeleton before writing is how experienced mathematicians work, and it is a habit that transfers directly to the two-column proofs in front of you. The reason column is where the *tools* go; this list is about the *shape*, and the shape comes first.
