Arithmetic has one distributive law: $3(4+5) = 3\cdot4 + 3\cdot5$. Multiplication spreads across addition.

Sets have **two**, and the second one has no arithmetic version at all.

## The two laws

$$
S \cap (T \cup R) = (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R) \tag{D1}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "the overlap of", $\cup$ is "the pool of". So the line reads: **overlapping $S$ with the pool of $T$ and $R$ gives the same collection as overlapping $S$ with each of them separately and then pooling the two results.***

$$
S \cup (T \cap R) = (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R) \tag{D2}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: same two marks. So the line reads: **pooling $S$ with the overlap of $T$ and $R$ gives the same collection as pooling $S$ with each of them separately and then taking the overlap of those two results.***

(D1) is the familiar shape, with overlap playing the part of $\times$ and pool the part of $+$.

(D2) is the one to stare at. Its arithmetic imitation is simply false: $1 + (2\cdot3) = 7$, while $(1+2)(1+3) = 12$. In sets, **both** operations spread across the other, and neither is the outer one.

Both laws come straight from module 1.3: *and* spreads across *or*, and *or* spreads across *and*. Each can be checked with an eight-row truth table.

## Proof of (D1)

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Being in the left-hand collection means being in $S$, and in at least one of $T$ and $R$. Being in the right-hand one means being in both $S$ and $T$, or else in both $S$ and $R$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Each collection sits inside the other.</div>
  <div slot="how">Both halves hand you an *or*, so both need a case split (module 1.12). Going forward, the split is $T$ against $R$; coming back, it is which of the two overlaps you landed in.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**First half.** Take anything in the left-hand collection. It is in $S$, and it is in $T$ or in $R$. Split on that *or*.

*Case 1: it is in $T$.* Being in $S$ as well, it is in the overlap of $S$ and $T$.

*Case 2: it is in $R$.* Being in $S$ as well, it is in the overlap of $S$ and $R$.

Either way it is in one of the two overlaps, and so in their pool — the right-hand collection. The cases cover everything.

**Second half.** Take anything in the right-hand collection: it is in the overlap of $S$ and $T$, or in the overlap of $S$ and $R$. Split on that *or*.

In *Case 1* it is in $S$ and in $T$, and being in $T$ puts it in the pool of $T$ and $R$. In *Case 2* it is in $S$ and in $R$, and being in $R$ does the same. Either way it is in $S$ and in that pool — the left-hand collection.

Each collection sits inside the other, so (D1) holds.
</Proof>

## Proof of (D2)

The second half of this one contains the only genuinely surprising step in the module.

<Proof>
**First half.** Take anything in the left-hand collection: it is in $S$, or in the overlap of $T$ and $R$.

If it is in $S$, then it is in both pools on the right, and we are done. If instead it is in both $T$ and $R$, then being in $T$ puts it in the first pool and being in $R$ puts it in the second. Either way it is in the overlap of the two pools.

**Second half.** Now take anything in the right-hand collection. It is in the pool of $S$ and $T$, **and** in the pool of $S$ and $R$. We need: it is in $S$, or it is in both $T$ and $R$.

Ask one question — is it in $S$?

*Case 1: yes.* Then it is certainly in the left-hand collection.

*Case 2: no.* It is in the pool of $S$ and $T$ and not in $S$, so it has to be in $T$. It is in the pool of $S$ and $R$ and not in $S$, so it has to be in $R$. So it is in both, which is the other thing we wanted.

Each collection sits inside the other, so (D2) holds.
</Proof>

Two moves in Case 2 are worth naming, because you will use both for the rest of the course.

**Ruling out one half of an *or*.** You knew "in $S$ or in $T$" and you knew "not in $S$", so $T$ was forced. That is how you get value out of an *or* you have been handed.

**Splitting on a question nothing asked you to ask.** The case split was *is it in $S$, or not?* — and nothing in the hypothesis suggested it. That split is always available, for any statement at all, and it is often the right move when nothing else presents itself.

## The two laws are mirror images

Swap every overlap with a pool in (D1) and out comes (D2). Module 2.7 explains why that is no accident: taking outsides exchanges the two operations, so the outside of (D1) *is* (D2), with each set replaced by its outside.

One proof, two theorems — the first time in this course that a result has come free.

<Warning title="Arithmetic intuition is only half right">
Treating overlap as $\times$ and pool as $+$ will carry you through many manipulations. It breaks in three places worth remembering:

- pooling a collection with itself changes nothing, while $a + a \neq a$;
- (D2) holds for sets and fails for numbers;
- there is nothing playing the part of subtraction — knowing the pool of $S$ and $T$ equals the pool of $S$ and $R$ does **not** make $T$ and $R$ equal.

Check identities about sets by following an element through, not by analogy with numbers.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Commutativity, associativity, De Morgan, the complement laws, and these two — that list is complete. Every true identity about pooling, overlapping and outsides follows from it, which is why following an element through always works, and why you never need a new trick.

The distributive laws are the fragile ones. Systems obeying every other law on the list while failing (D1) do exist, and they get studied closely, because that single failure changes everything downstream.
</Aside>
