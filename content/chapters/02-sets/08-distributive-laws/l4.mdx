$$
S \cap (T \cup R) = (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R) \tag{D1}
$$
$$
S \cup (T \cap R) = (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R) \tag{D2}
$$

(D1) is the familiar shape, with $\cap$ where $\times$ goes and $\cup$ where $+$ goes. (D2) has no arithmetic counterpart: $a + bc \neq (a+b)(a+c)$ in general. In sets, **both** operations distribute over the other, and neither is subordinate.

Both laws are transcriptions of logical equivalences (module 1.3):
$$
p \wedge (q \vee r) \equiv (p \wedge q) \vee (p \wedge r),
\qquad
p \vee (q \wedge r) \equiv (p \vee q) \wedge (p \vee r),
$$
each verifiable by an eight-row truth table.

## Proof of (D1)

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in S \cap (T \cup R) \iff (x \in S) \wedge \big((x \in T) \vee (x \in R)\big)$, and $x \in (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R) \iff \big((x\in S)\wedge(x\in T)\big) \vee \big((x\in S)\wedge(x\in R)\big)$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion.</div>
  <div slot="how">Both directions carry a disjunction in the hypothesis, so both need a case split (module 1.12). The forward split is on $T$ versus $R$; the backward split is on which of the two intersections $x$ lies in.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cap (T \cup R)$ be arbitrary. Then $x \in S$, and $x \in T$ or $x \in R$. Split on that disjunction.

*Case 1: $x \in T$.* With $x \in S$, $x \in S \cap T$.

*Case 2: $x \in R$.* With $x \in S$, $x \in S \cap R$.

Either way $x \in (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R)$, and the cases are exhaustive.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R)$ be arbitrary, and split on the outer disjunction. In *Case 1*, $x \in S \cap T$, so $x\in S$ and $x \in T \subseteq T \cup R$. In *Case 2*, $x \in S \cap R$, so $x \in S$ and $x \in R \subseteq T\cup R$. Both give $x \in S \cap (T \cup R)$.

Both inclusions hold, so (D1) is proved.
</Proof>

## Proof of (D2)

The reverse inclusion here contains the one genuinely interesting step in the module.

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$; split. If $x \in S$, then $x$ lies in both $S \cup T$ and $S \cup R$. If $x \in T \cap R$, then $x \in T$ puts it in $S \cup T$ and $x \in R$ puts it in $S \cup R$. Either way $x \in (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R)$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R)$, so $x \in S \cup T$ **and** $x \in S \cup R$. We must show $x \in S$ or $x \in T \cap R$. Split on whether $x \in S$.

*Case 1: $x \in S$.* Then immediately $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$.

*Case 2: $x \notin S$.* From $x \in S \cup T$ and $x \notin S$ we get $x \in T$. From $x \in S \cup R$ and $x \notin S$ we get $x \in R$. Hence $x \in T \cap R$, so $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$.

Both inclusions hold, so (D2) is proved.
</Proof>

The move in Case 2 — *from $p \vee q$ and $\neg p$, conclude $q$* — is the standard way to get value out of a union hypothesis when one side can be ruled out.

Note the split itself: $x \in S$ versus $x \notin S$, licensed by $p \vee \neg p$ rather than by anything in the hypothesis. That split is always available, and it is often the right move when nothing else presents itself.

## The two laws are duals

Swapping $\cap \leftrightarrow \cup$ turns (D1) into (D2), and module 2.7 explains why that is no coincidence: complementing exchanges $\cap$ and $\cup$, so complementing both sides of (D1) and applying De Morgan yields (D2). Concretely:
$$
\overline{S \cap (T \cup R)} = \overline S \cup \overline{(T \cup R)} = \overline S \cup (\overline T \cap \overline R),
$$
and doing the same to the right-hand side of (D1) gives $(\overline S \cup \overline T) \cap (\overline S \cup \overline R)$. So (D1) for $S,T,R$ *is* (D2) for $\overline S, \overline T, \overline R$. One proof, two theorems.

<Warning title="Arithmetic intuition is only half right">
$\cap$ behaves like $\times$ and $\cup$ like $+$ in (D1), and that analogy will get you through many manipulations. It breaks in three places worth remembering:

- $S \cup S = S$, but $a + a \neq a$;
- (D2) holds for sets but not for numbers;
- there is no subtraction: $S \cup T = S \cup R$ does not imply $T = R$.

Verify set identities by element chasing (§2.3), not by analogy.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Commutativity, associativity, De Morgan, the complement laws, and these two — that list is complete. Every true identity about $\cup$, $\cap$ and complement follows from it, which is why element chasing always works and why you never need a new trick.

The distributive laws are the fragile ones. Systems that satisfy every other law on the list but fail (D1) do exist, and they are studied precisely because that one failure changes everything downstream.
</Aside>
