$$
S \cap (T \cup R) = (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R) \tag{D1}
$$

*In words: meeting $S$ against "$T$ or $R$" gives the same collection as meeting it against each and pooling the results.*
$$
S \cup (T \cap R) = (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R) \tag{D2}
$$

D1 is the familiar shape. Put $\cap$ where $\times$ goes and $\cup$ where $+$ goes and you have $a(b+c) = ab + ac$, the law you have used since middle school.

D2 has **no arithmetic counterpart.** In numbers,
$$
a + bc \neq (a+b)(a+c)
$$
in general — try $a=1, b=1, c=1$: the left is $2$, the right is $4$.

So sets do something arithmetic cannot. **Both operations distribute over each other, and neither is subordinate to the other.** That symmetry is the real content of this module.

Underneath, as always, are the logic laws of module 1.3:
$$
p \wedge (q \vee r) \equiv (p \wedge q) \vee (p \wedge r), \qquad
p \vee (q \wedge r) \equiv (p \vee q) \wedge (p \vee r).
$$

## Proof of D1

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in S \cap (T \cup R)$ unfolds to: $x \in S$, and ($x \in T$ or $x \in R$).<br />$x \in (S\cap T) \cup (S \cap R)$ unfolds to: ($x \in S$ and $x \in T$) or ($x \in S$ and $x \in R$).</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion.</div>
  <div slot="how">Both directions carry a disjunction in the hypothesis, so <strong>both</strong> need a case split. Forward, split on $T$ versus $R$; backward, split on which intersection $x$ came from.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cap (T \cup R)$ be arbitrary. Then $x \in S$, and $x \in T$ or $x \in R$. Split on that disjunction.

*Case 1: $x \in T$.* Together with $x \in S$, this gives $x \in S \cap T$.

*Case 2: $x \in R$.* Together with $x \in S$, this gives $x \in S \cap R$.

In either case $x$ lies in one of the two intersections, hence in their union. The cases are exhaustive.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R)$ be arbitrary, and split on the outer disjunction.

*Case 1: $x \in S \cap T$.* Then $x \in S$ and $x \in T$, so $x \in T \cup R$, so $x \in S \cap (T \cup R)$.

*Case 2: $x \in S \cap R$.* Then $x \in S$ and $x \in R$, so $x \in T \cup R$, and again $x \in S \cap (T \cup R)$.

Both inclusions hold, so D1 is proved.
</Proof>

Notice that $x \in S$ was available in **every** case of both directions — it sits outside the disjunction, so the split never touches it. Recognising which parts of a hypothesis survive a case split is most of what makes these proofs routine.

## Proof of D2

The backward direction here contains the one genuinely interesting step in the module.

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$; split. If $x \in S$, then $x$ lies in both $S \cup T$ and $S \cup R$. If $x \in T \cap R$, then $x \in T$ puts it in $S \cup T$ and $x \in R$ puts it in $S \cup R$. Either way $x \in (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R)$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R)$, so $x \in S \cup T$ **and** $x \in S \cup R$. We must reach $x \in S$ or $x \in T \cap R$. Split on whether $x \in S$.

*Case 1: $x \in S$.* Then immediately $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$.

*Case 2: $x \notin S$.* From $x \in S \cup T$ and $x \notin S$ we get $x \in T$. From $x \in S \cup R$ and $x \notin S$ we get $x \in R$. So $x \in T \cap R$, and again $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$.

Both inclusions hold, so D2 is proved.
</Proof>

Two things happened in Case 2 that are worth naming.

**From *$p$ or $q$* together with *not $p$*, conclude $q$.** That is how you extract value from a union hypothesis when one side can be ruled out, and it is the step the whole proof turns on.

**The split was on $x \in S$ versus $x \notin S$** — not on anything in the hypothesis. That split is *always* available, by module 1.2's guarantee that exactly one holds, and reaching for it is often the right move when the hypothesis offers nothing to split on.

<Warning title="Arithmetic intuition is only half right">
The analogy $\cap \leftrightarrow \times$, $\cup \leftrightarrow +$ will carry you through many manipulations. It breaks in three places:

- $S \cup S = S$, but $a + a \neq a$;
- D2 holds for sets and fails for numbers;
- there is no subtraction: from $S \cup T = S \cup R$ you cannot conclude $T = R$.

Verify set identities by chasing an arbitrary element through the definitions, never by analogy with algebra.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Here is the practical version. Solving a compound inequality is applying these laws.

$$
|x| < 3 \ \wedge\ (x > 1 \ \vee\ x < -2)
$$

distributes into $(-3 < x < 3 \wedge x > 1)$ or $(-3 < x < 3 \wedge x < -2)$, which is $(1,3) \cup (-3,-2)$.

You have been doing this since Algebra I, splitting a compound condition into cases and reassembling the answer. D1 is the theorem that says the reassembly is valid — that breaking into cases and unioning the results gives the same set as the original condition, with nothing gained and nothing lost.
</Aside>
