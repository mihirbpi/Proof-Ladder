You already know one distributive law, from arithmetic:
$$
3 \times (4 + 5) = (3 \times 4) + (3 \times 5).
$$
Multiplication *spreads across* addition. Sets have the same kind of law — and then something extra that arithmetic cannot match.

Picture three hoops on the floor, called $S$, $T$ and $R$, with things scattered around and inside them.

## The two laws

**First law.** The overlap of $S$ with (everything pooled from $T$ and $R$) is the same collection as (the overlap of $S$ and $T$) pooled with (the overlap of $S$ and $R$).

That is the arithmetic law with *overlap* playing the part of $\times$ and *pool* playing the part of $+$.

**Second law.** Pooling $S$ with (the overlap of $T$ and $R$) is the same collection as (the pool of $S$ and $T$) overlapped with (the pool of $S$ and $R$).

**Read that one again.** It says pooling spreads across overlapping as well. **There is no arithmetic version of it.** Adding first and then multiplying is not the same as multiplying first and then adding: $1 + (2\times3)$ is $7$, while $(1+2)\times(1+3)$ is $12$.

So sets are more evenhanded than numbers. Neither operation is the outer one.

## Proving the first law

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">A thing in the left-hand collection is in $S$, and also in $T$ or in $R$.</div>
  <div slot="want">That same thing is in the overlap of $S$ and $T$, or in the overlap of $S$ and $R$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Check both boxes — two halves, as always for set equality. Going forward, the word *or* appears in what you know, so split into cases.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**First half.** Take a thing in the left-hand collection. It is in $S$, and it is in the pool of $T$ and $R$ — which means it is in $T$, or in $R$. That is an *or*, so there are two cases.

*Case 1: it is in $T$.* It is also in $S$, so it is in the overlap of $S$ and $T$ — and therefore in the pool on the right.

*Case 2: it is in $R$.* It is also in $S$, so it is in the overlap of $S$ and $R$ — and again in the pool on the right.

Both cases land in the right-hand collection, and there are no other cases.

**Second half.** Now take a thing in the right-hand collection. It is in the overlap of $S$ and $T$, or in the overlap of $S$ and $R$. Another *or*, so cases again.

*Case 1: it is in both $S$ and $T$.* Being in $T$, it is certainly in the pool of $T$ and $R$. And it is in $S$. So it is in the left-hand collection.

*Case 2: it is in both $S$ and $R$.* The same sentence with $R$ in place of $T$.

Each collection sits inside the other, so they are equal.
</Proof>

Notice that **both** halves needed a case split, and both splits came from the same place: a pool in what you were given. Whenever you follow a thing out of a pooled collection, expect cases.

## The second law

The proof has the same shape, but one step in it surprises people, so it is worth walking through.

Going forward you take a thing in the pool of $S$ with the overlap of $T$ and $R$, and split. If it is in $S$, it is in both pools on the right, and you are done. If instead it is in both $T$ and $R$, then again it is in both pools on the right.

### The interesting direction

Coming back is where the work is. You know the thing is in the pool of $S$ and $T$, and also in the pool of $S$ and $R$. You want: it is in $S$, or it is in both $T$ and $R$.

So ask one question — **is it in $S$?**

If yes, you are done.

**If not**, then being in the pool of $S$ and $T$, it has to be in $T$. And being in the pool of $S$ and $R$, it has to be in $R$. So it is in both, which is the other thing you wanted.

That *"if not, then it must be the other one"* move is small and enormously reusable: when an *or* is handed to you and you can rule out one side, the other side is yours.

<Warning title="Do not import arithmetic habits">
It is tempting to treat overlapping as $\times$ and pooling as $+$, then "simplify" using number rules you already know.

Sometimes that works and sometimes it does not, because sets obey laws numbers do not — pooling a collection with itself changes nothing, and the second law above has no arithmetic twin — and fail laws numbers obey, since there is nothing playing the part of subtraction.

Prove things about sets by following a thing through the words, not by analogy with numbers.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The two laws are mirror images of each other: swap every *pool* with *overlap* in one and out comes the other.

That mirror is real, and module 2.7 explains where it comes from — taking outsides swaps pooling and overlapping, so every true statement about sets has a true twin. Proving one law really does prove the other, which is the first time in this course that a piece of work has come free.
</Aside>
