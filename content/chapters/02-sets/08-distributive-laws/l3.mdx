You already know one distributive law, from arithmetic:
$$
3 \times (4 + 5) = (3 \times 4) + (3 \times 5).
$$
Multiplication *spreads across* addition. Sets have the same kind of law — and then something extra that arithmetic cannot match.

## The two laws

$$
S \cap (T \cup R) = (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R)
$$
$$
S \cup (T \cap R) = (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R)
$$

The first looks exactly like the arithmetic law with $\cap$ playing $\times$ and $\cup$ playing $+$.

The second is the striking one. **Read it again.** It says $\cup$ spreads across $\cap$ as well. There is no arithmetic version of this. Adding first and then multiplying is *not* the same as multiplying first and then adding: $1 + (2 \times 3)$ is $7$, while $(1+2)\times(1+3)$ is $12$.

So sets are more symmetric than numbers. Neither operation is the "outer" one.

## Proving the first law

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in S \cap (T \cup R)$ means $x \in S$, and also $x \in T$ or $x \in R$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$x$ is in $S \cap T$, or in $S \cap R$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Check both boxes — two subset proofs. In the forward direction the hypothesis contains an *or*, so split into cases.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cap (T \cup R)$. Then $x \in S$, and $x \in T \cup R$ — that is, $x \in T$ or $x \in R$. Since that is an *or*, we split into cases.

*Case 1: $x \in T$.* Together with $x \in S$, this gives $x \in S \cap T$, so $x$ is in the union $(S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R)$.

*Case 2: $x \in R$.* Together with $x \in S$, this gives $x \in S \cap R$, so again $x$ is in the union.

Both cases land in the right place, and there are no others.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in (S \cap T) \cup (S \cap R)$. Again an *or*, so cases.

*Case 1: $x \in S \cap T$.* Then $x \in S$ and $x \in T$. Since $x \in T$, certainly $x \in T \cup R$. So $x \in S \cap (T \cup R)$.

*Case 2: $x \in S \cap R$.* Then $x \in S$ and $x \in R$, so $x \in T \cup R$, so $x \in S \cap (T \cup R)$.

Both inclusions hold, so the sets are equal.
</Proof>

Notice that **both** directions needed a case split, and both splits came from the same source: a union in the hypothesis. Whenever you chase an element out of a union, expect cases.

## The second law, in words

The proof of $S \cup (T \cap R) = (S \cup T) \cap (S \cup R)$ has the same shape, but one step is worth previewing because it surprises people.

Chasing forward, you assume $x \in S \cup (T \cap R)$ and split. In the case $x \in S$, you need $x \in S \cup T$ and $x \in S \cup R$ — both immediate. In the case $x \in T \cap R$, you get $x \in T$ and $x \in R$, so again both.

### The interesting direction

Chasing backward is the interesting direction. You know $x \in S \cup T$ and $x \in S \cup R$, and you want $x \in S$ or $x \in T \cap R$. So ask: is $x$ in $S$? If yes, done. **If not**, then from $x \in S \cup T$ it must be $T$ that holds, and from $x \in S \cup R$ it must be $R$ — so $x \in T \cap R$. Done either way.

That "if not, then it must be the other one" move is a small, extremely reusable trick: when a union hands you a disjunction and you can rule out one side, the other side is yours.

<Warning title="Do not import arithmetic habits">
It is tempting to write $\cap$ as $\times$ and $\cup$ as $+$ and "simplify" using the number rules you already know. Sometimes that works and sometimes it does not, because sets satisfy laws numbers do not ($S \cup S = S$, and the second distributive law) and fail laws numbers satisfy (there is no subtraction inverse). Prove set identities by chasing elements, not by analogy.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The two laws are mirror images: swap every $\cap$ with $\cup$ in one and you get the other. That mirror is real, and module 2.7 explains it — complementation swaps $\cap$ and $\cup$, so every true statement about sets has a true twin. Proving one law really does prove the other.
</Aside>
