Listing elements works for small sets and fails immediately for interesting ones. You cannot list the reals between $0$ and $1$, and you should not want to list the multiples of $7$.

**Set-builder notation** describes a set by a condition instead:
$$
\{\, x \in A \mid p(x) \,\}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

read *the set of $x$ in $A$ such that $p(x)$*. The bar means *such that*; some books use a colon, $\{x \in A : p(x)\}$, and it means the same thing.

Membership is then decided by Chapter 1:
$$
x \in \{\, y \in A \mid p(y)\,\} \iff x \in A \ \wedge\ p(x).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\wedge$ is "and", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

**This equivalence is the whole module.** Every proof about a set-builder set runs through it — you unfold membership into a statement, work with the statement, and fold it back up.

## Two parts, both required

The notation has a **source set** $A$ and a **condition** $p$. Dropping either causes trouble.

$$
\{\, x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x^2 = 4 \,\} = \{-2, 2\}, \qquad
\{\, x \in \mathbb{N} \mid x^2 = 4 \,\} = \{2\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers, $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: the first is "the real numbers whose square is $4$".*

Same condition, different source, different set. **The source set is part of the definition**, exactly as the domain was part of a quantified claim in module 1.10.

Where the source is obvious from context authors often drop it and write $\{x \mid p(x)\}$. That is an abbreviation, not a different construction, and when you are proving something it is worth restoring.

## Sets defined by equations

This is the notation that has been sitting under your algebra work all along. A solution set *is* a set-builder set:

$$
\{\, x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x^2 - 1 = 0 \,\} = \{-1, 1\}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

$$
\{\, x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x^2 + 1 = 0 \,\} = \emptyset
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", $\emptyset$ is the empty collection, the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

$$
\{\, x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x^2 \geq 0 \,\} = \mathbb{R}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

The middle one is worth pausing on. Solving an equation and getting no answers does not mean the notation broke — it means the set is empty, which is a perfectly good value. And the third shows the other extreme: a condition every element satisfies picks out the whole source set.

**Solving an equation is computing a set-builder set in a listed form.** The two sides of each line above are the same object described two ways, and proving such an equality is what module 2.6 is about.

## Intervals

For subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ defined by inequalities there is a compressed notation. An **interval** is written

$$
[a,b] = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid a \leq x \leq b\}, \qquad
(a,b) = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid a < x < b\},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: the square brackets include the endpoints, the round ones exclude them.*

with square brackets including the endpoint and round brackets excluding it. The two mix freely: $[0, 1)$ contains $0$ and not $1$. Infinite endpoints always take a round bracket, since $\infty$ is not a number and cannot be included: $(-\infty, 3]$.

<Warning title="(a, b) is ambiguous notation">
$(2, 5)$ might be an interval of real numbers or an ordered pair (module 2.9). Nothing in the symbols distinguishes them.

Context always does — an interval appears where a set is expected, a pair where a point is expected — but this is a genuine collision in standard notation, and it is worth naming rather than being surprised by.
</Warning>

## Building sets from a rule

The condition need not be an equation. It can describe how elements are made:

$$
\{\, 2k \mid k \in \mathbb{Z} \,\} \quad\text{is the set of even integers,}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

and here the variable before the bar carries a formula rather than standing alone. Read it as *all things of the form $2k$, as $k$ ranges over $\mathbb{Z}$*.

Both styles describe the same kinds of sets, and often the same set:
$$
\{\, n \in \mathbb{Z} \mid n \text{ is even} \,\} = \{\, 2k \mid k \in \mathbb{Z} \,\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

The left **filters** — it starts with all integers and keeps some. The right **generates** — it starts with all integers and transforms them. Proving two such descriptions equal takes an argument in both directions, and module 2.6 supplies the method.

<Aside>
A forward reference worth having now. Chapter 5 builds the complex numbers, and the definition is a set-builder set:
$$
\mathbb{C} = \{\, a + bi \mid a, b \in \mathbb{R} \,\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\mathbb{C}$ is the complex numbers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

This is the generating style: every complex number is *of the form* $a + bi$ for real $a$ and $b$. Nothing mysterious is being asserted about $i$ — the set is simply being described by the shape of its elements, which is what generating notation does.
</Aside>

<Warning title="Not every condition builds a set">
Module 2.1 required sets to be well-defined, and set-builder notation makes it tempting to forget.

$\{x \mid x \text{ is a large number}\}$ is not a set, because the condition is not one that settles membership.

Notice how the source set protects you. $\{x \in A \mid p(x)\}$ carves a piece out of a set you already have, and carving cannot go wrong. It is the source-free form $\{x \mid p(x)\}$, quantified over everything that exists, that produced Russell's paradox — which is why the axiomatic version of set theory permits only the first.
</Warning>
