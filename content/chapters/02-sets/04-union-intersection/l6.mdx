Every compass-and-straightedge construction you have done works the same way: draw two loci, and take the points they have in common. The centre of a circumscribed circle is where two perpendicular bisectors meet. That "where they meet" has a name.

## The two operations

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\cup$ reads "union", $\subseteq$ reads "is a subset of".*

$$
S \cap T = \{\,x : x \in S \ \wedge\ x \in T\,\} \qquad \textbf{intersection}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\wedge$ is "and", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

$$
S \cup T = \{\,x : x \in S \ \vee\ x \in T\,\} \qquad \textbf{union}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\vee$ is "or", $\cup$ is "pooled with".*

$\cap$ is **and**. $\cup$ is **or**. That is the entire definition, and every property in this chapter is a property of $\wedge$ and $\vee$ from Chapter 1, translated.

<Warning title="Union is inclusive">
Module 1.3 insisted that mathematical *or* includes the both case, and $\cup$ inherits that. Elements of $S \cap T$ are elements of $S \cup T$.

The shaded lens in the second figure is not a mistake, and this is why $|S \cup T| = |S| + |T| - |S\cap T|$ rather than $|S| + |T|$: adding the sizes counts the overlap twice, so it must be subtracted back off.
</Warning>

## Constructions are intersections

$$
\text{Circumcentre of } \triangle ABC = \{Q : QA = QB\} \ \cap\ \{Q : QB = QC\}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

The two sets are perpendicular bisectors. Their intersection is the point equidistant from all three vertices — which is exactly why a circle can be drawn through $A$, $B$, and $C$.

Written this way, the concurrency theorem stops being magic. A point in the first set satisfies $QA = QB$; a point in the second satisfies $QB=QC$; a point in the intersection satisfies both, hence $QA = QC$ by transitivity of equality. **The third bisector was never needed as a separate assumption** — it comes free with the $\wedge$.

Compass-and-straightedge intersections everywhere:

- **Angle bisector construction:** two arcs of equal radius intersect in a point on the bisector.
- **Perpendicular through a point:** two arcs intersect in two points; the line through them is perpendicular.
- **Incentre:** the intersection of the three angle bisectors.

Each arc is a set of points, and each construction step is a $\cap$.

## Disjoint sets

$S$ and $T$ are **disjoint** when $S \cap T = \emptyset$.

  <VennTwo labelS="S" labelT="T" disjoint={true} />
</Figure>

Parallel lines are disjoint sets of points. So are the interior and the exterior of a circle. And so is any classification that partitions: the acute, right, and obtuse triangles are pairwise disjoint, and their union is all triangles.

That last pattern — pairwise disjoint pieces whose union is everything — is a **partition**, and it is what makes proof by cases (module 1.12) legitimate. Disjointness means no double counting; union-is-everything means no gaps.

## The laws come free

Because $\cap$ is $\wedge$ and $\cup$ is $\vee$, every logical equivalence from Chapter 1 becomes a set identity, with no new work:

| Logic | Sets |
|---|---|
| $p \wedge q \equiv q \wedge p$ | $S \cap T = T \cap S$ |
| $p \vee q \equiv q \vee p$ | $S \cup T = T \cup S$ |
| $p \wedge p \equiv p$ | $S \cap S = S$ |
| $p \vee (p \wedge q) \equiv p$ | $S \cup (S \cap T) = S$ |
| associativity of $\wedge$ | $(S\cap T)\cap U = S \cap (T \cap U)$ |

Verify one and you have verified the pattern. Module 2.6 shows how to write these proofs properly; the point here is that **you already proved them in Chapter 1** and are now reading the results in a second alphabet.

## Ordering

$$
S \cap T \subseteq S \subseteq S \cup T.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: the overlap sits inside $S$, and $S$ sits inside the pooled collection.*

Intersecting can only shrink; uniting can only grow. In the classification chart:
$$\{\text{rectangles}\} \cap \{\text{rhombi}\} = \{\text{squares}\}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

A quadrilateral is a square exactly when it is both a rectangle and a rhombus — four right angles *and* four congruent sides. The chart's diamond shape, with squares at the bottom where two branches rejoin, is a picture of that intersection.

Meanwhile $\{\text{rectangles}\} \cup \{\text{rhombi}\}$ is a set nobody has a name for, which is a reasonable indication of which operation classification actually uses.

## Many sets at once

Nothing restricts $\cap$ and $\cup$ to two sets. Given a whole collection of sets, their **intersection** holds the things lying in *every* one of them, and their **union** the things lying in *at least one*:
$$
S_1 \cap S_2 \cap \cdots \cap S_n, \qquad S_1 \cup S_2 \cup \cdots \cup S_n.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

The correspondence with the quantifiers of module 1.10 is exact: an intersection is a *for all*, and a union is a *there exists*. That is module 1.11's De Morgan connection stated one more way, and module 2.7 will cash it in.

<Aside>
The pattern reaches beyond geometry.

In probability, an **event** is a subset of the sample space. "$A$ and $B$ both occur" is $A \cap B$; "at least one occurs" is $A \cup B$; **mutually exclusive** is the probability word for *disjoint*. The addition rule
$$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A\cap B)$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

is the counting formula from the warning above, divided through by the size of the sample space.

In number theory, the common divisors of $12$ and $18$ are $D_{12} \cap D_{18}$, and the greatest common divisor is the largest element of that intersection. Two subjects, one operation.
</Aside>

## Empty intersections carry information

When a construction fails, the intersection is empty — and that is a fact worth stating rather than an error.

Two circles with radii $2$ and $3$ whose centres are $9$ apart do not meet: their intersection is $\emptyset$. This is precisely why the triangle inequality appears when you construct a triangle from three given lengths. If the two arcs are disjoint, no triangle exists, and the set language lets you say so in the same sentence as the successful case.
