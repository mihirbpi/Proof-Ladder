Two sets combine in two basic ways, and the definitions are Chapter 1 wearing set notation:

$$
S \cup T = \{\, x \mid x \in S \ \vee\ x \in T \,\}, \qquad
S \cap T = \{\, x \mid x \in S \ \wedge\ x \in T \,\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

**Union is *or*, and intersection is *and*.** Every fact in this module and the next three is a logic fact from Chapter 1, translated.

Since the *or* of module 1.3 is inclusive, $S \cup T$ contains the overlap. An element in both sets is in the union once — and only once, because module 2.1 said repetition is not data.

## Systems of equations, read as sets

Your algebra course has been intersecting sets all year without saying so.

The solution set of a single equation is a set-builder set (module 2.2). A **system** asks for the values satisfying *both* equations at once — and "both" is *and*, which is intersection:

$$
\underbrace{\{(x,y) \mid y = 2x - 1\}}_{\text{a line}} \ \cap\
\underbrace{\{(x,y) \mid y = -x + 5\}}_{\text{a line}} \ =\ \{(2, 3)\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

Solving the system computes that intersection. And the three cases you learned to distinguish are three facts about it:

| System | Intersection |
|---|---|
| one solution | exactly one element |
| no solution (parallel lines) | $\emptyset$ |
| infinitely many (same line) | the whole line |

Meanwhile a *factored* equation gives a union. From $(x-2)(x+3) = 0$, a product is zero when one factor is zero **or** the other is:
$$
\{x \mid (x-2)(x+3) = 0\} = \{x \mid x = 2\} \cup \{x \mid x = -3\} = \{2, -3\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

**Systems intersect; factoring unions.** The joining word in the algebra tells you which.

## Disjoint sets

$S$ and $T$ are **disjoint** when their intersection is empty — when no element belongs to both.

$$
\{x \in \mathbb{Z} \mid x \text{ even}\} \cap \{x \in \mathbb{Z} \mid x \text{ odd}\} = \emptyset,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", $\emptyset$ is the empty collection, $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

and parallel distinct lines are disjoint as sets of points, which is one way to say what parallel means.

Disjointness matters because it licenses counting by addition: if two finite sets are disjoint, the union has as many elements as the two counts added. Overlap breaks that, and Chapter 6 returns to it.

## Two containments that always hold

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\subseteq$ reads "is a subset of".*

> **Proposition.** For any sets $S$ and $T$: the intersection sits inside $S$, and $S$ sits inside the union.

<Proof>
**$S \cap T \subseteq S$.** Let $x \in S \cap T$ be arbitrary. By definition $x \in S$ and $x \in T$; for instance $x \in S$. As $x$ was arbitrary, $S \cap T \subseteq S$.

**$S \subseteq S \cup T$.** Let $x \in S$ be arbitrary. Then "$x \in S$ or $x \in T$" is true, since its first part is. So $x \in S \cup T$, and $S \subseteq S \cup T$.
</Proof>

Both halves are one line, and each is a logic rule with sets painted on: *an **and** gives you either half*, and *either half gives you an **or***. Chasing an element through a definition is the whole technique of this chapter, and this is its smallest instance.

**Intersection only shrinks and union only grows.** Worth remembering as a sanity check: if a manipulation ever produces an intersection larger than one of its inputs, something has gone wrong.

<Warning title="Everyday or is not this or">
"Soup or salad" means one, not both. Mathematical *or* means at least one, and both is allowed — module 1.3's point, now built into $\cup$.

If you genuinely need "exactly one of the two", that set is the union take away the intersection: in one of them, but not in both. It has a name, the *symmetric difference*, and the fact that it takes a formula while $\cup$ gets a symbol tells you which one mathematics needs more often.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The dictionary between logic and sets is exact, and it is worth writing down now because Chapters 2 and 3 keep using it:

| Logic | Sets |
|---|---|
| and | intersection |
| or | union |
| not | complement (module 2.5) |
| if–then | subset |

The last row is the surprising one. *$A$ is a subset of $S$* just **is** the if–then claim *being in $A$ forces being in $S$*, made about every object at once — so containment is implication in disguise, which is why proving a containment always feels like proving an if–then.

Modules 2.7 and 2.8 use the table directly: they take laws proved about *and* and *or* in Chapter 1 and read them off as laws about intersection and union, and the proofs are the same proofs.
</Aside>
