## Definitions

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\cup$ reads "union", $\cap$ reads "intersection", $\subseteq$ reads "is a subset of".*

$$
S \cup T = \{\, x \mid x \in S \vee x \in T \,\},
\qquad
S \cap T = \{\, x \mid x \in S \wedge x \in T \,\}.
$$

The **union** is the *or* set; the **intersection** is the *and* set. Unfolding the definitions gives the two lines that every proof in this module and the next four will use:
$$
x \in S \cup T \iff (x \in S) \vee (x \in T),
\qquad
x \in S \cap T \iff (x \in S) \wedge (x \in T).
$$

Since $\vee$ is inclusive (module 1.3), $S \cup T$ contains the overlap. There is no set operation in standard use corresponding to exclusive or; the nearest is the symmetric difference $(S \cup T) \setminus (S \cap T)$, which has to be built by hand.

## The logic–set dictionary

| Logic | Sets |
|---|---|
| $\wedge$ | $\cap$ |
| $\vee$ | $\cup$ |
| $\neg$ | complement (module 2.5) |

Every identity in the rest of this chapter is an identity from Chapter 1 read through this table. De Morgan's set laws (2.7) are De Morgan's logic laws; the distributive laws for sets (2.8) are distributivity of $\wedge$ over $\vee$. Nothing new is being discovered; a translation is being performed.

## The basic containments

> **Proposition.** For any sets $S, T$: (i) $S \cap T \subseteq S$, and (ii) $S \subseteq S \cup T$.

<Proof>
(i) Let $x \in S \cap T$ be arbitrary. By definition $x \in S$ and $x \in T$; in particular $x \in S$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $S \cap T \subseteq S$.

(ii) Let $x \in S$ be arbitrary. Then the disjunction "$x \in S$ or $x \in T$" holds, since its first disjunct does. Hence $x \in S \cup T$, and $S \subseteq S \cup T$.
</Proof>

Both proofs are three lines because the underlying logical facts — $p \wedge q \Rightarrow p$ and $p \Rightarrow p \vee q$ — are tautologies. That is the dictionary at work.

Chaining these gives $S \cap T \subseteq S \subseteq S \cup T$ by transitivity (module 2.3).

## Divisors, gcd, and lcm

$$
D_{20} = \{1,2,4,5,10,20\}, \qquad D_{24} = \{1,2,3,4,6,8,12,24\},
$$
$$
D_{20} \cap D_{24} = \{1,2,4\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: the divisors of $20$, the divisors of $24$, and then the ones they share.*

The intersection is the set of **common divisors**, and its greatest element is $\gcd(20,24) = 4$. Similarly, with $M_n$ the set of positive multiples of $n$,
$$
M_{12} \cap M_{18} = \{36, 72, 108, \ldots\},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: the positive multiples that $12$ and $18$ share.*
whose least element is $\operatorname{lcm}(12,18) = 36$.

So gcd and lcm are not independent algorithms; they are the extremal elements of an intersection. This also explains why $\gcd$ and $\operatorname{lcm}$ satisfy so many matching identities: $\gcd$ takes the biggest common divisor and $\operatorname{lcm}$ the smallest common multiple, exactly as $\cap$ takes the largest set inside both and $\cup$ the smallest set containing both.

Observe too that $M_{12} \cap M_{18} = M_{36}$: the common multiples of two numbers are exactly the multiples of their lcm. That is a theorem, not a coincidence, and it is provable by double inclusion once module 2.6 supplies the method.

## Disjointness

$S$ and $T$ are **disjoint** when $S \cap T = \emptyset$.

<Figure alt="Two separate non-touching circles labelled S and T." caption="Disjoint sets.">
  <VennTwo labelS="S" labelT="T" disjoint={true} />
</Figure>

Examples: the even and odd integers; $(-\infty, 0)$ and $[0, \infty)$; the multiples of $6$ and the numbers leaving remainder $2$ on division by $6$ — a fact worth proving, since no number can leave two different remainders.

Proving disjointness is proving a universal negative: $\forall x,\, \neg(x \in S \wedge x \in T)$. Module 1.12 says that means an arbitrary-element argument, and it is often cleanest by contradiction (module 2.5).

<Warning title="Notation collisions">
$\cup$ and $\cap$ join *sets*; the connectives of module 1.3 join *statements*. Writing a connective between two sets, or a set operation between two statements, is a type error — sets are not statements and statements are not sets. Writing them correctly is not pedantry; the distinction is what the dictionary is translating between.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Both operations are commutative and associative, and doing either one twice changes nothing ($S \cup S = S$) — each property inherited from the matching property of *or* and *and*. Associativity is what licenses $S \cup T \cup R$ without brackets. Neither operation has an inverse — you cannot "un-union" — which is exactly why $(\mathcal{P}(X), \cup, \cap)$ is a lattice rather than a group.
</Aside>
