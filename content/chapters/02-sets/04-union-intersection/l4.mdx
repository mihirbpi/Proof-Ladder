## Definitions

The **union** of $S$ and $T$ is everything that is in $S$ **or** in $T$ — or in both. It is written $S \cup T$.

The **intersection** is everything that is in $S$ **and** in $T$. It is written $S \cap T$.

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "union", $\cap$ is "intersection". So $S \cup T$ reads "$S$ union $T$" and means everything in at least one of them, while $S \cap T$ reads "$S$ intersect $T$" and means only the things in both.*

The union is the *or* set; the intersection is the *and* set. Unfolding those definitions gives the two sentences that every proof in this module and the next four will lean on:

> **Being in $S \cup T$** means being in $S$, **or** being in $T$.
>
> **Being in $S \cap T$** means being in $S$, **and** being in $T$.

Since *or* is inclusive (module 1.3), the union contains the overlap. There is no standard set operation matching the exclusive *or* — the nearest thing is *the union take away the intersection*, and it has to be built by hand.

## The logic–set dictionary

| Logic | Sets |
|---|---|
| and | intersection |
| or | union |
| not | complement (module 2.5) |

Every identity in the rest of this chapter is an identity from Chapter 1 read through this table. De Morgan's set laws (2.7) are De Morgan's logic laws; the distributive laws for sets (2.8) are *and* spreading across *or*. Nothing new is being discovered — a translation is being performed.

## The basic containments

> **Proposition.** For any sets $S$ and $T$: **(i)** the intersection sits inside $S$, and **(ii)** $S$ sits inside the union.

<Proof>
**(i)** Take anything in the intersection. By definition it is in $S$ and in $T$ — so in particular, it is in $S$. Nothing was assumed about which thing we took, so the whole intersection sits inside $S$.

**(ii)** Take anything in $S$. Then the sentence "it is in $S$, or it is in $T$" is true, because its left half is. So the thing is in the union, and all of $S$ sits inside it.
</Proof>

Both proofs are three lines because the facts underneath them are the two most obvious things in logic: *an **and** gives you either half*, and *either half gives you an **or***. That is the dictionary at work.

Chaining the two gives: the intersection sits inside $S$, which sits inside the union (module 2.3).

## Divisors, gcd, and lcm

$$
D_{20} = \{1,2,4,5,10,20\}, \qquad D_{24} = \{1,2,3,4,6,8,12,24\},
$$
Their intersection is $\{1,2,4\}$ — the **common divisors** — and the biggest of those is the greatest common divisor, $4$.

Now let $M_n$ be the positive multiples of $n$. The intersection of $M_{12}$ and $M_{18}$ is $\{36, 72, 108, \ldots\}$, the **common multiples**, and the smallest of those is the least common multiple, $36$.

So gcd and lcm are not two separate procedures to memorize. Each is an intersection, with the biggest or smallest thing in it picked out — which is also why the two satisfy so many matching identities.

Observe too that the common multiples of $12$ and $18$ are exactly the multiples of $36$. That is a theorem, not a coincidence, and it is provable by double inclusion once module 2.6 supplies the method.

## Disjointness

$S$ and $T$ are **disjoint** when their intersection is empty — when they share nothing at all.

<Figure alt="Two separate non-touching circles labeled S and T." caption="Disjoint sets.">
  <VennTwo labelS="S" labelT="T" disjoint={true} />
</Figure>

Examples: the even and odd integers; $(-\infty, 0)$ and $[0, \infty)$; the multiples of $6$ and the numbers leaving remainder $2$ on division by $6$ — a fact worth proving, since no number can leave two different remainders.

Proving two sets disjoint means proving that **nothing whatever** is in both — a claim about every object at once, with a *not* in it. Module 1.12 says that calls for the any-element argument, and module 2.5's *suppose not* is usually the cleanest way to run it.

<Warning title="Notation collisions">
Union and intersection join *sets*; the joining words of module 1.3 join *statements*. Writing a joining word between two sets, or a set operation between two statements, is a type error — sets are not statements and statements are not sets. Writing them correctly is not pedantry; the distinction is what the dictionary is translating between.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Both operations ignore order, both ignore where you put the brackets, and doing either one to a set twice changes nothing — each property inherited straight from *or* and *and*. Bracket-blindness is what lets you write a union of three sets without brackets at all.

Neither operation can be undone, though. Knowing the union of $S$ and $T$ does not tell you $T$, the way knowing $x + 3$ tells you $x$. That single missing feature is what separates these operations from arithmetic ones, and it is why the rules for them had to be worked out from scratch in this chapter.
</Aside>
