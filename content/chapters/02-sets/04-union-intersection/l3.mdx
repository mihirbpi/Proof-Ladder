You can combine two sets in two natural ways, and they correspond exactly to the two words you met in module 1.3.

## Union: the *or* set

The **union** of $S$ and $T$ is the set of everything that is in $S$ **or** in $T$ — or in both.

Mathematicians write it $S \cup T$ and say "$S$ union $T$". The cup-shaped mark is the only new thing here; the idea is just *pool the two collections together*.

<Figure alt="Two overlapping circles S and T with both entirely shaded, representing the union." caption="The union: everything in at least one circle.">
  <VennTwo labelS="S" labelT="T" shade={["S-only", "T-only", "both"]} />
</Figure>

Example: pooling $\{1,2,3\}$ with $\{3,4\}$ gives $\{1,2,3,4\}$.

Notice $3$ appears once, not twice. Sets do not count repeats (module 2.1).

## Intersection: the *and* set

The **intersection** of $S$ and $T$ is the set of everything that is in $S$ **and** in $T$.

Mathematicians write it $S \cap T$ and say "$S$ intersect $T$". The idea is *keep only the overlap*.

<Figure alt="Two overlapping circles S and T with only the middle overlap shaded, representing the intersection." caption="The intersection: only the overlap.">
  <VennTwo labelS="S" labelT="T" shade={["both"]} />
</Figure>

Example: the overlap of $\{1,2,3\}$ and $\{3,4\}$ is $\{3\}$.

## The dictionary

| Word from Chapter 1 | Set operation |
|---|---|
| **or** | union — pool them |
| **and** | intersection — keep the overlap |

This is not a coincidence or a memory aid. It is the definition. The set operations were *built* from the words, so every fact you know about *and* and *or* is about to become a fact about intersections and unions.

In particular, remember that mathematical *or* is inclusive. So the union definitely includes the overlap. The union is not "one or the other but not both".

## A real payoff: divisors

Let $D_{20}$ and $D_{24}$ be the sets of positive divisors:
$$
D_{20} = \{1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20\}, \qquad D_{24} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24\}.
$$

Their intersection is the numbers dividing **both**:
$$
\text{the overlap of } D_{20} \text{ and } D_{24} = \{1, 2, 4\}.
$$

These are called the common divisors of $20$ and $24$. And look at the biggest one: $4$. That is the **greatest common divisor** of $20$ and $24$ — a thing you have probably computed before without knowing it lived inside an intersection.

The same trick works upward. Let $M_{12}$ and $M_{18}$ be the positive multiples:
$$
M_{12} = \{12, 24, 36, 48, 60, \ldots\}, \qquad M_{18} = \{18, 36, 54, 72, \ldots\}.
$$
$$
\text{the overlap of } M_{12} \text{ and } M_{18} = \{36, 72, 108, \ldots\}
$$
and the *smallest* element, $36$, is the least common multiple.

So gcd and lcm are not separate tricks. They are the extreme elements of an intersection.

## Two facts that are always true

Look at the pictures again and you will believe both of these instantly:

$$
\text{the overlap sits inside } S, \qquad\text{and}\qquad S \text{ sits inside the union.}
$$

In words: intersecting can only shrink a set, and unioning can only grow it. Both are provable by taking any one and following it — if $x$ is in both $S$ and $T$, then certainly $x$ is in $S$; if $x$ is in $S$, then certainly $x$ is in $S$ or $T$.

## Disjoint sets

If the overlap is empty — nothing at all is in both — we say $S$ and $T$ are **disjoint**.

<Figure alt="Two separate circles S and T that do not touch, representing disjoint sets." caption="Disjoint sets: the intersection is empty.">
  <VennTwo labelS="S" labelT="T" disjoint={true} />
</Figure>

The even numbers and the odd numbers are disjoint. So are the positive numbers and the negative numbers. Drawing disjoint sets as separate circles is the honest picture; drawing them overlapping and then writing "empty" in the middle is confusing.

<Warning title="Union and intersection are easy to swap">
A trick that works: the intersection mark $\cap$ looks like the letter **A** with its legs, for **A**nd. And the union mark $\cup$ looks like a cup — a cup holds *everything* you pour in.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Pooling and overlapping do not care about order, and they do not care where you put the brackets, so pooling three collections at once needs no brackets and is safe. Both facts are inherited directly from the corresponding facts about *or* and *and*.
</Aside>
