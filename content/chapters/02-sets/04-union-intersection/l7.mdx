$$
S \cup T = \{x \mid x \in S \vee x \in T\},
\qquad
S \cap T = \{x \mid x \in S \wedge x \in T\}.
$$

The definitions are transcriptions of $\vee$ and $\wedge$, and the working forms — the ones every proof in Chapter 2 unfolds to — are
$$
x \in S \cup T \iff (x \in S) \vee (x \in T),
\qquad
x \in S \cap T \iff (x \in S) \wedge (x \in T). \tag{$\ast$}
$$

Since $\vee$ is inclusive, the union contains the intersection. Venn diagrams are legitimate for building intuition and for checking whether a proposed identity is plausible; they are not proofs, because a diagram of two or three sets encodes an assumption about which regions are nonempty that a general argument may not make.

## Basic algebra

All of the following are immediate from $(\ast)$ plus the corresponding fact about the connective:

| Property | Statement | Logical source |
|---|---|---|
| Commutativity | $S \cup T = T \cup S$ | $\vee$ symmetric |
| Associativity | $(S \cup T) \cup R = S \cup (T \cup R)$ | $\vee$ associative |
| Idempotence | $S \cup S = S$ | $p \vee p \equiv p$ |
| Identity | $S \cup \emptyset = S$, $S \cap X = S$ for $S \subseteq X$ | $p \vee F \equiv p$ |
| Absorption | $S \cup (S \cap T) = S$ | $p \vee (p \wedge q) \equiv p$ |

Associativity licenses $\bigcup$ over finitely many sets without parentheses.

### Intersection is smaller, union is larger

> **Proposition.** $S \cap T \subseteq S \subseteq S \cup T$.

<Proof>
Let $x \in S \cap T$. Then $x \in S \wedge x \in T$, so in particular $x \in S$. Hence $S \cap T \subseteq S$.

Let $x \in S$. Then $(x \in S) \vee (x \in T)$ holds, its first disjunct being true; so $x \in S \cup T$. Hence $S \subseteq S \cup T$. Transitivity of $\subseteq$ chains the two.
</Proof>

The proofs are short because $p \wedge q \Rightarrow p$ and $p \Rightarrow p \vee q$ are tautologies. Read as an order statement: $S \cap T$ is the **largest** set contained in both $S$ and $T$, and $S \cup T$ is the **smallest** set containing both. The two propositions above are half of that characterisation; the other half is that any set contained in both $S$ and $T$ is contained in $S \cap T$, which is equally quick.

## Disjointness

$S, T$ are **disjoint** when $S \cap T = \emptyset$. This is a universal negative, $\forall x\, \neg(x \in S \wedge x \in T)$, so §1.12 dictates an arbitrary-element argument.

> **Proposition.** In $\mathbb{R}$, the zero sets of $\sin$ and $\cos$ are disjoint.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$.</div>
  <div slot="want">No $x$ satisfies both $\sin x = 0$ and $\cos x = 0$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Suppose some $x$ did. Substituting into the Pythagorean identity forces $0 = 1$. This is *suppose not* (§2.5) in its shortest possible form.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose, for contradiction, that some $x \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfies $\sin x = 0$ and $\cos x = 0$. Then
$$1 = \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 0 + 0 = 0,$$
which is false. Hence no such $x$ exists, and $\{x \mid \sin x = 0\} \cap \{x \mid \cos x = 0\} = \emptyset$.
</Proof>

Their union, by contrast, is not all of $\mathbb{R}$ — $x = \pi/4$ lies in neither. It *is* the zero set of $\sin x \cos x$, i.e. of $\tfrac12 \sin 2x$, giving $\{n\pi/2 \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$; proving that equality is a double inclusion (§2.6).

## gcd and lcm are meets and joins

With $D_n$ the positive divisors and $M_n$ the positive multiples of $n$:
$$
D_{20} \cap D_{24} = \{1,2,4\}, \quad \max = 4 = \gcd(20,24),
$$
$$
M_{12} \cap M_{18} = \{36,72,\ldots\} = M_{36}, \quad \min = 36 = \operatorname{lcm}(12,18).
$$

The second display contains a genuine theorem: the common multiples of $a$ and $b$ are exactly the multiples of $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$. Dually, $D_a \cap D_b = D_{\gcd(a,b)}$. These say that $\gcd$ and $\operatorname{lcm}$ play exactly the roles $\cap$ and $\cup$ play for sets, now for the divisibility order on $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ — the same pattern a second time, and the reason gcd/lcm identities mirror $\cap$/$\cup$ identities so exactly. Both distributive laws hold there too, and for the same structural reason.

## Indexed families

For a family $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$ of sets indexed by a set $I$:
$$
\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i = \{x \mid \exists i \in I,\; x \in A_i\},
\qquad
\bigcap_{i \in I} A_i = \{x \mid \forall i \in I,\; x \in A_i\}.
$$

The binary operations were $\vee$ and $\wedge$; the indexed ones are $\exists$ and $\forall$. This is the same escalation §1.11 described — quantifiers as infinitary connectives — and it is why De Morgan for indexed families,
$$
\overline{\bigcup_{i} A_i} = \bigcap_i \overline{A_i},
$$
is proved by exactly the argument that gives $\neg \exists \equiv \forall \neg$.

Examples: $\bigcap_{n \in \mathbb{N}} (-\tfrac1{n+1}, \tfrac1{n+1}) = \{0\}$, and $\bigcup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} [-n, n] = \mathbb{R}$. Both require proof by double inclusion, and the first is the standard illustration that an infinite intersection of open intervals need not be open. Finitely many intersections preserve openness; infinitely many need not, and that asymmetry between the finite and the infinite is one you will meet repeatedly in calculus.

<Warning title="Venn diagrams are heuristics">
A two-circle diagram presupposes that all four regions are nonempty. If you are proving an identity, the diagram may still guide you, but the proof must be element chasing (§2.3). For three or more sets the diagrams stop being reliable anyway: no arrangement of circles produces all $2^n$ regions for $n \geq 4$.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Every identity in this chapter follows from a short list: commutativity, associativity, idempotence, identities, absorption, the distributive laws (§2.8), and De Morgan (§2.7). Nothing else is needed, and everything else is a consequence.

That list is worth knowing as a list, because the same seven properties govern $\wedge, \vee, \neg$ in Chapter 1 and the AND/OR/NOT of a digital circuit. Three subjects, one set of rules — which is why a fact proved in any one of them transfers to the other two without reproof.
</Aside>

Union and intersection are what you compute when a problem imposes more than one condition.

**Domains intersect.** The domain of $f+g$ is the intersection of the two domains, and the domain of $f/g$ is that intersection minus the zeros of $g$. Composite functions intersect too: $\operatorname{dom}(g\circ f) = \{x \in \operatorname{dom} f : f(x)\in\operatorname{dom} g\}$ — a pre-image, and §3.3's subject.

Solution sets of systems intersect; solution sets of factored equations unite. That is the entire logic of solving $(x-1)(x^2-4)>0$: factor, find where each factor changes sign, and assemble the answer as a union of intervals.

**The asymmetry between finite and infinite intersections has a calculus face.** Each set $(-1/n, 1/n)$ is an open interval, and
$$\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(-\tfrac1n, \tfrac1n\right) = \{0\}$$
is not. Infinitely many intersections can destroy a property that finitely many preserve — which is precisely why theorems about limits require care that theorems about finite sums do not, and why "the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits" holds for two terms and fails for infinitely many.

**In linear algebra, intersection preserves structure and union does not.** The intersection of two subspaces is a subspace — a three-line element chase. The union generally is not: the $x$-axis and the $y$-axis in $\mathbb{R}^2$ are each subspaces, and their union is not closed under addition, since $(1,0)+(0,1)$ escapes it.

That failure is why the subject defines the **sum** $U+W$ rather than using the union. It is the smallest subspace containing both, and needing to invent it is a consequence of exactly the fact above.

*You have matrices, determinants and systems; linear algebra builds its vocabulary on top of them. Above: **subspace** — a set of vectors closed under adding and scaling.*
</WhereThisGoes>
