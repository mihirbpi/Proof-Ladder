$$
S \cup T = \{x \mid x \in S \vee x \in T\},
\qquad
S \cap T = \{x \mid x \in S \wedge x \in T\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

The definitions are transcriptions of $\vee$ and $\wedge$, and the working forms — the ones every proof in Chapter 2 unfolds to — are
$$
x \in S \cup T \iff (x \in S) \vee (x \in T),
\qquad
x \in S \cap T \iff (x \in S) \wedge (x \in T). \tag{1}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\cup$ is "pooled with".*

Since $\vee$ is inclusive, the union contains the intersection. Venn diagrams are legitimate for building intuition and for checking whether a proposed identity is plausible; they are not proofs, because a diagram of two or three sets encodes an assumption about which regions are nonempty that a general argument may not make.

## Basic algebra

All of the following are immediate from (1) plus the corresponding fact about the joining word:

| Property | Statement | Logical source |
|---|---|---|
| Commutativity | $S \cup T = T \cup S$ | $\vee$ symmetric |
| Associativity | $(S \cup T) \cup R = S \cup (T \cup R)$ | $\vee$ associative |
| Idempotence | $S \cup S = S$ | $p \vee p \equiv p$ |
| Identity | $S \cup \emptyset = S$, $S \cap X = S$ for $S \subseteq X$ | $p \vee F \equiv p$ |
| Absorption | $S \cup (S \cap T) = S$ | $p \vee (p \wedge q) \equiv p$ |

Associativity is what lets you write $A \cup B \cup C$ over finitely many sets without parentheses.

### Intersection is smaller, union is larger

> **Proposition.** $S \cap T \subseteq S \subseteq S \cup T$.

<Proof>
Let $x \in S \cap T$. Then $x \in S \wedge x \in T$, so for instance $x \in S$. So $S \cap T \subseteq S$.

Let $x \in S$. Then $(x \in S) \vee (x \in T)$ holds, its left half being true; so $x \in S \cup T$. So $S \subseteq S \cup T$. Transitivity of $\subseteq$ chains the two.
</Proof>

The proofs are short because $p \wedge q \Rightarrow p$ and $p \Rightarrow p \vee q$ are tautologies. Read as an order statement: $S \cap T$ is the **largest** set contained in both $S$ and $T$, and $S \cup T$ is the **smallest** set containing both. The two propositions above are half of that characterization; the other half is that any set contained in both $S$ and $T$ is contained in $S \cap T$, which is equally quick.

## Disjointness

$S, T$ are **disjoint** when $S \cap T = \emptyset$. This is a universal negative, $\forall x\, \neg(x \in S \wedge x \in T)$, so §1.12 dictates an arbitrary-element argument.

> **Proposition.** In $\mathbb{R}$, the zero sets of $\sin$ and $\cos$ are disjoint.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$ for every real number $x$.</div>
  <div slot="want">No $x$ satisfies both $\sin x = 0$ and $\cos x = 0$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Suppose some $x$ did. Substituting into the Pythagorean identity forces $0 = 1$. This is *suppose not* (§2.5) in its shortest possible form.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose, for contradiction, that some $x \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfies $\sin x = 0$ and $\cos x = 0$. Then
$$1 = \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 0 + 0 = 0,$$
which is false. So no such $x$ exists, and $\{x \mid \sin x = 0\} \cap \{x \mid \cos x = 0\} = \emptyset$.
</Proof>

Their union, by contrast, is not all of $\mathbb{R}$ — $x = \pi/4$ lies in neither. It *is* the zero set of $\sin x \cos x$, i.e. of $\tfrac12 \sin 2x$, giving $\{n\pi/2 \mid n \in \mathbb{Z}\}$; proving that equality is a double inclusion (§2.6).

## gcd and lcm are meets and joins

With $D_n$ the positive divisors and $M_n$ the positive multiples of $n$:
$$
D_{20} \cap D_{24} = \{1,2,4\}, \quad \max = 4 = \gcd(20,24),
$$
$$
M_{12} \cap M_{18} = \{36,72,\ldots\} = M_{36}, \quad \min = 36 = \operatorname{lcm}(12,18).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

The second display contains a genuine theorem: the common multiples of $a$ and $b$ are exactly the multiples of $\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)$. Dually, $D_a \cap D_b = D_{\gcd(a,b)}$. These say that $\gcd$ and $\operatorname{lcm}$ play exactly the roles $\cap$ and $\cup$ play for sets, now for the divisibility order on $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ — the same pattern a second time, and the reason gcd/lcm identities mirror $\cap$/$\cup$ identities so exactly. Both distributive laws hold there too, and for the same structural reason.

## More than two sets

Nothing restricts the operations to two sets:
$$
A_1 \cup A_2 \cup \cdots \cup A_n = \{x \mid x \text{ lies in at least one } A_i\},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

$$
A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_n = \{x \mid x \text{ lies in every } A_i\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

The two-set operations were *or* and *and*; the long ones are *there exists an $i$* and *for every $i$*. This is the escalation §1.11 described, and it is why De Morgan for many sets,
$$
\overline{A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n} = \overline{A_1} \cap \cdots \cap \overline{A_n},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

is proved by exactly the argument that turns *not there exists* into *for all not*.

<Warning title="Venn diagrams are heuristics">
A two-circle diagram takes for granted that all four regions are nonempty. If you are proving an identity, the diagram may still guide you, but the proof must be element chasing (§2.3). For three or more sets the diagrams stop being reliable anyway: no arrangement of circles produces all $2^n$ regions for $n \geq 4$.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Every identity in this chapter follows from a short list: commutativity, associativity, idempotence, identities, absorption, the distributive laws (§2.8), and De Morgan (§2.7). Nothing else is needed, and everything else is a consequence.

That list is worth knowing as a list, because the same seven properties govern $\wedge, \vee, \neg$ in Chapter 1 and the AND/OR/NOT of a digital circuit. Three subjects, one set of rules — which is why a fact proved in any one of them transfers to the other two without reproof.
</Aside>

Union and intersection are what you compute when a problem imposes more than one condition.

**Domains intersect.** The domain of $f+g$ is the intersection of the two domains, and the domain of $f/g$ is that intersection minus the zeros of $g$. Composite functions intersect too: the symbol $\circ$ is read *"after"*, so $g \circ f$ means do $f$ first and feed the result to $g$ (§3.7); its domain is $\{x \in \operatorname{dom} f : f(x)\in\operatorname{dom} g\}$, a pre-image, and §3.3's subject.

Solution sets of systems intersect; solution sets of factored equations unite. That is the entire logic of solving $(x-1)(x^2-4)>0$: factor, find where each factor changes sign, and assemble the answer as a union of intervals.

**The asymmetry between finitely and infinitely many intersections has a calculus face.** Each interval $(-1/n, 1/n)$ is open, and yet intersecting *all* of them — for every $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ — leaves only the single point $0$, which is not an open interval. Infinitely many intersections can destroy a property that finitely many preserve — which is exactly why theorems about limits require care that theorems about finite sums do not, and why "the limit of a sum is the sum of the limits" holds for two terms and fails for infinitely many.

</WhereThisGoes>
