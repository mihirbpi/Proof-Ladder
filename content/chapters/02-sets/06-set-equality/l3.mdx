What does it mean for two sets to be *equal*? The answer is short, and it determines how every set-equality proof in mathematics is written.

## The definition

$$
S = T \quad\text{means}\quad S \subseteq T \;\text{ and }\; T \subseteq S.
$$

That is it. Two sets are equal exactly when each is a subset of the other. This is called **double inclusion**, and the named move — the same one from module 1.9 — is **check both boxes**.

Why two directions? Because one is not enough. If you only prove $S \subseteq T$, then $T$ might have extra elements you never looked for. Only the second inclusion rules that out.

## The shape of every proof

> **($\subseteq$)** Take any $x \in S$. … Therefore $x \in T$.
> **($\supseteq$)** Take any $x \in T$. … Therefore $x \in S$.
> Both inclusions hold, so $S = T$.

Two element chases (module 2.3), back to back. Label them, so you and your reader can both count to two.

## A worked example

Let $M_{12}$ be the set of positive multiples of $12$, and $M_{18}$ the positive multiples of $18$.

> **Claim.** $M_{12} \cap M_{18} = M_{36}$.

In words: the numbers that are multiples of both $12$ and $18$ are exactly the multiples of $36$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$M_{12} \cap M_{18}$ holds the numbers divisible by $12$ and by $18$. And $36$ is the smallest number divisible by both.</div>
  <div slot="want">Both inclusions.</div>
  <div slot="how">One direction is easy: any multiple of $36$ is a multiple of $12$ (since $36 = 12 \times 3$) and of $18$ (since $36 = 18 \times 2$). The other needs a fact about factors: if a number has both a $12$ and an $18$ inside it, it must have a $36$ inside it.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\supseteq$)** Take any multiple of $36$ — any one at all.

It is some number of $36$s. Each $36$ splits into three $12$s, so the whole thing splits into $12$s: it is a multiple of $12$. Each $36$ also splits into two $18$s, so it is a multiple of $18$ too.

Being in both, it is in $M_{12} \cap M_{18}$.

**($\subseteq$)** Now take any number in $M_{12} \cap M_{18}$. Both $12$ and $18$ divide it.

Split each divisor into its prime factors. Since $12 = 2\times2\times3$ divides our number, the number has **two $2$s and one $3$** inside it. Since $18 = 2\times3\times3$ divides it, the number has **one $2$ and two $3$s** inside it.

To satisfy both at once it must have at least two $2$s *and* at least two $3$s. Those multiply to $4 \times 9 = 36$, so $36$ divides it, and it is in $M_{36}$.

Both inclusions hold, so $M_{12} \cap M_{18} = M_{36}$.
</Proof>

Notice the two halves needed completely different reasoning. The first was three lines of substitution; the second needed a real fact about factors. That asymmetry is normal.

## When a hypothesis says *or*, split into cases

Here is a rule that will save you again and again in this chapter.

If somewhere in your proof you are handed a statement of the form "$A$ **or** $B$", you do not get to pick the convenient one. You must handle both. That is **splitting into cases** (module 1.12).

And where do *or* statements come from in set theory? From unions! Since $x \in S \cup T$ means $x \in S$ **or** $x \in T$, any proof that starts by chasing an element of a union will need cases.

> **Claim.** For any sets $S$ and $T$, $S \cup (S \cap T) = S$.

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cup (S \cap T)$. Then $x \in S$ or $x \in S \cap T$ — an *or*, so we split.

*Case 1: $x \in S$.* Then we are done, since the goal is $x \in S$.

*Case 2: $x \in S \cap T$.* Then $x \in S$ and $x \in T$, so in particular $x \in S$.

In both cases $x \in S$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in S$. Then the statement "$x \in S$ or $x \in S \cap T$" is true, because its first half is. So $x \in S \cup (S \cap T)$.

Both inclusions hold, so the sets are equal.
</Proof>

<Warning title='"Plainly equal" is not a proof'>
Sometimes a set equality is so believable that writing two inclusions feels silly. Write them anyway. Believability is not a reason, and the whole point of this chapter is that you now have a method that does not depend on how a thing looks.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Double inclusion is forced, not chosen. Two sets are equal when they have the same elements — that is, when *being in $S$* and *being in $T$* amount to the same thing, whatever you are holding. And module 1.9 said an *if and only if* is two claims, one each way. So the two inclusions were hiding inside the definition of equality all along.
</Aside>
