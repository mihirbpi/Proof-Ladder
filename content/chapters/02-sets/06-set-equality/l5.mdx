Module 2.1 said a set is determined by its elements. Turned into a definition:

$$
S = T \quad\text{means}\quad S \subseteq T \ \wedge\ T \subseteq S.
$$

This is **double inclusion**, and the named move is **check both boxes**.

The definition is a conjunction, so module 1.9's rule applies without exception: **a set equality is two proofs.** Each half is an arbitrary-element argument (module 2.3), and they run in opposite directions.

The template never varies:

> **($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S$ be arbitrary. … Therefore $x \in T$.
>
> **($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in T$ be arbitrary. … Therefore $x \in S$.
>
> Both inclusions hold, so $S = T$.

## Why it has to be two proofs

Set equality could have been left as an undefined notion, and it is not. It is *defined* as double inclusion, and the reason is module 1.9: "same elements" is an *if and only if*, and an *if and only if* is two implications.

$$
S = T \iff \forall x\,(x \in S \Leftrightarrow x \in T),
$$
and splitting the inner $\Leftrightarrow$ into its two directions gives exactly $S \subseteq T$ and $T \subseteq S$.

So the two-proof structure is not a convention. It is what the definition unfolds to.

## A worked example

> **Proposition.** $\{\, x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x^2 - 1 = 0 \,\} = \{-1, 1\}$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Membership on the left unfolds to *$x$ real and $x^2 = 1$*. Membership on the right unfolds to *$x = -1$ or $x = 1$* — a disjunction.</div>
  <div slot="want">Both inclusions.</div>
  <div slot="how">($\subseteq$) factor and use the zero-product property, which produces an *or* — so the conclusion is a disjunction and no case split is needed to reach it. ($\supseteq$) the hypothesis is a disjunction, so this direction does need a case split (module 1.12).</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x^2 - 1 = 0\}$ be arbitrary. Then $x^2 - 1 = 0$, so
$$
(x-1)(x+1) = 0.
$$
A product of real numbers is zero only if one factor is zero, so $x - 1 = 0$ or $x + 1 = 0$, giving $x = 1$ or $x = -1$. Either way $x \in \{-1, 1\}$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in \{-1, 1\}$ be arbitrary, so $x = -1$ or $x = 1$. Split.

*Case 1: $x = 1$.* Then $x^2 - 1 = 1 - 1 = 0$ ✓, and $1 \in \mathbb{R}$.

*Case 2: $x = -1$.* Then $x^2 - 1 = 1 - 1 = 0$ ✓, and $-1 \in \mathbb{R}$.

Both cases give $x^2 - 1 = 0$, so $x$ lies in the left-hand set.

Both inclusions hold, so the sets are equal.
</Proof>

Note the asymmetry. The forward direction was algebra, the backward direction was verification, and the two required different work. **That is normal**, and it is why one direction being easy tells you nothing about the other.

Note also where the case split appeared: in the direction whose *hypothesis* was a disjunction. That is the general rule — **an *or* in what you are given forces cases; an *or* in what you want does not.**

## A second example

> **Proposition.** $\{\, n \in \mathbb{Z} \mid n \text{ is even} \,\} = \{\, 2k \mid k \in \mathbb{Z} \,\}$.

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $n$ be an arbitrary even integer. By definition $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$, so $n$ has the required form and lies in the right-hand set.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $m$ be an arbitrary element of the right-hand set, so $m = 2k$ for some integer $k$. Then $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ by closure, and $m = 2k$ exhibits $m$ as even.

Both inclusions hold.
</Proof>

This one looks like it says nothing, and that appearance is the point. The two sides are the **filtering** and **generating** descriptions of module 2.2, and the proof is the statement that they agree. Each direction unfolds one definition and folds up the other.

<Warning title="Obviously equal is not a proof">
Both examples above are obvious. Neither proof is optional.

Two reasons. First, "obvious" is not checkable — a reader cannot verify your intuition, only your argument. Second, the technique is what transfers: modules 2.7 and 2.8 prove set equalities that are *not* obvious, and they use this exact template. If you skip the structure when it is easy, you will not have it when it is hard.

The specific failure to avoid is proving one direction and stopping. In unlabelled prose that looks like a finished proof, which is why the template insists on marking ($\subseteq$) and ($\supseteq$) explicitly.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Double inclusion is why $\subseteq$ behaves like $\leq$.

For real numbers, $a \leq b$ and $b \leq a$ force $a = b$. For sets, $S \subseteq T$ and $T \subseteq S$ force $S = T$ — the same shape, and in the set case it is the definition rather than a theorem.

This pattern recurs throughout mathematics: to prove two things equal, show each is *at most* the other. Chapter 3 uses it for the sizes of infinite sets, where showing two sets have the same size means producing a matching in each direction.
</Aside>
