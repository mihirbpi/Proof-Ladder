Your book's locus theorems always come in pairs, and this module explains why the pairing is forced rather than stylistic.

## The principle

Two sets are equal exactly when they have the same members (module 2.1). Written out:

$$
S = T \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \forall x\ (x \in S \Leftrightarrow x \in T).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when". So the line reads: two sets are equal exactly when everything is in both or in neither.*

A biconditional is two conditionals (module 1.9), so this splits:

$$
S = T \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad S \subseteq T \ \text{ and } \ T \subseteq S.
$$

**Double inclusion.** The named move is **check both boxes**, and there is nothing optional about it: an equality of sets *is* two subset claims, so a proof of set equality *is* two element-chasing proofs.

## The shape

> **($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S$. … Therefore $x \in T$.
>
> **($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in T$. … Therefore $x \in S$.
>
> Hence $S = T$.

Label the halves. Without labels a reader cannot tell which containment they are reading, and the commonest way to write a broken proof is to prove one direction twice.

## The locus theorem pairs

Now open your textbook to any locus result. It states two theorems:

> *Every point on the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{AB}$ is equidistant from $A$ and $B$.*
>
> *Every point equidistant from $A$ and $B$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{AB}$.*

Those are $S \subseteq T$ and $T \subseteq S$ for
$$S = \text{the perpendicular bisector}, \qquad T = \{Q : QA = QB\}.$$

Together they say $S = T$ — which is what licenses the *definition* of the perpendicular bisector as a locus. Your book proves both because one alone would not establish the sets are the same, and it separates them because the two proofs use different tools.

<Warning title="One direction is not enough">
Suppose only the first theorem were proved. You would know the bisector sits inside the equidistant set — but not that it fills it. As far as that theorem is concerned, the equidistant set could be a whole region with the bisector as a sliver inside it.

The second theorem is what rules that out. It is where the *only* in "the set of all and only the points equidistant" gets earned.
</Warning>

## A worked set equality

> **Proposition.** $S \cap (S \cup T) = S$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in S \cap (S\cup T)$ means $x \in S$ and ($x \in S$ or $x \in T$).</div>
  <div slot="want">Both containments.</div>
  <div slot="how">One direction is immediate — the left side is an intersection with $S$, so its elements are in $S$. The other needs building up. Expect an asymmetry: absorption laws almost always have one easy half.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cap (S \cup T)$. By definition of $\cap$, $x \in S$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in S$. Then $x \in S \cup T$, since membership in either set suffices for the union. So $x \in S$ and $x \in S \cup T$, hence $x \in S \cap (S\cup T)$.

Therefore $S \cap (S \cup T) = S$.
</Proof>

That is Chapter 1's absorption law, $p \wedge (p \vee q)\equiv p$, transcribed. Every logical equivalence becomes a set identity this way, and the transcription is mechanical once you have the dictionary.

## When one direction needs cases

Harder equalities have a direction where the hypothesis is a $\vee$ — and a $\vee$ in a hypothesis means proof by cases (module 1.12), because you are not told which disjunct holds.

> **Proposition.** $S \cup (S \cap T) = S$.

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cup (S\cap T)$. Then $x \in S$ **or** $x \in S \cap T$. We consider both.

*Case 1: $x \in S$.* Done.

*Case 2: $x \in S \cap T$.* Then in particular $x \in S$. Done.

Either way $x \in S$, and the cases are exhaustive.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in S$. Then $x \in S \cup (S\cap T)$, since membership in the first set suffices.

Therefore $S \cup (S\cap T) = S$.
</Proof>

**The asymmetry is structural, not accidental.** A $\cup$ in the *source* set forces cases; a $\cup$ in the *target* set makes the step trivial, because you need only land in one part. Reading the statement before writing lets you predict which direction will be the work — a habit worth building now, since it scales to every proof in Chapters 3 and 4.

<Aside>
Some equalities can be proved as a chain of equivalences:
$$x \in S \Leftrightarrow \cdots \Leftrightarrow x \in T,$$
which does both directions at once. It is efficient when every step is genuinely reversible.

It is also where wrong proofs hide, because a step that looks like $\Leftrightarrow$ is often only $\Rightarrow$. Until reversibility-checking is automatic, write the two inclusions separately. The extra four lines cost nothing and the structure is visible to your reader.
</Aside>

## Proving inequality

To show $S \neq T$, negate. By modules 1.9 and 1.11, the negation of double inclusion is: **at least one containment fails**, which means

$$\exists x\ (x \in S \wedge x \notin T) \quad\text{or}\quad \exists x\ (x \in T \wedge x \notin S).$$

One element, on one side and not the other. A single object settles it.

*Is $\{\text{rhombi}\} = \{\text{rectangles}\}$?* No: a rhombus with a $60°$ angle is in the first and not the second. One shape, one sentence, done — and note that you did **not** need to check the other direction. Failing either containment is enough.
