## The definition

$$
S = T \quad:\Leftrightarrow\quad S \subseteq T \;\wedge\; T \subseteq S.
$$

Set equality is **double inclusion**, and the named move is **check both boxes**. Every set-equality proof in mathematics has two halves, and they are usually different in difficulty and technique.

The definition is not an arbitrary convention. Sets are extensional (module 2.1): $S = T$ iff $\forall x\,(x \in S \Leftrightarrow x \in T)$. A biconditional is two implications (module 1.9), and each implication is a subset claim. So the two inclusions were already inside the definition of equality.

## Template

> **($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S$ be arbitrary. … Hence $x \in T$.
> **($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in T$ be arbitrary. … Hence $x \in S$.
> Therefore $S = T$. $\square$

Label the halves. Unlabelled proofs of equalities routinely contain the same direction twice.

## Disjunction forces cases

Since $x \in S \cup T$ unfolds to $x \in S \vee x \in T$, chasing an element out of a union hands you a disjunction — and a disjunctive hypothesis must be handled by **proof by cases** (module 1.12). You do not get to choose the convenient disjunct.

This single observation predicts the structure of most proofs in the rest of the chapter.

## The trigonometric proposition

Let
$$
S = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid \sin x = 0\}, \qquad
T = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid \cos x = 0\}, \qquad
R = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid \sin x \cos x = 0\}.
$$

> **Proposition.** $S \cup T = R$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in S \cup T$ means $\sin x = 0$ or $\cos x = 0$. $x \in R$ means the product $\sin x \cos x$ is zero. The relevant fact about $\mathbb{R}$: a product of two reals is zero if and only if at least one factor is zero (no zero divisors).</div>
  <div slot="want">Both inclusions.</div>
  <div slot="how">($\subseteq$) The hypothesis is a disjunction, so split into cases; in each, one factor vanishes and the product does too. ($\supseteq$) The hypothesis is a product being zero, so the no-zero-divisors fact hands us a disjunction directly.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cup T$ be arbitrary. Then $\sin x = 0$ or $\cos x = 0$; we split into cases.

*Case 1: $\sin x = 0$.* Then $\sin x \cos x = 0 \cdot \cos x = 0$.

*Case 2: $\cos x = 0$.* Then $\sin x \cos x = \sin x \cdot 0 = 0$.

In both cases $\sin x \cos x = 0$, so $x \in R$. The cases are exhaustive.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in R$ be arbitrary, so $\sin x \cos x = 0$. Since $\mathbb{R}$ has no zero divisors, a product is zero only if a factor is: $\sin x = 0$ or $\cos x = 0$. Hence $x \in S$ or $x \in T$, i.e. $x \in S \cup T$.

Both inclusions hold, so $S \cup T = R$.
</Proof>

The two halves used different facts: the forward direction used only that $0 \cdot a = 0$; the backward direction used the substantive no-zero-divisors property. Over a ring where that fails — $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, where $2 \cdot 3 = 0$ — the backward inclusion would be false. Noticing which structural fact each half consumes is how you learn where a theorem lives.

Note also that $S$ and $T$ are disjoint (module 2.4), so $R$ is partitioned by them — but disjointness played no role in the proof above. Do not import hypotheses you do not need.

## A second example: absorption

> **Proposition.** For all sets $S, T$: $S \cup (S \cap T) = S$.

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cup (S \cap T)$. Then $x \in S$ or $x \in S \cap T$.

*Case 1: $x \in S$.* Done.

*Case 2: $x \in S \cap T$.* Then $x \in S$ and $x \in T$, so $x \in S$.

Either way $x \in S$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in S$. Then the disjunction "$x \in S$ or $x \in S \cap T$" holds, since its first disjunct does. So $x \in S \cup (S \cap T)$.
</Proof>

Observe the asymmetry that recurs throughout: proving membership in a *union* is easy (establish one disjunct), while *using* membership in a union is expensive (case split). Intersection is the mirror image — using it is free, proving it costs two arguments.

<Warning title="Three failure modes">
**Proving one direction.** The commonest. Label your halves and count them.

**Reversible-step drift.** Writing a chain $x \in S \Leftrightarrow \cdots \Leftrightarrow x \in T$ is legitimate only if every step is genuinely biconditional. It usually is in set theory, since the definitions are biconditionals — but you must check, and stating two explicit inclusions is safer.

**Skipping a case.** If the hypothesis is a disjunction, both disjuncts need treatment. A missing case is a hole, not a stylistic lapse.
</Warning>
