## The definition

$$
S = T \quad:\Leftrightarrow\quad S \subseteq T \;\wedge\; T \subseteq S.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\wedge$ is "and". So the line reads: **$S$ and $T$ are the same collection exactly when each sits inside the other.*

Set equality is **double inclusion**, and the named move is **check both boxes**. Every set-equality proof in mathematics has two halves, and they are usually different in difficulty and technique.

The definition is not a convention someone picked. A set is fixed by its members and nothing else (module 2.1), so two sets are equal exactly when *being in one* and *being in the other* amount to the same thing, whatever object you hold up. And an if-and-only-if statement is two implications (module 1.9), and each implication is a subset claim. So the two inclusions were already inside the definition of equality.

## Template

> **First half.** Take any member of $S$. … So it is in $T$.
> **Second half.** Take any member of $T$. … So it is in $S$.
> Each sits inside the other, so the two sets are equal. $\square$

Label the halves. Unlabeled proofs of equalities routinely contain the same direction twice.

## An *or* in the hypothesis forces cases

Being in a union means being in one **or** the other, so following an element out of a union hands you an *or* — and an *or* in the hypothesis must be handled by **proof by cases** (module 1.12). You do not get to choose whichever half is convenient.

This single observation predicts the structure of most proofs in the rest of the chapter.

## A worked proposition

Let
$$
S = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x - 2 = 0\}, \qquad
T = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x + 3 = 0\}, \qquad
R = \{x \in \mathbb{R} \mid (x-2)(x+3) = 0\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: $S$ holds the numbers with $x - 2 = 0$, $T$ the numbers with $x + 3 = 0$, and $R$ the numbers where the product of those two is zero.*

> **Proposition.** The union of $S$ and $T$ is exactly $R$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Being in the union means $x - 2 = 0$ or $x + 3 = 0$. Being in $R$ means the product $(x-2)(x+3)$ is zero. The relevant fact about numbers: a product of two of them is zero if and only if at least one factor is zero.</div>
  <div slot="want">Both inclusions.</div>
  <div slot="how">($\subseteq$) The hypothesis is a disjunction, so split into cases; in each, one factor is zero and the product is too. ($\supseteq$) The hypothesis is a product being zero, so the zero-product fact hands us a disjunction directly.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>

*The symbols in this proof: $\in$ is "is in", $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers. In words, what follows shows: the two collections have exactly the same members, shown by checking each sits inside the other.*

**($\subseteq$)** Take any $x \in S \cup T$. Then $x - 2 = 0$ or $x + 3 = 0$; we split into cases.

*Case 1: $x - 2 = 0$.* Then $(x-2)(x+3) = 0 \cdot (x+3) = 0$.

*Case 2: $x + 3 = 0$.* Then $(x-2)(x+3) = (x-2) \cdot 0 = 0$.

In both cases $(x-2)(x+3) = 0$, so $x \in R$. The cases are exhaustive.

**($\supseteq$)** Take any $x \in R$, so $(x-2)(x+3) = 0$. In $\mathbb{R}$ a product is zero only if a factor is: $x - 2 = 0$ or $x + 3 = 0$. So $x \in S$ or $x \in T$, i.e. $x \in S \cup T$.

Both inclusions hold, so $S \cup T = R$.
</Proof>

The two halves used different facts: the forward direction used only that $0 \cdot a = 0$; the backward direction used the substantive fact that a product of reals cannot be zero unless a factor is. That fact is not automatic. On a six-hour clock, where the hours run $0$ to $5$ and adding wraps around, $2 \times 3$ lands on $0$ with neither factor zero — and there the backward inclusion would be false. Noticing which structural fact each half consumes is how you learn where a theorem lives.

Note also that $S$ and $T$ are disjoint (module 2.4), so $R$ is partitioned by them — but disjointness played no role in the proof above. Do not import hypotheses you do not need.

## A second example: absorption

> **Proposition.** For any sets $S$ and $T$: pooling $S$ with the overlap of $S$ and $T$ gives back $S$.

<Proof>

*The symbols in this proof: $\in$ is "is in", $\cup$ is "pooled with", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", $\subseteq$ is "sits inside". In words, what follows shows: pooling $S$ with the overlap of $S$ and $T$ gives back just $S$.*

**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in S \cup (S \cap T)$. Then $x \in S$ or $x \in S \cap T$.

*Case 1: $x \in S$.* Done.

*Case 2: $x \in S \cap T$.* Then $x \in S$ and $x \in T$, so $x \in S$.

Either way $x \in S$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in S$. Then "$x \in S$ or $x \in S \cap T$" holds, since its left half does. So $x \in S \cup (S \cap T)$.
</Proof>

Observe the asymmetry that recurs throughout: proving membership in a *union* is easy (prove either half), while *using* membership in a union is expensive (case split). Intersection is the mirror image — using it is free, proving it costs two arguments.

<Warning title="Three failure modes">
**Proving one direction.** The commonest. Label your halves and count them.

**Reversible-step drift.** Writing a chain of *exactly whens* from one membership statement to another is legitimate only if every single step really does run both ways. It usually is in set theory, since the definitions are if-and-only-if statements — but you must check, and stating two explicit inclusions is safer.

**Skipping a case.** If the hypothesis is an *or*, both halves need treatment. A missing case is a hole, not a stylistic lapse.
</Warning>
