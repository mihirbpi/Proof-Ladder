Module 1.1 left a loose end. The sentence
$$
3x - 1 = 0
$$
is not a statement: it has no truth value until you say something about $x$. Plug in $x = \tfrac13$ and it is true; plug in $x = 5$ and it is false.

A **quantifier** is what closes that gap. Attach one and you get a sentence with a definite truth value — a statement you can prove.

## The two quantifiers

$$
\forall x \in S,\ p(x) \qquad\text{and}\qquad \exists x \in S,\ p(x)
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some".*

The first reads *for all $x$ in $S$, $p(x)$*, and claims $p$ holds at **every** element of $S$. The second reads *there exists $x$ in $S$ such that $p(x)$*, and claims it holds at **at least one**.

Both name the set $S$ explicitly, and that is not decoration:

- $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ x^2 \geq 0$ — *every real number squares to at least $0$* — is **true**.
- $\forall x \in \mathbb{C},\ x^2 \geq 0$ is not even meaningful, since $\mathbb{C}$ has no order.
- $\exists x \in \mathbb{Z},\ 3x - 1 = 0$ is **false** — no integer works.
- $\exists x \in \mathbb{Q},\ 3x - 1 = 0$ is **true**, witnessed by $x = \tfrac13$.

The last two have the same condition and different truth values. **The set is part of the claim**, and a quantified statement written without one is unfinished.

## What each one costs to prove

This is the asymmetry that shapes the rest of the course.

| Claim | To prove | To disprove |
|---|---|---|
| $\forall x \in S,\ p(x)$ | argue about an arbitrary element | exhibit one failure |
| $\exists x \in S,\ p(x)$ | exhibit one success | argue about an arbitrary element |

One column is cheap and one is expensive, and which is which flips between the rows. Module 1.12 turns each of the four boxes into a template.

## Exactly one

$$
\exists!\, x \in S,\ p(x)
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\exists!$ is "there is exactly one".*

reads *there exists a unique $x$ in $S$ with $p(x)$*, and it is two claims bolted together: at least one, and at most one.

> **Proposition.** $\exists!\, x \in \mathbb{R},\ 3x - 1 = 0$ — *exactly one real number $x$ satisfies $3x-1=0$*.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$3x - 1 = 0$ is a linear equation with a nonzero coefficient on $x$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Two things, separately: some real number solves it, and no two different ones do.</div>
  <div slot="how">Existence by producing the number. Uniqueness by the standard move — suppose $a$ and $b$ both work, then force $a = b$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**Existence.** Take $x = \tfrac13$. Then $3\cdot\tfrac13 - 1 = 0$ ✓, and $\tfrac13 \in \mathbb{R}$.

**Uniqueness.** Suppose $a$ and $b$ are real numbers with $3a - 1 = 0$ and $3b - 1 = 0$. Then $3a - 1 = 3b - 1$, so $3a = 3b$, and dividing by $3$ gives $a = b$.

So exactly one real number satisfies the equation.
</Proof>

Notice the shape of the uniqueness half. It does not hunt for a second solution and fail to find one — failing to find something proves nothing. It **assumes two solutions and derives that they were the same one all along.**

<Warning title="Existence and uniqueness are separate jobs">
$\exists!$ is not a single stronger $\exists$. It is a conjunction, and module 1.9's rule applies: count the parts and prove each.

Your geometry course has already done this to you. *Every segment has exactly one midpoint* is an $\exists!$ claim, and the textbook proof has two halves — construct the point, then show any point with that property is the one you constructed. The second half is the one students skip.
</Warning>

## Nesting them

Quantifiers stack, and this is where reading carefully starts to matter:
$$
\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ \exists y \in \mathbb{R},\ y > x.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some".*

Read left to right: *given any real $x$, you can find a real $y$ bigger than it.* True — take $y = x + 1$.

Now swap the two quantifiers, changing nothing else:
$$
\exists y \in \mathbb{R},\ \forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ y > x.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some".*

*There is a single real $y$ bigger than every real $x$.* False — whatever $y$ you name, $x = y+1$ defeats it.

**Same symbols, same order of $x$ and $y$ in the inequality, opposite truth values.** The only difference is which quantifier came first.

The reason is what each one is allowed to depend on. In the true version $y$ is chosen *after* $x$, so it may depend on $x$ — and it does, since $y = x+1$. In the false version $y$ is fixed *before* $x$ is mentioned, so it must work for all of them at once, and no number does.

<Aside>
Order-of-quantifiers is not a technicality; it is the single hardest line in first-year calculus.

*For every tolerance there is a distance that keeps you inside it* is continuity. *There is one distance that works for every tolerance* is a much stronger statement, and it is false for most continuous functions.

Two statements, the same five ingredients, and swapping two words changes which one you have proved. Everything in this module is practice for reading that line correctly.
</Aside>
