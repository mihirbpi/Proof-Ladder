An **open statement** (or predicate (an open statement, §1.1)) $p(x)$ is a sentence with an unpinned variable in it; it has no truth value until the variable is pinned down. There are exactly two ways to bind it, and everything that makes mathematical statements hard to read comes from how they interact.

## The two quantifiers

$$
\forall x \in S,\; p(x)
\qquad\qquad
\exists x \in S,\; p(x)
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some". So the line reads: on the left, every element of $S$ has the property; on the right, at least one does.*

The first, the ***for all* quantifier**, says $p$ holds at every element of $S$; the second, the ***there exists* quantifier**, says $p$ holds at at least one. Both produce genuine statements: given $S$ and $p$, each is definitely true or definitely false.

### Two structural remarks

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\emptyset$ reads "the empty set", the set with nothing in it (§2.1).*

Both matter more than they look.

**The domain is part of the claim.** $\exists x,\, 3x - 1 = 0$ is true over the rationals, over the real numbers, and over $\mathbb{C}$; false over the integers. $\forall x,\, x^2 \geq 0$ is true over the real numbers and false over $\mathbb{C}$ (where $\geq$ is not even defined, which is a different and worse kind of failure). Writing $\forall x, \ldots$ without a domain is not shorthand; it is an incomplete sentence, and in a proof it is where errors hide.

### Quantifiers are *and* and *or* stretched to infinity

Over a finite domain $S = \{a_1, \ldots, a_n\}$,
$$
\forall x \in S,\, p(x) \;\equiv\; p(a_1) \wedge \cdots \wedge p(a_n),
\qquad
\exists x \in S,\, p(x) \;\equiv\; p(a_1) \vee \cdots \vee p(a_n).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or". So the line reads: over a finite collection $a_1, \ldots, a_n$, *every $x$ has the property* is just the properties of all the members joined by *and*, and *some $x$ has it* is the same list joined by *or*. The two words are long *and*s and long *or*s*

So $\forall$ is an endless chain of $\wedge$, and $\exists$ an endless chain of $\vee$. Every fact about quantifiers in this chapter — including the negation rules of §1.11 — is the corresponding fact about $\wedge$ and $\vee$ pushed to infinity.

A consequence worth stating: over $S = \emptyset$, the claim $\forall x \in \emptyset,\, p(x)$ — *every $x$ in the empty set satisfies $p$* — is **true** for every $p$, since there is no $x$ to check; and $\exists x \in \emptyset,\, p(x)$ — *some $x$ in the empty set satisfies $p$* — is **false**, since there is no $x$ to produce. The first is vacuous truth (§1.5) in quantifier form.

## Unique existence

$$
\exists! x \in S,\, p(x)
\;:\equiv\;
\big(\exists x \in S,\, p(x)\big) \wedge \big(\forall x, y \in S,\, (p(x) \wedge p(y)) \Rightarrow x = y\big).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists!$ is "there is exactly one", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces". So the line reads: exactly one element has the property — at least one does, and no two different ones both do.*

Existence and uniqueness are independent things to check, discharged separately.

> **Proposition.** $\exists! x \in \mathbb{R}$ with $3x - 1 = 0$ — *exactly one real number satisfies it*.

<Proof>
*Existence.* Take $x = \tfrac13$. Then $3 \cdot \tfrac13 - 1 = 0$.

*Uniqueness.* Suppose $a$ and $b$ are real numbers satisfy $3a - 1 = 0$ and $3b - 1 = 0$. Subtracting, $3a - 3b = 0$, so $3(a - b) = 0$. Since a product of non-zero reals is never zero and $3 \neq 0$, we get $a - b = 0$, i.e. $a = b$.
</Proof>

Note where the uniqueness argument actually spent its hypothesis: on the fact that a product of non-zero reals is never zero. Among remainders on division by $6$, $3x = 3$ has *three* solutions — $x = 1, 3, 5$ — and the "same" argument fails at exactly that step. Uniqueness proofs are where the surrounding structure shows through.

## The evidence asymmetry

| Statement | Proof requires | Disproof requires |
|---|---|---|
| $\forall x \in S,\, p(x)$ | an argument valid for arbitrary $x \in S$ | a single **counterexample** |
| $\exists x \in S,\, p(x)$ | a single **witness** | an argument valid for arbitrary $x \in S$ |

The diagonal structure is the reason mathematics is asymmetric in difficulty. A universal claim about an infinite domain can never be proved by computation, however extensive: Goldbach's conjecture has been verified past $4 \times 10^{18}$, and that is not one step of a proof. The other way round, a universal claim can be destroyed by a single number, which is why counterexample (§1.11: one case where the claim fails)-hunting is a real research activity.

<Warning title="The two moves that look like proofs and are not">
**Proving $\forall$ by example.** "The claim holds for $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots, 40$, so it holds." Euler's polynomial $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for $n = 0, \ldots, 39$ and composite at $n = 40$. Pólya's conjecture — that among $1, \ldots, n$ at least half the numbers have an odd number of prime factors — survives to $n \approx 9 \times 10^{8}$ and then fails.

**Disproving $\exists$ by failed search.** "I tried many values and none worked, so none exists." This is an argument about your search, not about the domain. Disproving $\exists x, p(x)$ requires proving $\forall x, \neg p(x)$ — a universal claim, with all the work that involves.
</Warning>

## Nested quantifiers: order is content

Quantifiers of different kinds cannot be swapped.

$$
\forall x \in \mathbb{R}\; \exists y \in \mathbb{R},\; x + y = 0
\qquad\text{versus}\qquad
\exists y \in \mathbb{R}\; \forall x \in \mathbb{R},\; x + y = 0
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some". So the line reads: on the left, each $x$ gets its own partner; on the right, one single $y$ would have to work for every $x$ at once.*

The first is true: given $x$, choose $y = -x$. The choice of $y$ is permitted to depend on $x$ because $y$ is introduced *after* $x$ and inside its reach. The second is false: it demands one $y$ that works for all $x$ simultaneously, and $x = 1, x = 2$ already conflict.

The governing principle: **an inner quantifier may depend on the outer ones; an outer one may not depend on the inner ones.** When you read a nested statement, read it as a game — the $\forall$ variables are chosen by an opponent, the $\exists$ variables by you, in the order written, and the statement is true iff you have a winning strategy.

Two of the *same* kind can be swapped freely: $\forall x \forall y$ says what $\forall y \forall x$ says, and likewise for two $\exists$. Mixed ones cannot, in general.

## Why quantifier order decides so much later

Here is a pair of statements that differ only in the order of two quantifiers.

> **(a)** For every tolerance, and for every point, there is a window around that point small enough to keep the output inside the tolerance.
>
> **(b)** For every tolerance, there is a *single* window that works at every point at once.

The only change is moving *there is a window* across *for every point*. In (a) the window may depend on where you are; in (b) one window must serve everywhere.

That single transposition separates two genuinely different behaviors. A function can wobble gently in the middle of its domain and wildly near the edge, satisfying (a) and failing (b) — $1/x$ close to zero is the standard example. Readers who find later courses unreadable are almost always readers for whom quantifier order is not yet load-bearing.

<Aside>
The game reading is not merely a mnemonic. It is formalized in logic as a two-player game, where the truth of a quantified formula is literally the existence of a winning strategy for the verifier. The number of quantifier alternations in a formula is a genuine complexity measure — it sorts into layers the arithmetical hierarchy, and it sorts into layers the polynomial hierarchy in complexity theory.
</Aside>
