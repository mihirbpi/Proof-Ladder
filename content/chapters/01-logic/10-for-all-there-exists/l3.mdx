Back in module 1.1 we met a sentence that was not quite a statement:

> This shape has four equal sides.

It is not true and it is not false, because we were never told which shape. This module shows the two ways to finish such a sentence.

## For all

Put the words **for all** in front and you get a real statement:

> **For all** rectangles, the shape has four equal sides.

Now it has a definite truth value. It is false — a $2$-by-$5$ rectangle is a rectangle and does not have four equal sides.

A **for all** claim says: this holds in every single case, no exceptions.

Here is a true one:

> **For all** squares, the shape is a rectangle.

Every square really is a rectangle, with no exceptions. That is the shape hierarchy you use when sorting quadrilaterals.

And a true one about numbers:

> **For all** counting numbers, the number multiplied by itself is not less than the number.

## There exists

The other way to finish the sentence is with **there exists**:

> **There exists** a rectangle with four equal sides.

This claims at least one case works. It does not claim they all do — most rectangles fail. It just says: somewhere out there, one of them does the job.

Is it true? Yes — a square. One example, and the claim is settled forever.

Here is another:

> **There exists** a counting number that leaves no remainder when you share it among $3$, among $4$, and among $5$.

True: $60$ works.

## The asymmetry — this is the important part

| Claim | To prove it | To disprove it |
|---|---|---|
| **For all** cases, … | argue about *every* case at once | find **one** case where it fails |
| **There exists** a case where … | find **one** case that works | argue that *every* case fails |

Proving a *for all* is hard and knocking it down is easy. Proving a *there exists* is easy and knocking it down is hard. The difficulty is always on one side, and which side depends entirely on the first two words.

This is not a small observation. It is why a single strange example can destroy a claim that a hundred examples had supported — module 1.11 is all about that.

## How you prove a *for all* without checking everything

You cannot check every rectangle; there are infinitely many. So here is the move, and it is the one that makes the rest of this course possible.

**Take any one and follow it.** Pick a case you know nothing special about, reason about it, and if you never used anything special, your reasoning covered all of them.

> **Claim.** For all squares, the shape is a rectangle.
>
> *Take any one and follow it.* Whatever square you picked, being a square it has four sides and four square corners. Four sides and four square corners is what a rectangle means. Then the one you picked is a rectangle — and you never used anything about *which* square it was, so this works for all of them.

## Exactly one

There is a third shape, greedier than the other two:

> There is **exactly one** counting number that is even and prime.

**Unique** means *exactly one*: at least one, and no more than one. Settling it takes two separate jobs.

1. **At least one.** Show something works: $2$ is even, and $2$ is prime. ✓
2. **At most one.** Show nothing else could. Any other even number splits into groups of $2$ with more than one group, so it has $2$ as a factor besides $1$ and itself — which means it is not prime.

Both jobs done, so the number is unique.

<Warning title="Say which collection you mean">
"For all shapes, the shape has four sides" is false if you mean *all* shapes, and true if you mean *all rectangles*.

Changing the collection changes the claim. Always say what you are quantifying over — a *for all* claim without a stated collection is not a finished sentence.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Almost every theorem you will ever meet is a *for all* statement in disguise. "If a shape is a square, it is a rectangle" really means "for all shapes, if the shape is a square then it is a rectangle." The *for all* is invisible but it is doing all the work — it is why the proof had to argue about a shape nobody had named.
</Aside>
