For a statement $p$, its **negation** $\neg p$ is the statement whose truth value is the opposite of $p$'s. Negation is the simplest connective in propositional logic and, precisely because it is simple, the source of most first-year proof errors.

Two consequences follow immediately:

- **Law of non-contradiction.** $p \wedge \neg p$ is always false.
- **Law of excluded middle.** $p \vee \neg p$ is always true.

Both look obvious in this setting; both are genuinely non-trivial once one moves to intuitionistic or paraconsistent logics, where excluded middle is not assumed. Classical mathematics, and this course, assumes both.

## Double negation

$\neg\neg p \equiv p$: negating twice returns you to the original. In a proof by contradiction (§2.1.6) one assumes $\neg q$, derives an impossibility, and concludes $\neg\neg q$, which is silently rewritten as $q$.

## Errors, catalogued

**Inequality boundaries flip.**
$$
\neg(x \geq 1) \equiv (x < 1), \quad \neg(x > 1) \equiv (x \leq 1), \quad \neg(x \neq 0) \equiv (x = 0).
$$

**Quantifier alternation.**
$$
\neg(\forall x\, p(x)) \equiv \exists x\, \neg p(x), \qquad \neg(\exists x\, p(x)) \equiv \forall x\, \neg p(x).
$$
Module 1.11 will prove these; they underwrite the entire practice of disproving universal claims by counterexample and existential claims by exhaustive negation.

**De Morgan (previewed).**
$$
\neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q, \quad \neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q.
$$
Negation swaps $\wedge$ and $\vee$. Module 1.4 proves this and it is arguably the most-used logical identity in the course.

<Warning title="Not the same as opposite">
"All primes are odd" and "no primes are odd" are both false. The negation of the first is "some prime is not odd" — witness $2$.
</Warning>

## Twin Primes, negated

The Twin Primes conjecture,
$$
\exists^\infty p \text{ prime with } p + 2 \text{ prime},
$$
has negation
$$
\text{there exists } N \text{ such that for all primes } p > N,\; p + 2 \text{ is not prime}.
$$
A proof of either would settle the conjecture. Notice the shape: negating a "$\exists^\infty$" produces an "eventually always not"; negating a $\forall$ produces an $\exists$.
