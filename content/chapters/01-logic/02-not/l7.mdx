For a statement $p$, its **negation** — written $\neg p$ and read *"not $p$"* — is the statement whose truth value is the opposite of $p$'s. Negation is the simplest joining word in propositional logic and, exactly because it is simple, the source of most first-year proof errors.

Two more symbols arrive in this module and are worth naming before they appear. $\wedge$ is read *"and"*, $\vee$ is read *"or"*, and $p \equiv q$ says the two statements have **identical truth tables** — not that they are the same sentence, but that they are true in exactly the same situations. §1.3 and §1.4 develop all three.

Two consequences follow immediately:

- **Law of non-contradiction.** $p \wedge \neg p$ is always false.
- **Law of excluded middle.** $p \vee \neg p$ is always true — every statement either holds or its negation does.

Both look obvious in this setting; both stop being obvious in systems of logic that decline to assume every statement is either true or false. Classical mathematics, and this course, assumes both.

## Double negation

$\neg\neg p \equiv p$: negating twice returns you to the original. In a proof by contradiction (§2.1.6) one assumes $\neg q$, derives an impossibility, and concludes $\neg\neg q$, which is silently rewritten as $q$.

That rewrite is the step most often left unmarked in a written proof, and it is worth knowing it is there. It is also the step that distinguishes proof by contradiction from proof by contrapositive (§1.8): the contrapositive route never introduces a double negation, and is correspondingly easier to check.

The same identity appears in three costumes later in the course, each time as an operation that undoes itself:

- $\overline{\overline S} = S$ for set complements (§2.5)
- $\overline{\bar z} = z$ for complex conjugation (§5.4)
- $(f^{-1})^{-1} = f$ for inverse functions

An operation that undoes itself is worth spotting, because it tells you immediately that applying it twice is wasted effort — a small but genuinely useful habit.

## Errors, catalogued

**Inequality boundaries flip.**
$$
\neg(x \geq 1) \equiv (x < 1), \quad \neg(x > 1) \equiv (x \leq 1), \quad \neg(x \neq 0) \equiv (x = 0).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

**Quantifier alternation.**
$$
\neg(\forall x\, p(x)) \equiv \exists x\, \neg p(x), \qquad \neg(\exists x\, p(x)) \equiv \forall x\, \neg p(x).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\neg$ is "not", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

Module 1.11 will prove these; they underwrite the entire practice of disproving universal claims by counterexample and existential claims by exhaustive negation.

**De Morgan (previewed).**
$$
\neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q, \quad \neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

Negation swaps $\wedge$ and $\vee$. Module 1.4 proves this and it is arguably the most-used logical identity in the course.

<Warning title="Not the same as opposite">
"All primes are odd" and "no primes are odd" are both false. The negation of the first is "some prime is not odd" — and $2$ settles it.

The general shape: $\neg(\forall x, p(x))$ is $\exists x, \neg p(x)$, and **not** $\forall x, \neg p(x)$. The second is the *contrary* — a stronger claim that can fail alongside the original.

The error is not confined to quantifiers. $\neg(\text{increasing})$ is not *decreasing*; a function can be neither. $\neg(\text{convergent})$ is not *"tends to infinity"*; $(-1)^n$ does neither. $\neg(x>0)$ is not $x<0$; zero exists. In each case the correct negation is the weakest statement incompatible with the original, and the tempting answer is something stronger.

**Assuming a contrary instead of a negation invalidates an indirect proof**, even when the contradiction you reach is genuine — because you assumed more than the negation gave you.
</Warning>

## Negation in the definitions ahead

Precalculus already trades in statements whose negations are non-obvious, and the pattern is worth seeing before the definitions get longer.

| Statement | Negation |
|---|---|
| $f$ is even | $f(-x)\neq f(x)$ for **some** $x$ |
| $f$ is bounded above | for **every** $M$, some $x$ has $f(x)>M$ |
| the sequence is increasing | some consecutive pair fails $a_n \leq a_{n+1}$ |
| $\theta$ is a solution of $\sin\theta = 1$ | $\sin\theta \neq 1$ |

The first three all convert a universal into an existential, and in each case the refutation is therefore a single witness — one $x$, one $M$-and-$x$ pair, one index. That asymmetry is the practical content of negation, and module 1.11 states it in general.

Note the second row especially: negating *bounded above* does not give *bounded below*, and it does not give *tends to infinity*. It gives an alternating $\forall\exists$ statement, and reading it off correctly is what module 1.11 exists for.

## Twin Primes, negated

The Twin Primes conjecture,
$$
\exists^\infty p \text{ prime with } p + 2 \text{ prime},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\exists$ is "there is some".*

has negation
$$
\text{there exists } N \text{ such that for all primes } p > N,\; p + 2 \text{ is not prime}.
$$
A proof of either would settle the conjecture. Notice the shape: negating a "$\exists^\infty$" produces an "eventually always not"; negating a $\forall$ produces an $\exists$.

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Negation is what turns a definition into a test you can apply.

**In calculus, the negated definitions are the working ones.** Definitions there are usually *for every … there is a …* claims, and you will rarely show one fails by staring at it. You negate it, mechanically, and read off what you now have to produce.

The classification of the ways a graph can break is a negation, expanded. Being unbroken at a point requires three things to hold and agree; negating that *and* — §1.3 calls it a conjunction — gives one case per thing that can go wrong, and the number of cases is exactly the number of conditions.

The lesson generalizes: **when a definition is a universal, its negation is an existential, and existentials are what proofs can hold onto.** A universal asks you to handle every case; its denial asks you to exhibit one. Getting that negation right is most of the work.

</WhereThisGoes>
