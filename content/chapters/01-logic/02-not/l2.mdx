Every telling has an **opposite**. The opposite of *the door is open* is *the door is not open*.

If one is true, the other is false. If one is false, the other is true. They never both win, and they never both lose. It is the fairest game there is.

Try a few:

- *It is raining.* → *It is not raining.*
- *This box is empty.* → *This box is not empty.*
- *Six is bigger than four.* → *Six is not bigger than four.*

The last one is false, because six really is bigger than four. That is fine. An opposite does not have to be true. It just has to disagree.

## A trap! Watch your step

Sam dumps out the block bin. Every single block is red.

*All the blocks are red.* — True.

Now, what is the opposite? Sam says: *all the blocks are blue!*

Nope. Nice try, Sam. That is a different telling, and it is also false. Two falses do not make an opposite.

The real opposite is: *not all the blocks are red.* That means at least one block is some other color. One green block. One yellow block. Even one tiny chipped orange block hiding under the couch — that would be enough to knock the first telling down.

## How to catch a fake opposite

A telling and its opposite must always **disagree**. Try it on a bin with three red blocks and one blue one:

- *All the blocks are red.* — False.
- *All the blocks are blue.* — Also false. They agree! So this is not the opposite.
- *Not all the blocks are red.* — True. They disagree. That is the one.

Sam's sentence and the real opposite behave differently, and now you can prove it with one bin of blocks.

<Warning title="Watch out">
The opposite of *all of them* is not *none of them*. The opposite of *all the blocks are red* is *at least one block is not red*. One is enough.
</Warning>
