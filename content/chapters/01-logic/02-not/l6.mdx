Every indirect proof in your geometry course begins the same way: *assume the opposite of what you want to prove.*

Getting that opposite right is the whole game. Get it wrong and the proof proves something else, usually something weaker, and the contradiction you eventually reach proves nothing about the theorem you were after.

## Definition

For a statement $p$, the **negation** $\neg p$ is the statement that is true exactly when $p$ is false.

    { p: true,  "¬p": false },
    { p: false, "¬p": true }

Two rows, and everything else in this module follows from them.

$p$ and $\neg p$ split every situation into two groups with no overlap and no gap. Two consequences, both used constantly:

- $p$ and $\neg p$ are **never both true**. An argument that forces both has shown its assumption to be false — the engine of indirect proof.
- One of the two is **always** true. That is the license for every case split you write.

Module 1.3 names these two facts and writes them in symbols, once *and* and *or* are on the table.

When your proof says *"either $P$ is inside the triangle or it is not,"* it is quoting the second, and it then owes an argument in each branch.

## Double negation

The symbol $\equiv$ is read *is logically equivalent to*: the two sides have identical truth tables. §1.4 treats it properly.

$$\neg\neg p \equiv p.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

The symbol $\equiv$ reads *is logically equivalent to* — the two have identical truth tables, and §1.4 treats it properly. Inverting twice is not an inversion. This is the last step of every indirect proof: having shown $\neg q$ leads to a contradiction, you conclude $\neg\neg q$, and silently replace it with $q$.

Your book usually leaves that replacement unstated. It is worth knowing it is happening.

## Negating inequalities and measures

The boundary is where negations go wrong.

$$\neg(x \geq 1) \text{ is } x<1, \qquad \neg(x>1) \text{ is } x \leq 1, \qquad \neg(x = 0) \text{ is } x\neq0.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not".*

**The negation of $>$ is $\leq$, not $<$.** Dropping the equality case is the standard slip, and it produces a proof with a hole exactly at the boundary — which is where the interesting cases usually live.

In geometry the same care applies:

| Statement | Negation |
|---|---|
| $\angle A$ is acute | $m\angle A \geq 90°$ |
| $\triangle ABC$ is equilateral | at least one pair of sides is not congruent |
| $\ell_1 \parallel \ell_2$ | $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ are not parallel |
| $P$ is on $\overline{AB}$ | $P$ is not on $\overline{AB}$ |

<Warning title="Not-acute is not obtuse">
$\neg(\text{acute})$ is **not** *obtuse*. An angle can be right, and right is neither.

Similarly, $\neg(\text{equilateral})$ is not *scalene* — an isosceles triangle is neither equilateral nor scalene. And $\neg(\ell_1\parallel\ell_2)$ is not *"$\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ intersect"*: in space they might be skew, meeting nowhere and still not parallel.

**The negation of a statement is not its opposite extreme.** The opposite extreme is a different, stronger claim, and assuming it in an indirect proof means assuming more than you are entitled to — which invalidates the proof even if you reach a contradiction.

This is the single most common error in indirect proofs, and it is why the first line of one deserves care.
</Warning>

## Negating quantified statements

Two symbols are needed here, and module 1.10 develops them properly. $\forall$ is read *"for all"* and $\exists$ is read *"there exists"*, so $\forall x,\ P(x)$ says $P$ holds for every $x$, and $\exists x,\ P(x)$ says at least one $x$ makes it hold.


$$\neg\big(\forall x,\ P(x)\big) \equiv \exists x,\ \neg P(x)$$

*So the line reads: saying "not everything has the property" is the same as saying "something does not have it."*

$$\neg\big(\exists x,\ P(x)\big) \equiv \forall x,\ \neg P(x)$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\neg$ is "not", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

### Two consequences worth having now

Push the negation inward, flip the quantifier. Module 1.11 develops this properly; these two are worth carrying from here.

**The negation of a universal is a counterexample claim.** *"Every rhombus is a rectangle"* is negated by *"some rhombus is not a rectangle"* — and a single rhombus with a $60°$ angle settles it.

**The negation of "all A are B" is not "no A is B."** Both can be false together: in a room of mixed hats, *"all hats are red"* and *"no hat is red"* both fail. The genuine negation is *"some A is not B"*, and one instance is enough.

Applied to your book: *"every parallelogram is a rectangle"* is false. Its negation is not *"no parallelogram is a rectangle"* — which is also false — but *"some parallelogram is not a rectangle,"* which is true — draw a slanted one.

## Negating compound statements

Negating an *and* gives an *or*, and negating an *or* gives an *and*. The denial of "both hold" is "at least one fails" — **not** "both fail". The joining word swaps as the negation passes through, and module 1.4 states this in symbols and proves it.

The practical consequence appears immediately in congruence criteria. To negate

> $\overline{AB}\cong\overline{DE}$ **and** $\angle B\cong\angle E$ **and** $\overline{BC}\cong\overline{EF}$

you get *at least one of the three fails* — an **or**, not an **and**. Assuming all three fail would be assuming far more than the hypothesis gives, and any proof built on it is broken.

## Negation in an indirect proof

> **Proposition.** A triangle cannot have two right angles.

Negate: *suppose a triangle has two right angles.* Then those two alone sum to $180°$, so the third angle measures $0°$ — impossible, since an angle of a triangle has positive measure.

Notice the negation was straightforward here because the claim was already negative. That is module 1.8's observation, and it is a reason to reach for the indirect route: **the negation of a negative claim is a positive one, and positive claims give you something to work with.**

<Aside>
Twin Primes says there are infinitely many primes $p$ with $p+2$ also prime.

Its negation says there are only **finitely many** such pairs — which would mean a complete, finite list exists.

That is a genuinely different kind of claim, and it shows what negation is for: it tells you what a refutation would have to look like. Nobody has produced such a list, and nobody has proved one cannot exist, which is exactly why the conjecture is open in both directions.
</Aside>
