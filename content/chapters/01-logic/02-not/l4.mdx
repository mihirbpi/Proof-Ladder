For any statement $p$, its **negation** — written $\neg p$ — is the statement that is true exactly when $p$ is false. Negation flips the truth value.

Two immediate consequences:

- $p$ and $\neg p$ are never both true.
- $p$ and $\neg p$ are never both false.

So one of them is always true and the other always false — exactly one.

Both consequences have names, and both get used as proof tools later:

- $p \wedge \neg p$ is false no matter what $p$ is. A statement of that shape is a **contradiction**. Derive one and you have shown that whatever you assumed cannot hold.
- $p \vee \neg p$ is true no matter what $p$ is — the **law of the excluded middle**. It is what licenses splitting an argument into the case where $p$ holds and the case where it doesn't, with nothing left over.

Neither is a technicality. Two of the three main proof techniques in this chapter run on them.

## Double negation

If negating flips the truth value, then negating twice puts it back. That is,
$$
\neg\neg p \text{ has the same truth value as } p.
$$
This looks obvious but it is a real fact worth naming: *double negation is the identity*. It shows up whenever you find yourself in a contradiction proof (§2.5).

It is also the quiet last step of every such proof. You want to show $q$. You assume $\neg q$, reach a contradiction, and conclude that $\neg q$ is false — which is to say $\neg\neg q$. Then you write down $q$ and move on. The replacement is double negation, and most write-ups never mention it.

## Negating with variables

- Negation of $x \geq 1$ is $x < 1$. (Note the flip *and* the change from $\geq$ to $<$.)
- Negation of $x \neq 0$ is $x = 0$.
- Negation of "$n$ is even" is "$n$ is odd" — but only because every integer is one or the other. Without that background fact, the negation would just be "$n$ is not even."

That last one is worth pausing on. *Not even* and *odd* mean the same thing for integers and not for other kinds of numbers: $1/2$ is not even, and it is not odd either. Whenever you replace a negation with a positive-sounding word, you are using a fact about what the possibilities are, and that fact deserves to be stated.

<Warning title="Common mistake">
The negation of $x \geq 1$ is $x < 1$, *not* $x \leq 1$. The negation of $x > 1$ is $x \leq 1$, not $x < 1$. The boundary case flips with the inequality.
</Warning>

## Negating quantities

- Negation of *there are infinitely many primes* is *there are only finitely many primes*.
- Negation of *every number is even* is *at least one number is not even*.

Both illustrate that to negate a universal claim you produce one exception — this is the "one bad apple" that module 1.11 will make formal.

The reverse direction is less familiar and just as useful. To negate *there exists a thing with this property*, you must rule out every candidate — which is a universal claim, and so needs a genuine argument rather than a single example. Disproving an existence claim is the harder of the two jobs, and knowing which one you are facing tells you what kind of work to expect.
