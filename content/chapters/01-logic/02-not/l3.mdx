Every statement has a partner called its **negation**. If the statement is true, its negation is false. If the statement is false, its negation is true. They can never both be true, and they can never both be false.

This is the first tool in the course, and it is used constantly. Many proofs work by showing that the negation of what you want cannot possibly be true — and if the negation is impossible, the original must hold, because there is nothing else for it to be.

We usually make the negation of a sentence by inserting the word *not*.

- Statement: *The number $12$ is even.* True.
- Negation: *The number $12$ is not even.* False.

- Statement: *$7$ is a multiple of $3$.* False.
- Negation: *$7$ is not a multiple of $3$.* True.

## Negating a comparison

Say a ride has a height rule: **you must be $48$ inches tall or taller.**

What is the negation? It is: **you are shorter than $48$ inches.**

Look hard at what happens at exactly $48$ inches. Someone exactly $48$ tall *does* meet the rule. So they must **not** meet the negation — and they don't, because $48$ is not shorter than $48$.

<Warning title="Watch the exact number">
The negation of *"$48$ or taller"* is *"shorter than $48$"* — not *"$48$ or shorter."*

Test it at exactly $48$. Someone exactly $48$ inches tall would meet **both** the rule and *"$48$ or shorter."* Both cannot be true at once, so that cannot be the negation.

Every statement and its negation split everyone into two groups, with nobody in both and nobody left out. **Check the exact boundary number** and you will catch this mistake every time.
</Warning>

## Negating twice

Negating flips the truth value. So negating *twice* flips it back to where it started.

- *The number $12$ is even.* True.
- *The number $12$ is not even.* False.
- *It is not true that $12$ is not even.* True again.

The third sentence is clumsy to say, but it means exactly the same thing as the first. Two nots cancel. English does not always work this way — "I didn't see nothing" usually means you saw nothing — but in mathematics the rule has no exceptions.

## Negating quantities

- The negation of *there are infinitely many primes* is *there are only finitely many primes*.
- The negation of *every number is even* is *at least one number is not even*.

Notice a pattern: the negation of "all of them" is not "none of them" — it's "at least one is not." One counterexample is enough. Module 1.11 will use this idea a lot.

## Why "none of them" is wrong

Take a shelf holding some red books and some green ones.

- *Every book is red.* False — the green ones say so.
- *No book is red.* Also false — the red ones say so.

Both are false at the same time, and a statement and its negation can never do that. So they are not negations of each other. The real negation of *every book is red* is *at least one book is not red*, and on this shelf that is true.

**The negation of a statement is not its opposite extreme.** The opposite extreme claims much more, and claiming more is exactly what you must not do when you are trying to knock a statement down. One exception is all you need, and one exception is all you should claim.
