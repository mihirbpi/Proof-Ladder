*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\wedge$ reads "and", $\vee$ reads "or", $\neg$ reads "not", $\equiv$ reads "is equivalent to".*

You can deny a statement, and you can join two statements with *and* or *or*. What happens when you do both?

The answer is not "negate each piece and leave the connective alone." Negation does not slide quietly through a connective — it changes it on the way.

## The laws

$$
\neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as". So the line reads: **not (p and q)** says the same as **(not p) or (not q)**; **not (p or q)** says the same as **(not p) and (not q)**.*

$$
\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

In one sentence: **a negation distributes over a connective, but swaps $\wedge$ and $\vee$ as it goes.**

## The argument in words

The table proves the laws but does not explain them. Here is the reason, and this version is the one that survives when the laws reappear for sets and for quantifiers.

Ask how much work it takes to *break* each kind of claim.

**To break "both hold"**, you need only knock down one piece. One failure suffices — and "at least one fails" is exactly $\vee$.

**To break "at least one holds"**, one failure is not enough; the other piece might still carry the claim. You must knock down every piece — and "all fail" is exactly $\wedge$.

Breaking is easy where holding is hard, and hard where holding is easy. The swap is the bookkeeping of that trade.

Note that "at least one fails" is inclusive: possibly both do. That is why the right side of the first law uses $\vee$ and not exclusive or.

## Duality

The two laws are not independent. Put the denials $\neg p$ and $\neg q$ into the first law in place of $p$ and $q$, then use that denying twice returns you to the start:
$$
\neg(\neg p \wedge \neg q) \equiv p \vee q,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

and negating both sides gives the second law.

So negation is an **involution** — an operation that undoes itself — and it **exchanges** $\wedge$ and $\vee$. Each law is the other one seen through a mirror, and proving one proves both.

<Warning title="Do not keep the connective">
Denying "both hold" does **not** give "both fail".

Check row 2 of the table: $p$ true, $q$ false. The left side is T, the right side is F.

Keeping the connective makes the negation **too strong** — it claims both parts failed, when the negation only entitles you to one. This is the single most common error with these laws, and it is invisible unless you check a row.

A second trap is scope. A denial attaches to as little as possible, so without brackets it lands on the first piece only — not on the whole *and*. When a negation is meant to govern a whole compound, the parentheses are not optional.
</Warning>

## Why this matters: negating a hypothesis

Almost every theorem has a compound hypothesis. To prove one by contrapositive (module 1.8) you must negate that hypothesis correctly — and a mis-negation means an afternoon spent proving the wrong statement.

Consider:

> If $x$ is rational and $y$ is irrational, then $x+y$ is irrational.

Writing the rationals for the set of rational numbers, so that $x$ rational says *$x$ is rational*, the hypothesis is a conjunction and De Morgan applies:
$$
\neg\big(x \in \mathbb{Q} \wedge y \notin \mathbb{Q}\big) \equiv x \notin \mathbb{Q} \vee y \in \mathbb{Q}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and".*

The contrapositive is therefore:

> If $x + y$ is rational, then $x$ is irrational **or** $y$ is rational.

That is strictly weaker, and therefore easier, than the naive "and" version.

### Proving an *or*

Assume the negation of one disjunct and derive the other. Here: assume $x+y \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $x$ rational; then $y = (x+y)-x$ is a difference of rationals, hence rational — the remaining disjunct.

And an *or* in a **hypothesis** forces proof by cases instead (module 1.12). So De Morgan does not merely restate a claim; it tells you what shape the proof will have.

## Generalisation

The laws iterate. Denying a long chain of *and*s turns every one of them into *or*: at least one link of the chain has to fail. Denying a long chain of *or*s turns every one into *and*: every link has to fail. Both follow from the two-statement case above, applied repeatedly (Chapter 6 makes "repeatedly" precise).

Now let the chain run forever. A *for all* claim is an endless chain of *and*s, and a *there exists* claim is an endless chain of *or*s — so the same swap applies, and denying *"every one works"* gives *"at least one fails."* Those are the quantifier rules of module 1.11, and they are not an analogy: they are this module's laws with the chain made infinite.

<Aside>
The laws will appear a third time in module 2.7, for sets: the outside of an overlap is the union of the two outsides, and the outside of a union is the overlap of the two outsides. Taking the outside of a set plays the part of denial, overlap plays *and*, and union plays *or*.

One law, three costumes. Learning to expect the swap is learning to predict the shape of an argument before writing it.
</Aside>
