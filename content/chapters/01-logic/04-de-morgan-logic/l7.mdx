Negation does not simply pass through the binary connectives untouched. That single fact, stated carefully, is De Morgan's laws, and it is the reason that negating a real hypothesis is a step worth slowing down for.

## Logical equivalence

Two statements $p, q$ built from the same atomic statements are **logically equivalent**, written $p \equiv q$, when they receive the same truth value under every assignment of truth values to the atoms. Equivalently: their truth-table columns coincide.

Two consequences worth stating explicitly:

- $p \equiv q$ if and only if $p \Leftrightarrow q$ is a tautology — true under every assignment (§1.3). The relation $\equiv$ lives in the metalanguage; $\Leftrightarrow$ lives inside the object language. In practice mathematicians blur the two, and no harm comes of it, but the distinction is real.
- Equivalence licenses **substitution**: if $p \equiv q$, then replacing an occurrence of $p$ inside a larger statement by $q$ produces an equivalent statement. This is what makes rewriting a hypothesis a legitimate move in a proof.

## The laws

$$
\neg(p \wedge q) \;\equiv\; \neg p \vee \neg q,
\qquad
\neg(p \vee q) \;\equiv\; \neg p \wedge \neg q.
$$

### The argument in words

It is worth also seeing the argument in words, because that is the version that generalises to sets and to quantifiers:

> Suppose $p \wedge q$ fails. Then it is not the case that both hold, so at least one fails — which is precisely $\neg p \vee \neg q$. Conversely, if at least one of $p, q$ fails, then certainly not both hold. The two conditions describe the same situations, hence the same truth value.

Note that "at least one fails" is an *inclusive* claim: possibly both do. That is why the right-hand side uses $\vee$ and not exclusive or.

## Duality

The two laws are not independent facts; each follows from the other by negating and using $\neg \neg p \equiv p$. Substituting $\neg p, \neg q$ for $p, q$ in the first law and negating both sides:
$$
\neg(\neg p \wedge \neg q) \equiv \neg\neg p \vee \neg \neg q \equiv p \vee q,
$$
and negating gives $\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q$. So negation is an involution that exchanges $\wedge$ and $\vee$ — an operation that preserves the structure but flips it. This exchange is called *De Morgan duality*, and it holds in every structure where the laws hold.

## The application that motivates the module

The point of De Morgan's laws in a proof course is negating hypotheses. Consider the following, which the contrapositive method (§1.8) will need:

> **Proposition.** If $x$ is rational and $y$ is irrational, then $x + y$ is irrational.

To prove this by contrapositive we need the negation of the conclusion implying the negation of the hypothesis. The hypothesis is a conjunction, so:
$$
\neg\big(x \in \mathbb{Q} \;\wedge\; y \notin \mathbb{Q}\big) \;\equiv\; x \notin \mathbb{Q} \;\vee\; y \in \mathbb{Q}.
$$
The contrapositive therefore reads: *if $x + y$ is rational, then $x$ is irrational or $y$ is rational.* That is a strictly weaker — and therefore easier — target than "$x$ is irrational and $y$ is rational," which is what a careless negation produces.

<Warning title="Two failures worth naming">
**Over-negation.** $\neg(p \wedge q) \not\equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q$. Row 2 of the table separates them. The wrong version asserts that both conjuncts failed; the negation only entitles you to one.

**Scope errors.** $\neg p \wedge q$ and $\neg(p \wedge q)$ are different statements with different columns. Since $\neg$ binds more tightly than $\wedge$, the unparenthesised $\neg p \wedge q$ means $(\neg p) \wedge q$. When a negation is meant to govern a whole compound, the parentheses are not optional.
</Warning>

## The general form

For any number of statements the laws iterate:
$$
\neg(p_1 \wedge p_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge p_n) \equiv \neg p_1 \vee \neg p_2 \vee \cdots \vee \neg p_n,
$$
$$
\neg(p_1 \vee p_2 \vee \cdots \vee p_n) \equiv \neg p_1 \wedge \neg p_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge \neg p_n,
$$
by induction on $n$ (Chapter 6 supplies the induction; the base case $n = 2$ is what we proved). Now let the list run on without end — replacing the chain of *and*s by "for all $x$" and the chain of *or*s by "there exists $x$" — and you get exactly the quantifier negation rules of §1.11:
$$
\neg \forall x\, p(x) \equiv \exists x\, \neg p(x), \qquad \neg \exists x\, p(x) \equiv \forall x\, \neg p(x).
$$
Universal quantification really is an infinitary conjunction, and existential quantification an infinitary disjunction. The analogy is not decorative; it is the reason the rules look the same.

<Aside>
Augustus De Morgan published these in the 1840s, in the same wave of work that gave us Boole's algebra of logic. The laws themselves were known to medieval logicians — William of Ockham states both — but De Morgan gave them symbolic form, which is what made them usable inside calculations rather than only inside arguments.
</Aside>

De Morgan's laws are what you will use to say precisely how a calculus hypothesis fails.

**Discontinuity.** A function is continuous at $a$ when the left limit, the right limit, and $f(a)$ all exist **and** agree. Negate that conjunction and you get a disjunction — the classification of discontinuities you meet in the first weeks of calculus. Removable, jump, and infinite are the branches of $\neg(p\wedge q\wedge r)$, and the reason there are exactly three kinds is that there are exactly three conjuncts.

**Failure of the Mean Value Theorem.** The hypothesis is *continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$*. Its negation is *not continuous on $[a,b]$ **or** not differentiable somewhere in $(a,b)$* — and every standard counterexample exhibits exactly one of the two disjuncts, never both. Knowing the negation is an *or* tells you a single failure point suffices.

In each case the swap is doing the same work: it converts "the theorem does not apply" into a usable list of alternatives, and it tells you in advance that the proof will need cases (§1.12).
