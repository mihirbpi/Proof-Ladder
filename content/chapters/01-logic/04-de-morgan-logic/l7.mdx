Negation does not simply pass through *and* and *or* untouched. That single fact, stated carefully, is De Morgan's laws, and it is the reason that negating a real hypothesis is a step worth slowing down for.

## Logical equivalence

Two statements $p, q$ built from the same atomic statements are **logically equivalent**, written $p \equiv q$, when they receive the same truth value under every assignment of truth values to the atoms. Equivalently: their truth-table columns coincide.

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\neg$ reads "not", $\wedge$ reads "and", $\vee$ reads "or". The quantifiers arrive later in the module: $\forall$ reads "for all", $\exists$ reads "there exists", and $\Leftrightarrow$ reads "if and only if".*

Two consequences worth stating explicitly:

- $p \equiv q$ if and only if $p \Leftrightarrow q$ is a tautology — true under every assignment (§1.3). The symbol $\equiv$ is a remark *about* two statements; $\Leftrightarrow$ is part of a statement itself. In practice mathematicians blur the two, and no harm comes of it, but the distinction is real.
- Equivalence licenses **substitution**: if $p \equiv q$, then replacing an occurrence of $p$ inside a larger statement by $q$ produces an equivalent statement. This is what makes rewriting a hypothesis a legitimate move in a proof.

## The laws

$$
\neg(p \wedge q) \;\equiv\; \neg p \vee \neg q,
\qquad
\neg(p \vee q) \;\equiv\; \neg p \wedge \neg q.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as". So the line reads: denying an *and* gives an *or* of the denials, and denying an *or* gives an *and* of them.*

### The argument in words

It is worth also seeing the argument in words, because that is the version that generalizes to sets and to quantifier (§1.10: the *for every* and *there is* words)s:

> Suppose $p \wedge q$ fails. Then it is not the case that both hold, so at least one fails — which is exactly $\neg p \vee \neg q$. In the other direction, if at least one of $p, q$ fails, then certainly not both hold. The two conditions describe the same situations, so the same truth value.

Note that "at least one fails" is an *inclusive* claim: possibly both do. That is why the right-hand side uses $\vee$ and not exclusive or.

## Each law is the other in a mirror

The two laws are not independent facts; each follows from the other by negating and using $\neg \neg p \equiv p$. Substituting $\neg p, \neg q$ for $p, q$ in the first law and negating both sides:
$$
\neg(\neg p \wedge \neg q) \equiv \neg\neg p \vee \neg \neg q \equiv p \vee q,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

and negating gives $\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q$ — *denying “$p$ or $q$” is claiming that neither one holds*. So negation undoes itself and exchanges $\wedge$ and $\vee$ — it keeps the structure and flips it. Each law is the other one seen in a mirror, and the mirroring holds wherever the laws do.

## The application that motivates the module

The point of De Morgan's laws in a proof course is negating hypotheses. Consider the following, which the contrapositive method (§1.8) will need:

> **Proposition.** If $x$ is rational and $y$ is irrational, then $x + y$ is irrational.

To prove this by contrapositive (§1.7: swap the two halves and deny both) we need the negation of the conclusion implying the negation of the hypothesis. The hypothesis is a conjunction, so:
$$
\neg\big(x \in \mathbb{Q} \;\wedge\; y \notin \mathbb{Q}\big) \;\equiv\; x \notin \mathbb{Q} \;\vee\; y \in \mathbb{Q}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and".*

The contrapositive (§1.7: swap the two halves and deny both) therefore reads: *if $x + y$ is rational, then $x$ is irrational or $y$ is rational.* That is a strictly weaker — and therefore easier — target than "$x$ is irrational and $y$ is rational," which is what a careless negation produces.

<Warning title="Two failures worth naming">
**Over-negation.** $\neg(p \wedge q) \not\equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q$ — *denying that both hold is not the same as claiming both fail*. Row 2 of the table separates them. The wrong version says that both halves failed; the negation only entitles you to one.

**Scope errors.** A denial with no brackets and a denial governing a whole compound are different statements with different columns. Negation binds more tightly than the joining words, so without brackets it lands on the first piece only. When a negation is meant to govern a whole compound, the parentheses are not optional.
</Warning>

## The general form

For any number of statements the laws iterate:
$$
\neg(p_1 \wedge p_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge p_n) \equiv \neg p_1 \vee \neg p_2 \vee \cdots \vee \neg p_n,
$$

*So the line reads: denying that all of them hold is the same as saying at least one of them fails — however many there are.*

$$
\neg(p_1 \vee p_2 \vee \cdots \vee p_n) \equiv \neg p_1 \wedge \neg p_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge \neg p_n,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

by induction on $n$ (Chapter 6 supplies the induction; the base case $n = 2$ is what we proved). Now let the list run on without end — replacing the chain of *and*s by "for all $x$" and the chain of *or*s by "there exists $x$" — and you get exactly the quantifier negation rules of §1.11:
$$
\neg \forall x\, p(x) \equiv \exists x\, \neg p(x), \qquad \neg \exists x\, p(x) \equiv \forall x\, \neg p(x).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\neg$ is "not", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

Universal quantification really is an endless chain of *ands*, and *there exists* an endless chain of *ors*. The analogy is not decorative; it is the reason the rules look the same.

<Aside>
Augustus De Morgan published these in the 1840s, in the same wave of work that gave us Boole's algebra of logic. The laws themselves were known to medieval logicians — William of Ockham states both — but De Morgan gave them symbolic form, which is what made them usable inside calculations rather than only inside arguments.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

De Morgan's laws are what you will use to say exactly how a calculus hypothesis fails.

**Breaks in a graph.** A curve is unbroken at a point when three things all hold and agree. Negate that *and* and you get an *or* — which is exactly the list of ways a graph can break that you meet in the first weeks. The number of kinds is the number of conditions, and that is not a coincidence: it is De Morgan.

**Failure of a hypothesis, generally.** Every theorem ahead of you has a hypothesis built from *and*s. Its failure is therefore an *or*, and each branch of that *or* is one standard counterexample. The counterexamples in a textbook are not a grab-bag — they are the branches of a disjunction, enumerated.

In each case the swap is doing the same work: it converts "the theorem does not apply" into a usable list of alternatives, and it tells you in advance that the proof will need cases (§1.12).

</WhereThisGoes>
