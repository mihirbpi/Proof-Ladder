Indirect proof — the kind your geometry course runs when a direct argument stalls — begins by assuming the *opposite* of what you want. When what you want is a compound statement, negating it correctly is not obvious, and getting it wrong wastes the whole proof.

This module gives the rule.

## The laws

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\neg$ reads "not", $\wedge$ reads "and", $\vee$ reads "or", $\equiv$ reads "is equivalent to".*

$$
\neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q
$$

*So the line reads: denying that both hold is the same as saying at least one of them fails.*

$$
\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as". So the line reads: denying *either one* says the same as *both fail**

In one sentence: **a negation distributes over a joining word, but swaps $\wedge$ and $\vee$ as it goes.**

## The argument in words

The table proves the laws but does not explain them. Here is the version that survives when the laws reappear for sets and quantifiers (§1.10: the *for every* and *there is* words).

Ask what it takes to *break* each kind of claim.

**To break "both hold"**, you need only knock down one piece. One failure is enough — and "at least one fails" is $\vee$.

**To break "at least one holds"**, one failure is not enough; the other piece might still carry the claim. You must knock down every piece — and "all fail" is $\wedge$.

Breaking is easy where holding is hard. The swap is the bookkeeping of that trade.

## Where this bites in geometry

Take a theorem with a compound hypothesis — congruence criteria all have one:

> If $\overline{AB}\cong\overline{DE}$ **and** $\angle B \cong \angle E$ **and** $\overline{BC}\cong\overline{EF}$, then $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF$.

Now suppose you want the contrapositive (module 1.7), because you know the triangles are *not* congruent and want to conclude something about the parts.

Negating the hypothesis correctly gives:

> $\overline{AB}\not\cong\overline{DE}$ **or** $\angle B\not\cong\angle E$ **or** $\overline{BC}\not\cong\overline{EF}$.

**At least one part fails, and you do not get to say which.** That is a much weaker — and much more useful — conclusion than "all three fail," which is what a careless negation produces and which is simply not implied.

<Warning title="Do not keep the joining word">
$$\neg(p \wedge q) \;\not\equiv\; \neg p \wedge \neg q.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\equiv$ is "says the same as". So the line reads: denying *both* is **not** the same as *both fail* — the crossed-out sign marks the tempting step that is wrong*

Check row 2 of the table: $p$ true, $q$ false. The left side is T, the right is F.

Keeping the joining word makes the negation **too strong**. Applied to the triangle example, it would claim all three correspondences fail when only one has to — and you would spend the proof trying to prove something the hypothesis never gave you.

A second trap is scope. Since $\neg$ binds more tightly than $\wedge$, the unparenthesized $\neg p \wedge q$ means $(\neg p)\wedge q$, **not** $\neg(p\wedge q)$. When a negation is meant to govern a whole compound, the parentheses are not optional.
</Warning>

## The *or* in a hypothesis means cases

De Morgan does more than restate a claim — it tells you what shape the proof will take.

An *or* in a **hypothesis** forces proof by cases (module 1.12): you must handle each half of the *or*, because you do not get to choose which one holds. So when contraposing a conjunctive hypothesis, you can predict in advance that the resulting proof will have branches.

That is exactly why indirect proofs in geometry so often split: *"Case 1: the lines meet. Case 2: the lines are parallel."*

## Negating a theorem statement

Here is the procedure, applied to something from your book.

> **Theorem.** If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, then its opposite sides are congruent **and** its opposite angles are congruent.

To run an indirect proof of this you negate the conclusion. The conclusion is a conjunction, so its negation is a disjunction:

> its opposite sides are **not** congruent, **or** its opposite angles are not congruent.

**At least one fails, and you are not told which.** So the indirect proof will have two cases, and you must handle both — one where the sides fail, one where the angles do.

A student who negates carelessly writes *"neither pair is congruent"* and then proves something about that. It is a true-sounding sentence, it is strictly stronger than the negation, and a contradiction derived from it proves nothing about the theorem.

**The negation is the only assumption you are entitled to.** Anything stronger is smuggling in a hypothesis.

## Nested compounds

Apply the laws one layer at a time, from the outside in.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\neg\big(p \wedge (q\vee r)\big) &\equiv \neg p \vee \neg(q\vee r)\\
&\equiv \neg p \vee (\neg q \wedge \neg r).
\end{aligned}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", the bar on top is "everything outside", $\equiv$ is "says the same as". So the line reads: push the denial inward one layer at a time, swapping *and* with *or* at each step.*

Each pass moves the $\neg$ one level deeper and swaps one joining word. Nothing is guessed, and the parentheses track which joining word the negation is currently passing.

Reading the result: *the conjunction fails when $p$ fails, or when both $q$ and $r$ fail.* Check it against the original and it is plainly right — but it is not something most people produce correctly without the rules.

## Each law is the other in a mirror

The two laws are not independent. Substituting $\neg p, \neg q$ for $p, q$ in the first and using $\neg\neg p \equiv p$:
$$
\neg(\neg p \wedge \neg q) \equiv p \vee q,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as". So the line reads: denying that both fail says the same as *at least one holds**

and negating both sides gives the second law.

So negation is an operation that **exchanges** $\wedge$ and $\vee$. Each law is the other seen in a mirror, and proving one proves both.

<Aside>
The laws will appear twice more in this course, in near-identical form.

Module 1.11 gives them for quantifiers: $\neg\forall$ becomes $\exists\neg$, and $\neg\exists$ becomes $\forall\neg$. That is the same law with infinitely many terms, since "for all" is a giant *and* and "there exists" a giant *or*.

Module 2.7 gives them for sets: $\overline{S \cap T} = \overline S \cup \overline T$, with complement playing $\neg$.

One law, three costumes. Learning to expect the swap is learning to predict the shape of an argument before writing it.
</Aside>
