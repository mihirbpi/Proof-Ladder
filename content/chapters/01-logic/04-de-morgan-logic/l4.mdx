*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\neg$ reads "not", $\wedge$ reads "and", $\vee$ reads "or".*

You can negate a statement, and you can join two statements with $\wedge$ or $\vee$. The question this module answers is what happens when you do both: how do you correctly compute $\neg(p \wedge q)$?

The answer is not "$\neg p \wedge \neg q$." Negation does not slide quietly through a connective. It changes the connective on the way.

## Logical equivalence

First, the tool for saying two statements are the same. Two statements are **logically equivalent**, written $\equiv$, when their truth-table columns match in every row. Equivalent statements are interchangeable: if you have proved one, you have proved the other.

Note that $\equiv$ is a claim *about* statements, not a connective inside one.

## The first law

$$\neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q$$

The last two columns agree in all four rows. That *is* the proof — a truth-table verification is a complete proof of a logical equivalence, because four rows exhaust every possibility.

## The second law

$$\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q$$

  ]}
/>

Together these are **De Morgan's laws**. In one sentence: *a negation distributes over a connective, but it swaps $\wedge$ and $\vee$ as it goes.*

## A worked negation

Let $p$: "$21$ is a multiple of $4$" and $q$: "$21$ is a multiple of $5$."

The claim $p \vee q$ says $21$ is a multiple of $4$ or of $5$. It is false. De Morgan tells us what its falsity amounts to:
$$
\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q,
$$
that is: $21$ is not a multiple of $4$ **and** $21$ is not a multiple of $5$. Both. Check: $21 = 4 \cdot 5 + 1$ and $21 = 5 \cdot 4 + 1$. Neither divides. Correct.

Now the other one. $p \wedge q$ says $21$ is a multiple of both. Its negation is $\neg p \vee \neg q$: $21$ fails at least one of them. That is a weaker claim than "$21$ fails both," and weaker is right — the negation should demand no more than it has to.

<Warning title="Do not over-negate">
The single most common error with De Morgan is negating both parts and keeping the connective:
$$
\neg(p \wedge q) \;\not\equiv\; \neg p \wedge \neg q.
$$
Check row 2 of the first table: $p$ true, $q$ false. The left side is T, the right side is F. Different columns, so not equivalent. Keeping the connective makes the negation *too strong* — it claims both parts failed when only one had to.
</Warning>

## Why this matters more than it looks

Almost every theorem you will meet has a compound hypothesis: "if $n$ is even **and** $n > 2$, then …". To prove such a theorem by contrapositive (module 1.8) you must negate that hypothesis — and if you negate it wrongly, you will spend an afternoon proving the wrong statement. De Morgan is the rule that keeps you honest:
$$
\neg(\text{$n$ even} \wedge n > 2) \equiv (\text{$n$ odd}) \vee (n \leq 2).
$$
An *or* in the hypothesis means the proof will need **cases** — and that is the shape almost every contrapositive proof of a conjunctive hypothesis takes.
