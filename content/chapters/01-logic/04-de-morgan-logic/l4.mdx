*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\neg$ reads "not", $\wedge$ reads "and", $\vee$ reads "or".*

You can deny a statement, and you can join two statements with *and* or *or*. This module answers what happens when you do both: **what does it take for "both $p$ and $q$" to fail?**

Not "both fail." Denial does not slide quietly through a joining word — it changes the joining word on the way.

## Logical equivalence

First, the tool for saying two statements are the same. Two statements are **logically equivalent**, written $\equiv$, when their truth-table columns match in every row. Equivalent statements are interchangeable: if you have proved one, you have proved the other.

Note that $\equiv$ is a claim *about* statements, not a joining word inside one.

## The first law

**For "both $p$ and $q$" to fail, at least one of them must fail.** In symbols:

$$\neg(p \wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as". So the line reads: **not (p and q)** says the same thing as **(not p) or (not q)**.*

The last two columns agree in all four rows. That *is* the proof — a truth-table verification is a complete proof of a logical equivalence, because four rows exhaust every possibility.

## The second law

**For "$p$ or $q$" to fail, both must fail.** In symbols:

$$\neg(p \vee q) \equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\vee$ is "or", $\equiv$ is "says the same as". So the line reads: **not (p or q)** says the same thing as **(not p) and (not q)**.*

Together these are **De Morgan's laws**. In one sentence: *a negation distributes over a joining word, but it swaps $\wedge$ and $\vee$ as it goes.*

## A worked negation

Let $p$: "$21$ is a multiple of $4$" and $q$: "$21$ is a multiple of $5$."

"$p$ or $q$" says $21$ is a multiple of $4$ or of $5$. It is false. The second law says what that failure amounts to: $21$ is not a multiple of $4$ **and** $21$ is not a multiple of $5$. Both. Check: $21 = 4 \cdot 5 + 1$ and $21 = 5 \cdot 4 + 1$. Neither divides. Correct.

Now the other one. "$p$ and $q$" says $21$ is a multiple of both. Denying it says $21$ fails **at least one** of them. That is a weaker claim than "$21$ fails both," and weaker is right — a denial should demand no more than it has to.

<Warning title="Do not over-negate">
The single most common error with De Morgan is negating both parts and keeping the joining word — reading the denial of "both hold" as "both fail".

Check row 2 of the first table: $p$ true, $q$ false. The left side is T, the right side is F. Different columns, so not equivalent. Keeping the joining word makes the negation *too strong* — it claims both parts failed when only one had to.
</Warning>

## Reading the laws in everyday language

The two laws are not mathematical curiosities; you already use them, and hearing them in ordinary sentences makes them hard to forget.

Suppose a sign reads **"you must be tall enough and have a ticket."** What does it take to be turned away? Not failing both — failing *either* is enough. Denial of an *and* is an *or*.

Now a sign reading **"we accept cash or card."** What does it take for a payment to be refused? It must fail on cash **and** fail on card. One failure is not enough. Denial of an *or* is an *and*.

Notice how the strength moves. The first denial is easy to satisfy — one failure does it. The second is hard — everything must fail. That is exactly the pattern the truth tables recorded, and it is why the joining word has to flip: keeping it would make one denial too strong and the other too weak.

## A test you can run on yourself

Here is a check worth doing whenever a denial feels uncertain. Take the statement, imagine the single cheapest way for it to fail, and see whether your denial covers that case.

For *"$n$ is even and $n > 2$"*, the cheapest failure is $n = 3$: odd, but comfortably above $2$. A denial reading *"$n$ is odd and $n \leq 2$"* does not cover $n = 3$ — so that denial is wrong, and the cheapest case exposed it in one line.

For *"$n$ is even or $n > 2$"*, the cheapest failure has to defeat both halves at once, and $n = 1$ does it. A denial reading *"$n$ is odd or $n \leq 2$"* would count $n = 4$ as a failure, which it plainly is not.

One well-chosen number settles it either way, and choosing the number is faster than staring at the symbols.

## Why this matters more than it looks

Almost every theorem you will meet has a compound hypothesis: "if $n$ is even **and** $n > 2$, then …". To prove such a theorem by contrapositive (module 1.8) you must deny that hypothesis — and if you deny it wrongly, you will spend an afternoon proving the wrong statement. De Morgan keeps you honest: the denial is *$n$ is odd **or** $n \leq 2$*, not both at once.

An *or* in the hypothesis means the proof will need **cases** — and that is the shape almost every contrapositive (§1.7: swap the two halves and deny both) proof of a conjunctive hypothesis takes.
