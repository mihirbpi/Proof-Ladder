You know how to negate one statement (module 1.2). You know how to join two statements with *and* and *or* (module 1.3). Now: what happens when you negate a joined statement?

The answer is a surprise, and it is worth remembering for the rest of your life in mathematics.

## Negating an *and*

Take a true-looking claim about $60$:

> $60$ is a multiple of $4$ **and** $60$ is a multiple of $5$.

Both parts are true, so this is true. Now take a claim about $21$:

> $21$ is a multiple of $4$ **and** $21$ is a multiple of $5$.

This is false. But *why* is it false? Because $21$ is not a multiple of $4$ — and, as it happens, also not a multiple of $5$. Either failure alone would have been enough.

So when a claim "both of these hold" fails, what do we know? Only that **at least one part failed**. We are not told which:

> **not** ($A$ and $B$)  is the same as  (**not** $A$) **or** (**not** $B$)

## Negating an *or*

Now the other direction:

> $21$ is a multiple of $4$ **or** $21$ is a multiple of $5$.

This is false too. For an *or* claim to fail, you cannot get away with one failure — you need **both** parts to fail. And they do: $21$ is not a multiple of $4$, and $21$ is not a multiple of $5$.

> **not** ($A$ or $B$)  is the same as  (**not** $A$) **and** (**not** $B$)

## The pattern

These two rules are called **De Morgan's laws**, after Augustus De Morgan, who wrote them down in the 1840s. Here is the whole thing in one sentence:

> Putting "not" in front of a joined statement pushes the "not" onto each piece **and swaps the joining word**.

*And* becomes *or*. *Or* becomes *and*. Every single time.

<Warning title="The mistake almost everyone makes first">
The negation of "$x$ is even **and** $x$ is big" is **not** "$x$ is odd **and** $x$ is small." That is far too strong a claim — it says both things went wrong. The correct negation is "$x$ is odd **or** $x$ is small": at least one went wrong, and you don't get to say which.
</Warning>

## Why the swap makes sense

Think about how much work it takes to *break* each kind of claim.

- To break *both of these hold*, you only need to knock down one piece. Easy. One failure is enough — that is exactly what *or* means.
- To break *at least one of these holds*, one failure is not enough; the other piece might still be carrying the claim. You have to knock down every piece. That is exactly what *and* means.

Breaking is easy where holding is hard, and hard where holding is easy. The swap is the arithmetic of that trade.
