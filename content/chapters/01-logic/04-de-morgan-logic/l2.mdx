Sam has a going-outside checklist:

- [ ] hat
- [ ] coat

The rule is: *Sam has the hat **and** Sam has the coat.*

## Crossing out an AND

Now suppose that rule is **not** true. Something on the checklist is unchecked. Which one?

We do not know! It could be the hat. It could be the coat. It could be both.

So the opposite of *hat **and** coat* is:

> *no hat **or** no coat.*

Look what happened. The **and** turned into an **or**.

## Crossing out an OR

Now try a different rule. *Sam has the hat **or** Sam has the coat.* Sam only needs one to make this true.

How could that be false? Sam would have to be missing the hat **and** missing the coat. Both gone. Nothing on the list.

So the opposite of *hat **or** coat* is:

> *no hat **and** no coat.*

The **or** turned into an **and**!

<Warning title="The swap always happens">
**Crossing out** a telling with AND in it gives you OR. Crossing out a telling with OR in it gives you AND. The little word always flips. It never stays put.
</Warning>

This is one of the most useful rules in all of math, and you just learned it with a hat.

<TryIt>
Say: *I have shoes and I have a jacket.* Now say the opposite, with an OR in it. Then say: *I have shoes or I have a jacket*, and say that opposite too.
</TryIt>
