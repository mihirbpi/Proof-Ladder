Four tasks, four templates. Which one you need is decided entirely by the outermost quantifier and by whether you are proving or refuting.

| Goal | Method |
|---|---|
| Prove $\forall x \in S$, the property holds | **take any one and follow it** — argue about a single $x \in S$, assuming nothing about it |
| Disprove $\forall x \in S$, the property holds | **one bad apple** — exhibit a $c \in S$ where it fails |
| Prove $\exists x \in S$, the property holds | exhibit a **witness** $c \in S$ where it holds |
| Disprove $\exists x \in S$, the property holds | show it fails for every $x \in S$ |

The two right-hand cells in the disproof column are exactly the negation laws of module 1.11. Nothing new is being asserted; the table just names the work.

## Task 1: the stand-in element

To prove that every $x$ in $S$ has a property, introduce a **stand-in element** of $S$ — a specific object, fixed for the duration of the argument, about which you assume *nothing beyond membership in $S$*. Then prove the property of it. Since the argument used no special feature, it applies unchanged to every element.

> **Proposition.** For every integer $n \geq 3$, the integer $n^2 - 1$ is composite.

("Composite" means: expressible as a product of two integers each greater than $1$.)

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">an integer $n$, $n \geq 3$. Nothing else about $n$.</div>
  <div slot="want">A factorization $n^2 - 1 = ab$ with $a, b > 1$.</div>
  <div slot="how">$n^2 - 1$ is a difference of squares, so it factors as $(n-1)(n+1)$ identically. Then check both factors exceed $1$, which is where the hypothesis $n \geq 3$ is spent.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $n$ be any integer with $n \geq 3$.

By the difference of squares identity, $n^2 - 1 = (n-1)(n+1)$.

Since $n \geq 3$, we have $n - 1 \geq 2 > 1$ and $n + 1 \geq 4 > 1$. Then $n^2 - 1$ is a product of two integers each greater than $1$, so composite.

Nothing was assumed about $n$ $\geq 3$, the claim holds for all such $n$.
</Proof>

Note the hypothesis $n \geq 3$ is not decoration: at $n = 2$ the factorization gives $1 \cdot 3$, and $3$ is prime. Universal claims fail at their boundaries, and the boundary is where you should always test.

<Warning title="Arbitrary means unchosen">
"Take any $n$" and "let $n = 5$" are different openings. The moment your proof says something about $n$ that was not given — that it is even, that it is positive, that it is not $0$ — the element has stopped standing in for all the others, and you have proved a restricted claim. If you genuinely need such an assumption, you must split into cases and handle the others.
</Warning>

## Task 2: counterexample

> **Claim.** Every prime is odd.

$2$ is prime and even. Refuted. By the negation law, exhibiting one failure *is* a proof of the negation, so this is a complete disproof rather than evidence.

For claims of the form *for every $x$, if the hypothesis holds then so does the conclusion*, the counterexample must satisfy the hypothesis and violate the conclusion — module 1.11 derived this from $\neg(p \Rightarrow q) \equiv p \wedge \neg q$ — denying *if $p$ then $q$* means asserting $p$ and denying $q$.

## Task 3: witness

> **Claim.** $\exists! x \in \mathbb{R}$ with $3x - 1 = 0$ — *exactly one real number $x$ satisfies $3x-1=0$*.

<Proof>
*Existence.* Take $x = \tfrac13$; then $3 \cdot \tfrac13 - 1 = 0$.

*Uniqueness.* Suppose $a$ and $b$ are real numbers both satisfy the equation. Then $3a - 1 = 0 = 3b - 1$, so $3a = 3b$, and dividing by $3$ gives $a = b$.
</Proof>

The uniqueness half has a fixed shape worth memorizing: **suppose two objects both have the property, then prove they are equal.** Not "suppose there are two different ones and get a contradiction" — that works too, but the direct version is shorter and does not need contradiction.

## Task 4: disproving an existential, and proof by cases

> **Claim.** $\exists x \in \mathbb{R}$ with $x^2 < 0$ — *some real number has a negative square*.

To refute this we must prove $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, x^2 \geq 0$ — *every real number squares to at least zero* — a universal claim, so back to task 1. But no single argument covers all reals here, so we **split into cases**.

<Proof>

*The symbols in this proof: $\forall$ is "for every", $\in$ is "is in", $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers. In words, what follows shows: every real number has a square that is zero or bigger.*

Let $x$ be any real number. Exactly one of $x > 0$, $x = 0$, $x < 0$ holds.

*Case 1: $x > 0$.* A product of two positive reals is positive, so $x^2 > 0$.

*Case 2: $x = 0$.* Then $x^2 = 0$.

*Case 3: $x < 0$.* Then $-x > 0$, and $x^2 = (-x)(-x)$ is a product of two positives, so positive.

In every case $x^2 \geq 0$. The cases are exhaustive, so $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, x^2 \geq 0$, and therefore no real $x$ satisfies $x^2 < 0$.
</Proof>

**Proof by cases** is licensed by any split covering every case — here, that every integer is even or odd. Two things to check: the cases must **cover** the domain, and each must be proved separately.

<Aside>
Where does a disjunction to split on come from? Usually from an *or* in a hypothesis, or from a known split like even/odd. This is why module 1.3's inclusive *or* matters: "$p$ or $q$" hands you two cases and you must prove the goal in both.
</Aside>
