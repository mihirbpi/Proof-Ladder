You have two kinds of quantified claim and two things you might want to do with each. That is four jobs, and each has its own method.

|  | To prove | To disprove |
|---|---|---|
| **For all** cases, … | take any one and follow it | one bad apple |
| **There exists** a case where … | show one example | show every case fails |

## The new move: take any one and follow it

Proving a *for all* claim looks impossible — there are usually infinitely many cases. The trick is to argue about **one** case, chosen so badly that the argument works for all of them.

You pick a case you know *nothing special* about. Mathematicians have a word for a choice made that way: they call it **arbitrary**. It does not mean random — it means you gave yourself no extra information to work with. Then you reason. If your reasoning never uses a special fact about your choice, it would have worked for any other choice too — so it works for all of them.

## A worked example

> **Claim.** Take any counting number from $3$ upwards. Multiply it by itself, then subtract $1$. The answer always splits into two factors, both bigger than $1$.

Check two. Start with $3$: $9-1=8=2\times4$ ✓. Start with $5$: $25-1=24=4\times6$ ✓.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Our number is a counting number, at least $3$. Nothing else.</div>
  <div slot="want">Two factors of the answer, each bigger than $1$.</div>
  <div slot="how">There is a pattern in the examples. For $3$ the factors were $2$ and $4$ — one below and one above. For $5$ they were $4$ and $6$ — again one below and one above. So try showing that the answer is always *(one less)* times *(one more)*.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Take any counting number from $3$ upwards, and follow it. We know nothing else about it.

Picture a square whose side is your number. Shorten one side by $1$ and lengthen the other by $1$. You lose a strip and gain a strip, and the strips differ by exactly one tile.

So *(one less)* times *(one more)* is exactly one less than your number multiplied by itself — exactly the answer we were asked about.

Now the two factors. Your number is at least $3$, so *one less* is at least $2$ ✓, and *one more* is at least $4$ ✓. Both beat $1$.

And our number was any counting number from $3$ up, so this holds for all of them.
</Proof>

Notice we never used a particular number. Every step would hold starting from $7$, or $400$, or a number nobody has written down. That is what makes it a proof of the *for all* claim.

<Warning title="Arbitrary means you don't get to choose">
A proof that begins "start with $5$" proves something about $5$. To prove a *for all* claim you must let the case be handed to you.

If you catch yourself saying "and since our number is even…", stop — you assumed something you were not given, unless you also handle the odd case. See below.
</Warning>

## Splitting into cases

Sometimes you cannot argue about all cases the same way. Then you may **split into cases**, as long as your cases cover everything.

> **Claim.** Take any counting number and multiply it by the next one up. The answer is always even.

<Proof>
Take any counting number and follow it. Either it is even or it is odd — those are the only options.

**Case 1: it is even.** Then it is a pile of $2$s, and copies of a pile of $2$s stay a pile of $2$s. Even ✓

**Case 2: it is odd.** Then the next one up is even — a pile of $2$s — and the same reasoning applies. Even ✓

There are no other cases, so the answer is always even.
</Proof>

The rule for cases: they must **cover everything**. Leave a case out and your proof has a hole in it.

## The other three jobs

- **Disprove a *for all*:** one bad apple. "Every rectangle has four equal sides" dies to a $2$-by-$5$ rectangle.
- **Prove a *there exists*:** show one. "There is a shape with four equal sides and four square corners" — a square. Done.
- **Disprove a *there exists*:** show *every* case fails — a *for all* proof in disguise. "There is a counting number bigger than all the others" is false: whatever you name, adding one beats it.

<Aside>
Three of the four jobs have a hard version and an easy version, and the hard ones are hard for the same reason: they are secretly *for all* claims, and *for all* claims cannot be settled by looking.
</Aside>
