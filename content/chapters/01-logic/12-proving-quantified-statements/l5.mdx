Modules 1.10 and 1.11 fixed what quantified claims mean and how they negate. This one turns that into four templates. Which you reach for is decided entirely by two things: the outermost quantifier, and whether you are proving or refuting.

|  | To prove | To disprove |
|---|---|---|
| $\forall x \in S,\ p(x)$ | arbitrary element | one counterexample |
| $\exists x \in S,\ p(x)$ | exhibit a witness | prove $\forall x,\ \neg p(x)$ |

The two cheap boxes are the diagonal. The two expensive ones are the other diagonal, and they are expensive for the same reason: both are secretly universal claims, and universal claims cannot be settled by looking.

## Template 1: the arbitrary element

To prove $\forall x \in S,\ p(x)$, take an **arbitrary element** of $S$ — one handed to you, about which you assume nothing beyond membership — and derive $p(x)$.

> **Proposition.** For every integer $n \geq 3$, the number $n^2 - 1$ is composite.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$n$ is an integer, $n \geq 3$, and nothing else.</div>
  <div slot="want">Two factors of $n^2 - 1$, each greater than $1$ — that is what *composite* demands.</div>
  <div slot="how">$n^2 - 1$ is a difference of squares, so it factors on sight. The work is then checking that both factors clear $1$, which is where $n \geq 3$ gets used.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $n$ be an arbitrary integer with $n \geq 3$. Factoring,
$$
n^2 - 1 = (n-1)(n+1).
$$
Both factors are integers. Since $n \geq 3$ we have $n - 1 \geq 2 > 1$, and $n + 1 \geq 4 > 1$.

Then $n^2 - 1$ is a product of two integers each exceeding $1$, so composite. Since $n$ could have been any of them, this holds for every integer $n \geq 3$.
</Proof>

The proof never used a particular $n$. Every line would read identically starting from $7$, or $400$, or a number nobody has written down — **and that is the whole force of the method.** One argument, infinitely many cases.

<Warning title="Arbitrary means handed to you">
The hypothesis $n \geq 3$ may be used. Nothing else may.

If you find yourself writing "and since $n$ is even…", stop: nobody said $n$ was even. Either drop the assumption or split into cases and handle both.

A proof that opens "let $n = 5$" is a proof about $5$.
</Warning>

## Template 2: exhibit a witness

To prove $\exists x \in S,\ p(x)$, produce one element and check it. A **witness** is that element.

> **Proposition.** $\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\ x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0$ — *some real number $x$ makes $x^2-5x+6$ equal to zero*.

<Proof>
Take $x = 2$. Then $4 - 10 + 6 = 0$ ✓, and $2 \in \mathbb{R}$.
</Proof>

Two lines, and it is complete. You need not say where $2$ came from, whether you factored or guessed, or whether other solutions exist. **A witness needs to be exhibited and verified, not motivated.**

## Template 3: one counterexample

Module 1.11 covered this: to disprove a universal claim, produce a single failing element.

> *Every quadratic with integer coefficients has integer roots.*

$x^2 - 2 = 0$ has roots $\pm\sqrt2$. Done.

## Template 4: prove the universal negation

To disprove $\exists x \in S,\ p(x)$ you must rule out every candidate — which by module 1.11 is the universal claim $\forall x \in S,\ \neg p(x)$ — *no $x$ in $S$ satisfies $p$* — and so needs Template 1.

> **Proposition.** There is no real $x$ with $x^2 < 0$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Every real number is negative, zero, or positive — one of exactly three, and no fourth option exists.</div>
  <div slot="want">$x^2 \geq 0$ for every real $x$.</div>
  <div slot="how">No single algebraic step covers all three, since the sign rule for products differs by case. So **split into cases** and check that they exhaust the possibilities.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $x$ be an arbitrary real number. Exactly one of the following holds.

*Case 1: $x > 0$.* A product of two positives is positive, so $x^2 > 0$.

*Case 2: $x = 0$.* Then $x^2 = 0$.

*Case 3: $x < 0$.* A product of two negatives is positive, so $x^2 > 0$.

In every case $x^2 \geq 0$. The cases exhaust the real numbers, and $x$ was arbitrary, so no real number has $x^2 < 0$.
</Proof>

**Splitting into cases** is the named move here, and it carries one thing to check: the cases must **cover everything**. Leave a case out and the proof has a hole exactly where the missing case lived.

## Uniqueness, as a reusable pattern

$\exists!\, x \in S,\ p(x)$ is two jobs (module 1.10), and the second always has the same shape:

> **Suppose $a$ and $b$ both satisfy $p$. Show $a = b$.**

Learn it as one line, because it never varies. What varies is only the algebra in the middle.

> **Proposition.** A linear equation $mx + c = 0$ with $m \neq 0$ has exactly one real solution.

<Proof>
**Existence.** $x = -c/m$ is defined since $m \neq 0$, and $m(-c/m) + c = -c + c = 0$ ✓.

**Uniqueness.** Suppose $ma + c = 0$ and $mb + c = 0$. Subtracting, $m(a - b) = 0$. Since $m \neq 0$, we may divide, giving $a - b = 0$, so $a = b$.
</Proof>

Note where $m \neq 0$ was used **twice**, and differently: to build the witness, and to license the division. A hypothesis earning its keep in two distinct places is a good sign the statement is stated tightly.

<Aside>
Uniqueness proofs feel strange the first time because they never search. You do not look for a second solution and report failure — failing to find something is a fact about your search.

Instead you assume two exist and squeeze them together. Your geometry course runs the same argument for the midpoint of a segment: assume two midpoints, then show the assumption forces them to be the same point.
</Aside>
