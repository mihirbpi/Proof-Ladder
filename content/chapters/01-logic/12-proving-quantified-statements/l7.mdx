This module converts the negation laws of §1.11 into working method. Four tasks; each has a template determined by the outermost quantifier and the direction of the goal.

| Goal | Template |
|---|---|
| Prove $\forall x \in S,\, p(x)$ | take an $x \in S$ about which **nothing is assumed**; prove $p(x)$ |
| Refute $\forall x \in S,\, p(x)$ | exhibit a **counterexample** |
| Prove $\exists x \in S,\, p(x)$ | exhibit a **witness**, or argue non-constructively |
| Refute $\exists x \in S,\, p(x)$ | prove $\forall x \in S,\, \neg p(x)$ |

## The arbitrary-element rule, and why it is sound

The rule behind the first row is the **arbitrary-element rule**: from a proof of $p(c)$ in which $c$ is a constant subject to no hypothesis specific to it, infer $\forall x,\, p(x)$.

The side condition is the whole content, and the two words pull in opposite directions:

- **Fixed**, because you must be able to compute with $c$ as a definite object — write $c^2$, apply lemmas to it, name quantities depending on it.
- **Arbitrary**, because your derivation may use only facts that follow from $c \in S$. The moment you use a property not shared by all of $S$, generalization becomes unsound.

The soundness argument is exactly this: a derivation of $p(c)$ using only $c \in S$ is, syntactically, a template. Substituting any other $d \in S$ for $c$ throughout gives a derivation of $p(d)$. So $p$ holds everywhere on $S$.

> **Proposition.** For all an integer $n$ with $n \geq 3$, $n^2 - 1$ is composite.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">an integer $n$, $n \geq 3$; nothing further.</div>
  <div slot="want">$n^2 - 1 = ab$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, $a, b > 1$.</div>
  <div slot="how">$n^2 - 1 = (n-1)(n+1)$ is an identity in the integers, valid for every $n$; the hypothesis $n \geq 3$ is needed only to force both factors above $1$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $n$ be any integer with $n \geq 3$. Then
$$n^2 - 1 = (n-1)(n+1),$$
an identity in the integers. Since $n \geq 3$, we have $n - 1 \geq 2$ and $n + 1 \geq 4$, so both factors are integers exceeding $1$. So $n^2 - 1$ is composite. Since $n$ could have been any of them subject only to $n \geq 3$, the claim holds universally on that domain.
</Proof>

The hypothesis is sharp: at $n = 2$ the factorisation reads $1 \cdot 3$, and $3$ is prime. Universal statements characteriztically fail at the boundary of their domain, which is the first place to test any conjecture.

<Warning title="The arbitrary element loses its status silently">
Every one of these breaks generalization, and none of them looks like an error locally:

- assuming a parity ("since $n$ is even, $n/2 \in \mathbb{Z}$") that was never given;
- dividing by an expression without establishing it is nonzero;
- taking a square root and writing $\sqrt{x^2} = x$, which assumes $x \geq 0$;
- choosing $c$ to make a step convenient.

If a case assumption is genuinely needed, it must be introduced as one branch of an exhaustive case split, with the other branches proved too.
</Warning>

## Existential proofs: constructive and otherwise

Proving $\exists x \in S,\, p(x)$ by exhibiting a **witness** is the constructive route and is always preferable when available. It is not always available. Two standard non-constructive patterns — routes that prove existence without producing the object:

- **Contradiction.** Assume $\forall x \in S,\, \neg p(x)$ — *that nothing in $S$ satisfies $p$* — and derive a contradiction. This proves existence without producing anything.
- **Pigeonhole / counting.** Show the set of $x$ with $p(x)$ is nonempty by counting, without ever naming a member of it.

The classic illustration: *there exist irrational $a, b$ with $a^b$ rational.* Consider $\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}}$. Either it is rational — take $a = b = \sqrt2$ — or it is irrational, in which case take $a = \sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$, $b = \sqrt2$, so that $a^b = \sqrt2^{\,2} = 2$. Either way such a pair exists, and the proof does not tell you which pair. Excluded middle (every statement is either true or false, §1.2) is doing the work; a constructivist rejects the argument, though the theorem itself has constructive proofs by other routes.

## Uniqueness

$\exists! x \in S,\, p(x)$ requires two independent arguments.

<Proof>
*Existence.* $x = \tfrac13$ satisfies $3x - 1 = 0$.

*Uniqueness.* Suppose $a$ and $b$ are real numbers satisfy $3a - 1 = 0$ and $3b - 1 = 0$. Then $3(a - b) = 0$, and dividing by $3$ gives $a - b = 0$, so $a = b$.
</Proof>

The uniqueness template is *suppose two objects have the property; prove they are equal* — preferable to *suppose two distinct objects and derive a contradiction*, which needs contradiction for no gain. Note also where the argument actually leaned: on the fact that a product of non-zero reals is never zero. In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ the equation $3x = 3$ has three solutions and the identical manipulation fails at that step. Uniqueness proofs are diagnostic of the surrounding structure.

## Proof by cases

Licensed by any exhaustive disjunction: if $p_1 \vee \cdots \vee p_k$ holds and each $p_i \Rightarrow q$, then $q$.

> **Proposition.** $\neg \exists x \in \mathbb{R}$ with $x^2 < 0$ — *no real number has a negative square*.

<Proof>
We prove the equivalent universal statement $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, x^2 \geq 0$ — *every real number squares to at least $0$*. Let $x$ be any real number. Every real is positive, zero, or negative — exactly one of $x > 0$, $x = 0$, $x < 0$ holds.

If $x > 0$, then $x^2 > 0$ as a product of positives. If $x = 0$, then $x^2 = 0$. If $x < 0$, then $-x > 0$ and $x^2 = (-x)(-x) > 0$.

In all cases $x^2 \geq 0$; the cases are exhaustive. So no real number has negative square.
</Proof>

Two things to check: **exhaustiveness** (a missing case is a hole, and it is the most common structural defect in student proofs) and **each branch actually proved**. Disjointness is tidiness, not required. The disjunction to split on typically comes from a hypothesis containing $\vee$, from the positive/zero/negative split, from even versus odd, or from the division algorithm — the last generalizing parity to remainders mod $d$ (§4.4).

<Aside>
The matching rule for *there exists* runs the other way and has its own side condition. From $\exists x,\, p(x)$ you may introduce a **fresh** constant $c$ with $p(c)$ — fresh meaning it appears nowhere earlier. Reusing a name is the classic fallacy: from $\exists x, x^2 = 4$ and $\exists x, x^3 = 27$, using the same $c$ for both would give a $c$ with $c^2 = 4$ and $c^3 = 27$. Each existential gets its own name.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

These four templates are the complete strategic vocabulary for quantified goals, and they do not change in the courses ahead — only the difficulty of filling them in does.

**The limit proofs you will write are the *there exists* template.** *Let a tolerance be given* opens a *for all*; *here is the window that meets it* discharges a *there exists*; *now check it works* is the verification. Nothing in the shape is new. What is new is the algebra of finding the window, which is scratch work, not proof.

**Some existence theorems produce nothing.** Calculus has several results of the form *somewhere on this stretch, such a point exists* — a place where a curve crosses a value, or where its slope matches the average slope across the stretch. They guarantee a point and give you no way to find it. That is a genuine and slightly unsettling feature: the proof shows the thing must be there without ever locating it.

**Proof by cases** appears wherever an absolute value or a piecewise definition does, and the thing to check is the one stated above: the cases must be exhaustive, and each must be proved separately.

</WhereThisGoes>
