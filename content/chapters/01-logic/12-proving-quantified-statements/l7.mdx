This module converts the negation laws of §1.11 into working method. Four tasks; each has a template determined by the outermost quantifier and the direction of the goal.

| Goal | Template |
|---|---|
| Prove $\forall x \in S,\, p(x)$ | fix an **arbitrary but fixed** $x \in S$; prove $p(x)$ |
| Refute $\forall x \in S,\, p(x)$ | exhibit a **counterexample** |
| Prove $\exists x \in S,\, p(x)$ | exhibit a **witness**, or argue non-constructively |
| Refute $\exists x \in S,\, p(x)$ | prove $\forall x \in S,\, \neg p(x)$ |

## Universal generalisation, and why it is sound

The rule behind the first row is **universal generalisation**: from a proof of $p(c)$ in which $c$ is a constant subject to no hypothesis specific to it, infer $\forall x,\, p(x)$.

The side condition is the whole content. "Arbitrary but fixed" is a genuinely two-sided phrase:

- **Fixed**, because you must be able to compute with $c$ as a definite object — write $c^2$, apply lemmas to it, name quantities depending on it.
- **Arbitrary**, because your derivation may use only facts entailed by $c \in S$. The moment you use a property not shared by all of $S$, generalisation becomes unsound.

The soundness argument is exactly this: a derivation of $p(c)$ using only $c \in S$ is, syntactically, a template. Substituting any other $d \in S$ for $c$ throughout yields a derivation of $p(d)$. So $p$ holds everywhere on $S$.

> **Proposition.** For all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $n \geq 3$, $n^2 - 1$ is composite.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$n \in \mathbb{Z}$, $n \geq 3$; nothing further.</div>
  <div slot="want">$n^2 - 1 = ab$ with $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$, $a, b > 1$.</div>
  <div slot="how">$n^2 - 1 = (n-1)(n+1)$ is an identity in $\mathbb{Z}$, valid for every $n$; the hypothesis $n \geq 3$ is needed only to force both factors above $1$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ be arbitrary with $n \geq 3$. Then
$$n^2 - 1 = (n-1)(n+1),$$
an identity in $\mathbb{Z}$. Since $n \geq 3$, we have $n - 1 \geq 2$ and $n + 1 \geq 4$, so both factors are integers exceeding $1$. Hence $n^2 - 1$ is composite. As $n$ was arbitrary subject only to $n \geq 3$, the claim holds universally on that domain.
</Proof>

The hypothesis is sharp: at $n = 2$ the factorisation reads $1 \cdot 3$, and $3$ is prime. Universal statements characteristically fail at the boundary of their domain, which is the first place to test any conjecture.

<Warning title="The arbitrary element loses its status silently">
Every one of these breaks generalisation, and none of them looks like an error locally:

- assuming a parity ("since $n$ is even, $n/2 \in \mathbb{Z}$") that was never given;
- dividing by an expression without establishing it is nonzero;
- taking a square root and writing $\sqrt{x^2} = x$, which assumes $x \geq 0$;
- choosing $c$ to make a step convenient.

If a case assumption is genuinely needed, it must be introduced as one branch of an exhaustive case split, with the other branches proved too.
</Warning>

## Existential proofs: constructive and otherwise

Proving $\exists x \in S,\, p(x)$ by exhibiting a **witness** is the constructive route and is always preferable when available. It is not always available. Two standard non-constructive patterns:

- **Contradiction.** Assume $\forall x \in S,\, \neg p(x)$; derive a contradiction. This establishes existence without producing anything.
- **Pigeonhole / counting.** Show the set of $x$ with $p(x)$ is nonempty by a cardinality argument.

The classic illustration: *there exist irrational $a, b$ with $a^b$ rational.* Consider $\sqrt{2}^{\sqrt{2}}$. Either it is rational — take $a = b = \sqrt2$ — or it is irrational, in which case take $a = \sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$, $b = \sqrt2$, so that $a^b = \sqrt2^{\,2} = 2$. Either way such a pair exists, and the proof does not tell you which pair. Excluded middle is doing the work; a constructivist rejects the argument, though the theorem itself has constructive proofs by other routes.

## Uniqueness

$\exists! x \in S,\, p(x)$ requires two independent arguments.

<Proof>
*Existence.* $x = \tfrac13$ satisfies $3x - 1 = 0$.

*Uniqueness.* Suppose $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ satisfy $3a - 1 = 0$ and $3b - 1 = 0$. Then $3(a - b) = 0$; since $\mathbb{R}$ is an integral domain and $3 \neq 0$, $a - b = 0$, so $a = b$.
</Proof>

The uniqueness template is *suppose two objects have the property; prove they are equal* — preferable to *suppose two distinct objects and derive a contradiction*, which needs contradiction for no gain. Note also where the argument actually leaned: on $\mathbb{R}$ having no zero divisors. In $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$ the equation $3x = 3$ has three solutions and the identical manipulation fails at that step. Uniqueness proofs are diagnostic of the ambient structure.

## Proof by cases

Licensed by any exhaustive disjunction: if $\varphi_1 \vee \cdots \vee \varphi_k$ holds and each $\varphi_i \Rightarrow \psi$, then $\psi$.

> **Proposition.** $\neg \exists x \in \mathbb{R}$ with $x^2 < 0$.

<Proof>
We prove the equivalent universal statement $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\, x^2 \geq 0$. Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary. By trichotomy exactly one of $x > 0$, $x = 0$, $x < 0$ holds.

If $x > 0$, then $x^2 > 0$ as a product of positives. If $x = 0$, then $x^2 = 0$. If $x < 0$, then $-x > 0$ and $x^2 = (-x)(-x) > 0$.

In all cases $x^2 \geq 0$; the cases are exhaustive. Hence no real number has negative square.
</Proof>

Two obligations: **exhaustiveness** (a missing case is a hole, and it is the most common structural defect in student proofs) and **completeness of each branch**. Disjointness is aesthetic, not required. The disjunction to split on typically comes from a hypothesis containing $\vee$, from trichotomy, from parity, or from the division algorithm — the last generalising parity to remainders mod $d$ (§4.4).

<Aside>
Existential instantiation is the dual rule and has its own side condition. From $\exists x,\, p(x)$ you may introduce a **fresh** constant $c$ with $p(c)$ — fresh meaning it appears nowhere earlier. Reusing a name is the classic fallacy: from $\exists x, x^2 = 4$ and $\exists x, x^3 = 27$, instantiating both to the same $c$ would yield a $c$ with $c^2 = 4$ and $c^3 = 27$. Each existential gets its own name.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
These four templates are the complete strategic vocabulary for quantified goals, and they do not change in later mathematics — only the difficulty of filling them in does. Universal generalisation and existential instantiation are formal inference rules of natural deduction, with the side conditions above stated syntactically as freshness constraints; get them wrong in a proof assistant and the system refuses the step, which is one of the more instructive experiences available to a mathematics student. The constructive/classical divide sharpens here too: in intuitionistic logic and in type theory under the Curry–Howard correspondence, a proof of $\exists x,\, p(x)$ *is* a pair consisting of a witness and a proof about it, so non-constructive existence proofs simply do not typecheck. That is the technical sense in which a classical existence theorem can be true and yet give you nothing you can compute.
</WhereThisGoes>
