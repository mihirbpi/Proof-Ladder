In module 1.7 you learned the good flip: swap the two parts and say *not* to both. That flip always means the same thing.

Here is why that is useful. If the flipped promise is easier to check, you can check that one instead. Same promise, less work. We call it a **backwards check**.

## A backwards check with numbers

> If two apples cannot be shared fairly between two people, then there was an odd number of apples.

Hmm. Starting from "cannot be shared fairly" is hard. Where do you even begin?

So flip it around the good way:

> If there was an even number of apples, then they can be shared fairly.

Oh! That one is easy. Even means you can make groups of two.

<Proof level="l2">
Say the number of apples is even. Even means you can put all the apples into pairs, with none left over.

Now hand one apple from each pair to each person. Everyone gets the same number. Nothing is left over.

So they were shared fairly.
</Proof>

We proved the flipped one. And because the flipped one always means the same thing as the first one, we proved the first one too. That is the whole trick.

<TryIt>
Get an even pile of things — 8 crayons, say. Share them into two equal groups. Now take one away and try again. What goes wrong? That is the odd case.
</TryIt>
