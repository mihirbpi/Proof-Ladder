Since $p \Rightarrow q \equiv \neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$ (§1.7), the two conditionals stand or fall together, and a proof of one is a proof of the other. **Proof by contrapositive** is the deliberate exercise of that choice. The interesting content of this module is not the equivalence — that is already proved — but the judgement of when to invoke it.

## The proposition

> **Proposition.** For all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, if $x^3 < 0$ then $x < 0$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Directly: the hypothesis $x^3 < 0$ tells us about a cube. To extract information about $x$ we would need the monotonicity of $t \mapsto t^{1/3}$ — which is either the proposition itself or a fact requiring its own proof. Factoring $x^3 = x \cdot x^2$ works but forces a case split on $x^2 = 0$ versus $x^2 > 0$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$x < 0$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Contrapose. The negation of the conclusion is $x \geq 0$ — a positive, usable hypothesis — and the negation of the hypothesis is $x^3 \geq 0$, reachable by two applications of the ordered-field fact that a product of non-negatives is non-negative.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
We prove the contrapositive: for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$, if $x \geq 0$ then $x^3 \geq 0$.

Let $x \geq 0$. Since $\mathbb{R}$ is an ordered field, the product of two non-negative elements is non-negative; applying this to $x$ and $x$ gives $x^2 \geq 0$, and applying it to $x^2$ and $x$ gives $x^3 \geq 0$.

Thus $x \geq 0 \Rightarrow x^3 \geq 0$. By contraposition, $x^3 < 0 \Rightarrow x < 0$, which is the proposition.
</Proof>

Observe what the switch bought: no case analysis, no appeal to the behaviour of cube roots, and a hypothesis ($x \geq 0$) that could be fed directly into a known lemma. That is the general pattern — contraposition trades an uninformative hypothesis for an informative one.

## When to contrapose

Three reliable indicators. Each is a statement about *usability of information*, which is the right way to think about proof strategy generally.

**1. The hypothesis is a negation.** "$x \notin \mathbb{Q}$", "$n$ is odd" (i.e. not even), "$f$ is not injective", "$S \neq \emptyset$". A negation licenses no substitution. You cannot write down what an irrational number *is*; you can only write down what it is not. Contraposition moves the negation to the conclusion, where "prove $\neg P$" is a manageable goal.

**2. The hypothesis concerns a composite quantity, the conclusion a simple one.** In "$n^2$ odd $\Rightarrow$ $n$ odd", the hypothesis constrains $n^2$; you want a constraint on $n$. Inverting the squaring is the difficulty. Flip and the hypothesis constrains $n$, from which $n^2$ is computed forwards.

**3. Both parts are negations.** Then the contrapositive has none, and is almost certainly easier.

Contraposition is not a default. When the hypothesis is already a positive existence statement ("there is a $k$ with $n = 2k$"), direct proof is shorter and reads better.

## The worked classic

> **Theorem.** For $n \in \mathbb{Z}$: $n^2$ is even $\iff$ $n$ is even.

This is a biconditional, so it needs two proofs (§1.9). One direction is direct; the other wants contraposition. That asymmetry is the whole lesson.

<Proof>
($\Rightarrow$) We prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is odd then $n^2$ is odd. Suppose $n$ is odd, so $n = 2k+1$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then
$$n^2 = 4k^2 + 4k + 1 = 2(2k^2 + 2k) + 1,$$
and $2k^2 + 2k \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $n^2$ is odd.

($\Leftarrow$) Directly: suppose $n$ is even, so $n = 2k$ with $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $n^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2)$ with $2k^2 \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $n^2$ is even.
</Proof>

The forward direction has hypothesis "$n^2$ even" — composite quantity, so indicator 2 applies, and contraposition converts it into the tractable "$n$ odd". The backward direction has hypothesis "$n$ even" — already ideal, so direct proof wins. Recognising which direction needs which is what a Discussion section is for.

This theorem is not an exercise: it is the lemma on which the irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$ turns (§4.7), and the contrapositive step here is the step that makes that proof possible.

<Warning title="Negations of inequalities and of quantified statements">
$\neg(x < 0)$ is $x \geq 0$, not $x > 0$. $\neg(x \leq 5)$ is $x > 5$. $\neg(\forall x\, P(x))$ is $\exists x\, \neg P(x)$ (§1.11), not $\forall x\, \neg P(x)$. A contrapositive built on a mis-negation is a proof of a different proposition — usually a strictly weaker one, which is why the error survives casual checking.
</Warning>

## Contraposition versus contradiction

These are routinely conflated. The distinction:

| | assume | derive | conclude |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contrapositive | $\neg q$ | $\neg p$ | $p \Rightarrow q$ |
| Contradiction | $p \wedge \neg q$ | some $r \wedge \neg r$ | $p \Rightarrow q$ |

Contradiction gives you a strictly larger set of assumptions to work with, which is occasionally decisive — but it costs clarity, because the reader must hold a false hypothesis in mind for the length of the proof, and because the contradiction reached may be far from where the trouble originated.

A useful self-check: write your contradiction proof, then look at whether the assumption $p$ was ever used. If it was not, you have written a contrapositive proof inside a contradiction wrapper. Remove the wrapper. Referees notice.

<Aside>
There is an asymmetry the table hides. $(p \Rightarrow q) \Rightarrow (\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p)$ holds in intuitionistic logic; the converse does not, since recovering $q$ from $\neg\neg q$ needs double-negation elimination. So "proof by contrapositive" in the sense used here — proving $\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$ in order to conclude $p \Rightarrow q$ — is a classical move. In practice this matters only if you care whether your proof yields an algorithm; but that is precisely what constructive mathematics and dependent type theory do care about.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
The judgement being trained here — *which form of a statement gives me the most usable hypothesis* — is the core skill of proof strategy, and it does not stop scaling. In analysis, the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ definition of continuity is routinely contraposed into the sequential characterisation because sequences are easier to manipulate than arbitrary neighbourhoods. In topology, "$f$ is continuous iff preimages of open sets are open" is preferred to the pointwise definition for exactly this reason, and its contrapositive form (preimages of closed sets are closed) is the version used in half of all applications. In algebra, the contrapositive of "if $R$ has no zero divisors then cancellation holds" is how integral domains get used in practice. The equivalence is trivial; knowing which side to stand on is the craft.
</WhereThisGoes>
