Module 1.7 established $p \Rightarrow q \equiv \neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$. Since the two are equivalent, proving either proves both.

**Proof by contrapositive** exercises that choice deliberately: when the hypothesis you are handed is awkward, prove the contrapositive instead. The named move is **flip it around**.

Your course calls a nearby technique *indirect proof*. The two are related and not identical, and the last section here sorts them out — the distinction is worth having, because textbooks blur it.

## First proposition

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\in$ reads "is in".*

> **Proposition.** For every real number $x$: if $x^3 < 0$ then $x < 0$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Directly, the hypothesis constrains $x^3$. To get information about $x$ we would have to invert the cubing — but "$x^3<0$ implies $x<0$" *is* the proposition, so invoking it is circular. Factoring $x^3 = x\cdot x^2$ works but needs a case split on whether $x^2 = 0$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$x < 0$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Contrapose. The negation of the conclusion is $x \geq 0$ — a positive, usable hypothesis — and the negation of the hypothesis is $x^3 \geq 0$, two steps away from the fact that a product of non-negatives is non-negative.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
We prove the contrapositive: if $x \geq 0$ then $x^3 \geq 0$.

Let $x \geq 0$. The product of two non-negative reals is non-negative, so $x^2 = x\cdot x \geq 0$, and then $x^3 = x^2 \cdot x \geq 0$.

Thus $x \geq 0 \Rightarrow x^3 \geq 0$. Since a conditional is logically equivalent to its contrapositive, the proposition follows.
</Proof>

Three lines, no cases. Note the discipline: **announce the switch in the first line and announce what it gives you in the last.** A reader who misses the announcement will think you proved the wrong statement.

<Warning title="Negate inequalities correctly">
$\neg(x<0)$ is $x \geq 0$, **not** $x > 0$. Writing $x>0$ silently drops the case $x=0$, and a proof that never handles it does not prove the proposition.

Likewise $\neg(x\leq5)$ is $x>5$, and $\neg(\ell_1 \parallel \ell_2)$ is *not* "$\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$ intersect" — in space they might be skew. A contrapositive built on a mis-negation proves a different, usually weaker, statement.
</Warning>

## A geometry proposition

> **Proposition.** If a triangle has no two congruent sides, then it has no two congruent angles.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Both hypothesis and conclusion are negative. Neither gives anything to construct with — "no two sides congruent" cannot be substituted into anything.</div>
  <div slot="want">No two angles congruent.</div>
  <div slot="how">Both parts are negations, so contraposing removes both: *if a triangle has two congruent angles, then it has two congruent sides.* That is a theorem you already have — the converse of the base-angle theorem.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
We prove the contrapositive: if a triangle has two congruent angles, then it has two congruent sides.

Suppose $\triangle ABC$ has $\angle B \cong \angle C$. By the converse of the base-angle theorem, the sides opposite those angles are congruent, so $\overline{AC}\cong\overline{AB}$.

Hence the triangle has two congruent sides. By contraposition, the proposition holds.
</Proof>

The contrapositive turned a statement about what a figure *lacks* into a statement about what it *has* — and the second kind connects to theorems, while the first does not.

## A decision rule

Reach for the contrapositive when:

**1. The hypothesis is a negation.** "$\ell_1$ is not parallel to $\ell_2$", "the triangle is not equilateral", "$x$ is not rational". A negation licenses no substitution.

**2. The hypothesis is about a composite quantity and the conclusion about a simple one.** "$n^2$ is even $\Rightarrow$ $n$ is even": the hypothesis constrains $n^2$, and inverting the squaring is the difficulty. Flipping puts the simple quantity where you can act on it.

**3. Both parts are negations.** Then the contrapositive has none — as in the triangle proposition above.

Otherwise try directly first. Contraposition is a tool, not a default.

## Contrapositive versus indirect proof

Your course probably uses *indirect proof* for both of these, but they differ:

| | assume | derive | conclude |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Contrapositive** | $\neg q$ | $\neg p$ | $p \Rightarrow q$ |
| **Contradiction** | $p$ **and** $\neg q$ | an impossibility | $p \Rightarrow q$ |

Contradiction gives you strictly more to work with — you keep the original hypothesis *and* the negated conclusion — which is occasionally decisive. It costs clarity, because the reader must carry a false assumption throughout.

**A self-check worth running.** Write your indirect proof, then look at whether you ever used the original hypothesis $p$. If you did not, you wrote a contrapositive proof inside a contradiction wrapper. Remove the wrapper; the proof gets shorter and clearer.

A great many textbook "indirect proofs" are contrapositive proofs in disguise, and spotting that is a genuine improvement in your writing.

<Aside>
Compound hypotheses need De Morgan (module 1.4) before contraposing. For a theorem with three congruence conditions, the contrapositive hypothesis is a single negation and the conclusion is a three-way *or* — strictly weaker than the naive conjunction, and provable where the conjunction is not.

Mis-negating here does not merely make the proof harder; it sets a target that does not follow from the hypothesis at all.
</Aside>
