Every indirect proof begins by assuming the opposite of the goal. Module 1.4 handled compound goals. This module handles quantified ones — and quantified statements are what your theorems actually are.

## The two rules

$$
\neg\big(\forall x \in S,\ P(x)\big) \;\equiv\; \exists x \in S,\ \neg P(x)
$$
$$
\neg\big(\exists x \in S,\ P(x)\big) \;\equiv\; \forall x \in S,\ \neg P(x)
$$

In words: **push the negation inward and flip the quantifier.** The domain $S$ never changes.

## Why they hold

A *for all* claim says the property holds throughout $S$. For that to fail, some element must fail — one is enough, and one is required. That is exactly a *there exists* claim about the denial.

A *there exists* claim says at least one element works. For that to fail, none can work — every element must fail. That is a *for all* claim about the denial.

<Aside>
These are De Morgan's laws from module 1.4, extended to infinitely many terms.

Over a finite domain $S = \{a, b, c\}$, a *for all* claim is just an **and**: the property holds at $a$, and at $b$, and at $c$. Denying it, by De Morgan, gives an **or** — the property fails at $a$, or at $b$, or at $c$ — which is a *there exists*. Same law, longer.

Over an infinite domain the conjunction cannot literally be written, but the rule survives unchanged. One law, wearing a third costume.
</Aside>

## The counterexample, explained

Here is the payoff. To refute
$$\forall x \in S,\ P(x)$$
you must prove its negation, which is
$$\exists x \in S,\ \neg P(x)$$
— an existential claim. And existentials are proved by producing a witness.

**That is what a counterexample is.** It is not a rhetorical move or a shortcut. It is a complete proof of the negation, in the form that the negation demands.

Two things follow, and both matter for your homework.

**One is enough, permanently.** A single valid counterexample settles the matter. There is nothing more to say, and finding a second adds no strength.

**It must be checked in full.** A counterexample must lie in the domain $S$ *and* satisfy $\neg P$. Both halves need verifying. Offering a right triangle as a counterexample to a theorem about acute triangles proves nothing about the theorem.

<Warning title="Do not negate the domain">
$$
\neg\big(\forall x \in S,\ P(x)\big) \quad\text{is}\quad \exists x \in S,\ \neg P(x)
$$
and **not** $\exists x \notin S,\ \neg P(x)$.

A counterexample must live inside the domain. Suppose a claim is made about all parallelograms and you produce a trapezoid where it fails. You have refuted nothing — the trapezoid was never covered by the claim.

This is the single most common error in refutations, and it is why the first sentence of any counterexample should verify membership: *"Let $ABCD$ be the parallelogram with vertices …"*
</Warning>

## Negating a theorem for indirect proof

> **Theorem.** If two lines are cut by a transversal so that alternate interior angles are congruent, then the lines are parallel.

Restore the quantifier and the conditional:
$$\forall \text{ line pairs } (\ell_1,\ell_2) \text{ with transversal } t:\ \big(\text{alt. int. congruent} \Rightarrow \ell_1 \parallel \ell_2\big).$$

To prove this indirectly, negate. First flip the quantifier:
$$\exists \text{ a pair}:\ \neg\big(\text{alt. int. congruent} \Rightarrow \ell_1\parallel\ell_2\big),$$
then use module 1.7: denying an if–then claim means the hypothesis holds and the conclusion fails.
$$\exists \text{ a pair}:\ \text{alt. int. congruent} \ \wedge\ \ell_1 \not\parallel \ell_2.$$

So the indirect proof opens: *"Suppose alternate interior angles are congruent but the lines are not parallel."* Then the lines meet, forming a triangle, and the Exterior Angle Theorem is contradicted.

Notice that the two negation rules had to be applied **in order, outermost first**. Getting that sequence right is the whole skill.

## Nested quantifiers

Apply the rules one at a time, working from the outside in. Each pass flips one quantifier and moves the $\neg$ one level deeper.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\neg\ \forall x\, \exists y\, \forall z\ P(x,y,z)\\
\equiv\ &\exists x\ \neg\ \exists y\, \forall z\ P(x,y,z)\\
\equiv\ &\exists x\, \forall y\ \neg\ \forall z\ P(x,y,z)\\
\equiv\ &\exists x\, \forall y\, \exists z\ \neg P(x,y,z).
\end{aligned}
$$

**Every quantifier flips, in place, and the order is preserved.** The negation is mechanical once you trust the two rules — which is exactly why the rules are worth memorising rather than re-deriving.

## Negating uniqueness

$\exists!$ is not a single quantifier, so it has no single flip rule. Unfold it first into its two halves — something works, and no two different things both work:
$$
\exists! x,\, P(x) \quad\equiv\quad \underbrace{\exists x,\, P(x)}_{\text{existence}} \ \wedge\ \underbrace{\forall x\, \forall y\, \big((P(x)\wedge P(y)) \Rightarrow x = y\big)}_{\text{uniqueness}}.
$$

Negating a conjunction gives a disjunction (module 1.4), so the negation is:

> **either** no $x$ satisfies $P$, **or** two distinct ones do.

That matches intuition — "not exactly one" means "none or several" — but writing it out is what tells you the refutation has *two* available routes. If one is hard, try the other.

## Checklist

When you are asked to negate a statement:

1. Restore every suppressed quantifier and domain.
2. Push $\neg$ inward one layer at a time, outermost first.
3. Flip each quantifier as the negation passes it; leave domains alone.
4. At the innermost level, negate the predicate — with De Morgan for $\wedge/\vee$ and $p \wedge \neg q$ for $\Rightarrow$.
5. Read the result aloud. If it does not sound like the opposite of what you started with, you dropped a step.
