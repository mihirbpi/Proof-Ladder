Module 1.10 gave you two kinds of telling. Now let's take the opposite of each one and see what happens.

## Knocking down an *every*

> **Every** animal in the picture has four legs.

To say the opposite, you do not say "no animal has four legs." That is much too strong! Most of them do.

The opposite is just:

> **Some** animal in the picture does not have four legs.

And to show that, you point at the duck. **One bad apple** and you are finished. You do not check the cow. You do not check the pig. One duck, done.

## Knocking down a *some*

> **Some** block in the box is purple.

Now flip it. What is the opposite? Not "some block is not purple" — there could be a purple one hiding under a green one.

The opposite is:

> **Every** block in the box is not purple.

And to show *that*, you have to pull out every single block and check it. All of them. No shortcuts.

## The pattern

The little word at the front always swaps.

| Telling | Its opposite |
|---|---|
| **Every** one works | **Some** one fails |
| **Some** one works | **Every** one fails |

<Warning title="Do not go too far">
The opposite of *every animal has four legs* is **not** *every animal does not have four legs*. That says all five animals are wrong, and only the duck is. The opposite only claims that **at least one** breaks the rule.
</Warning>

This swap is the same trick you saw in module 1.4, where crossing out an AND gave you an OR. It is the same rule wearing a different hat, and you will meet it a third time in chapter 2.

<TryIt>
Say: *every sock in the drawer is white.* Now say the opposite. Then go find one bad apple. If you cannot find one, the claim survives!
</TryIt>
