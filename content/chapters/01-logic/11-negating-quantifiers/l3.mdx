You can now build claims with *for all* and *there exists* (module 1.10). This module answers one question: what is the opposite of such a claim?

The answer is a rule you will use every time you try to knock something down.

## The opposite of a *for all*

> **Every** rectangle has four equal sides.

What would it take for this to be false? Not for every rectangle to have unequal sides — that is far too strong, and it is wrong anyway, since squares are rectangles with four equal sides. It would take just **one** rectangle without four equal sides.

> **Some** rectangle does not have four equal sides.

And there is one: a $2$-by-$5$ rectangle. The claim is dead.

A single example that kills a *for all* claim is called a **counterexample**, and the move is called **one bad apple**.

> The opposite of *for all cases, this holds* is *there is one case where it fails*.

## The opposite of a *there exists*

> **There exists** a counting number that is bigger than every other counting number.

What would it take for this to be false? It would take *every* counting number failing. Not one, not most — all of them.

> **For every** counting number, it is not bigger than every other counting number.

And that is true: whatever number you name, adding one gives a bigger one. So the original claim is false.

> The opposite of *there is a case where this holds* is *for all cases, it fails*.

## The rule in one line

> Taking the opposite **swaps the quantifier** and then says *not* to what comes after.

*For all* becomes *there exists*. *There exists* becomes *for all*. Every time.

If that feels familiar, it should. In module 1.4 you learned that saying "not" to an AND gives you an OR, and to an OR gives you an AND. This is the same law. A *for all* is a giant AND — "this one **and** that one **and** the next one, forever" — and a *there exists* is a giant OR. Same swap, bigger scale.

## More counterexamples

> **Every** prime number is odd.

Opposite: some prime number is not odd. And there is one — $2$ is prime and even. Dead.

> **Every** four-sided shape with four equal sides is a square.

Opposite: some four-sided shape with four equal sides is not a square. And there is one — a rhombus that has been squashed over, with four equal sides and no square corners. Dead.

That second one is worth pausing on, because it is a shape you can draw, and it is exactly the counterexample that keeps *square* and *four equal sides* from meaning the same thing.

<Warning title="The over-negation trap, again">
The opposite of "every prime is odd" is **not** "every prime is even." That is a rival claim, not the negation, and it happens to be false too — which means that if you disprove *it*, you have proved nothing about the original.

The negation of a *for all* is always a *there exists*. It claims one failure, not universal failure.
</Warning>

## Why counterexamples feel so good

There is a real asymmetry in the work.

- To prove *every rectangle has four equal sides*, you would need an argument covering infinitely many rectangles.
- To disprove it, you draw one $2$-by-$5$ rectangle. Done.

That is not laziness — it is exactly what the negation rule licenses. A *for all* claim is a promise about every case, so one broken case breaks the promise. This is why mathematicians, faced with a claim they suspect is false, go hunting for small examples first.

<Aside>
Counterexamples are not just for classroom exercises. In $1769$ Euler guessed that you can never add three fourth powers and get a fourth power, and more generally that adding $k$-th powers needs at least $k$ of them. The guess stood for $200$ years.

In $1966$, Lander and Parkin published a paper that was essentially one line long:
$$27^5 + 84^5 + 110^5 + 133^5 = 144^5.$$
One bad apple, and a two-century-old guess was over.
</Aside>
