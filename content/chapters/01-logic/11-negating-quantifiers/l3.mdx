You can now build claims with *for all* and *there exists* (module 1.10). This module answers one question: what is the opposite of such a claim?

The answer is a rule you will use every time you try to knock something down.

## The opposite of a *for all*

> **Every** prime number is odd.

What would it take for this to be false? Not for every prime to be even — that is far too strong, and obviously wrong since $3$, $5$ and $7$ are all odd primes. It would take just **one** prime that is not odd.

> **Some** prime number is not odd.

And there is one: $2$. It is prime, and it is even. The claim is dead.

A single example that kills a *for all* claim is called a **counterexample**, and the move is called **one bad apple**.

> The opposite of *for all $x$, $p(x)$* is *there exists $x$ with not $p(x)$*.

## The opposite of a *there exists*

> **There exists** a number $x$ with $x \times x = -1$.

(We are talking about ordinary numbers here — the ones on the number line. Chapter 5 will build new ones where this changes.)

What would it take for this to be false? It would take *every* number failing. Not one, not most — all of them.

> **For every** number $x$, $x \times x$ is not $-1$.

And that is true: a positive times a positive is positive, a negative times a negative is positive, and $0 \times 0 = 0$. No number squares to a negative. So the original claim is false.

> The opposite of *there exists $x$ with $p(x)$* is *for all $x$, not $p(x)$*.

## The rule in one line

> Taking the opposite **swaps the quantifier** and then negates what comes after.

*For all* becomes *there exists*. *There exists* becomes *for all*. Every time.

If that feels familiar, it should. In module 1.4 you learned that saying "not" to an AND gives you an OR, and to an OR gives you an AND. This is the same law. A *for all* is a giant AND — "this one **and** that one **and** the next one, forever" — and a *there exists* is a giant OR. Same swap, bigger scale.

<Warning title="The over-negation trap, again">
The opposite of "every prime is odd" is **not** "every prime is even." That is a rival claim, not the negation, and it happens to be false too — which means that if you disprove *it*, you have proved nothing about the original.

The negation of a *for all* is always a *there exists*. It claims one failure, not universal failure.
</Warning>

## Why counterexamples feel so good

There is a real asymmetry in the work.

- To prove *every prime is odd*, you would need an argument covering infinitely many primes.
- To disprove it, you say: "$2$." One character.

That is not laziness — it is exactly what the negation rule licenses. A universal claim is a promise about every case, so one broken case breaks the promise. This is why mathematicians, faced with a claim they suspect is false, go hunting for small examples first.

<Aside>
Counterexamples are not just for classroom exercises. In 1769 Euler conjectured that no fourth power is a sum of three fourth powers, and more generally that summing $k$-th powers needs at least $k$ terms. It stood for 200 years. In 1966 Lander and Parkin published a paper that was essentially one line long: $27^5 + 84^5 + 110^5 + 133^5 = 144^5$. One bad apple, and a two-century conjecture was over.
</Aside>
