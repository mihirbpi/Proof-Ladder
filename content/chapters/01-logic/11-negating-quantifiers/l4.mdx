Two rules, and they are the De Morgan laws of module 1.4 at infinite scale:

$$
\neg\big(\forall x \in S,\; x \text{ has the property}\big) \;\equiv\; \exists x \in S,\; x \text{ does not}
$$
$$
\neg\big(\exists x \in S,\; x \text{ has the property}\big) \;\equiv\; \forall x \in S,\; x \text{ does not}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\neg$ is "not", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

In words: **negation swaps the quantifier and negates the statement inside.** The domain $S$ is untouched.

## Why these are De Morgan

Over a small collection the two quantifiers stop being mysterious, because you can write them out in full.

Take $S = \{1, 2, 3\}$. Then *every member of $S$ is even* says exactly this:

> $1$ is even, **and** $2$ is even, **and** $3$ is even.

A *for every* claim is a string of **and**s. In the same way, *some member of $S$ is even* is a string of **or**s — $1$ is even, or $2$ is, or $3$ is.

Apply the first De Morgan law of module 1.4. The denial becomes "$1$ is not even **or** $2$ is not even **or** $3$ is not even" — which says exactly that *some* member of $S$ is not even. Same law, more terms.

## Counterexamples

> **Claim.** Every prime number is odd.

Its denial: *some* prime is not odd. A single example settles it — module 1.12 calls such an example a *witness* — and here it is $2$. Prime, and even. Claim refuted.

A single element refuting a universal claim is a **counterexample**, and the named move is **one bad apple**. Note what the negation rule guarantees: to refute a *for all* claim, exhibiting one $x$ where the property fails is not merely *good evidence* — it is a complete disproof, because the negation rule says that is exactly what the negation asserts.

## Compound conditions: use De Morgan inside too

> **Claim.** Every number on the line is positive or negative.

Negate:
First the quantifier rule: *not every real is positive or negative* becomes *some real is neither positive nor negative*.

Then De Morgan on the inside: *neither positive nor negative* means *not positive **and** not negative* — that is, $x \leq 0$ and $x \geq 0$ together.

So the negation asks for a real number that is both $\leq 0$ and $\geq 0$. There is exactly one: $x = 0$. The original claim is false, and $0$ is the counterexample.

Notice that we needed *two* rules in sequence: the quantifier rule to get past the *for all*, then De Morgan to get past the *or*. Negation is pushed inward one layer at a time.

## Negating a conditional inside a quantifier

Most theorems say *for every $x$ in $S$, if the hypothesis holds then so does the conclusion*. Since denying an if–then claim means the hypothesis holds while the conclusion fails (module 1.7), the negation is: **some $x$ in $S$ satisfies the hypothesis and fails the conclusion.**
That is the recipe for a counterexample. Not an element failing the hypothesis — such an element makes the conditional vacuously true and refutes nothing.

Example. *For every real $x$, if $x^2 > 1$ then $x > 1$* is false, and a counterexample has to satisfy $x^2 > 1$ **and** $x \leq 1$: take $x = -3$. Offering $x = 0.5$ would be useless — it fails the hypothesis.

<Warning title="Three standard errors">
**Over-negation.** The negation of *every $x$ has the property* is **not** *every $x$ lacks it*. "Not every prime is odd" ≠ "every prime is even."

**Negating the domain.** Negating a claim about every $x$ in the integers gives a claim about some $x$ in the integers — still over the integers. The set does not change.

**Wrong counterexample shape.** To refute an if–then claim you need the hypothesis to hold and the conclusion to fail. An element where both fail proves nothing.
</Warning>

## Nested quantifiers

Push the denial inward one quantifier at a time, flipping each as it passes:
$$
\neg\big(\forall x\, \exists y,\; x \text{ and } y \text{ fit}\big)
\equiv \exists x\, \neg\big(\exists y,\; x \text{ and } y \text{ fit}\big)
\equiv \exists x\, \forall y,\; x \text{ and } y \text{ do not fit}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\neg$ is "not", $\equiv$ is "says the same as". So the line reads: denying *for every $x$ there is a $y$ that fits* gives: there is some $x$ for which no $y$ fits — that is, some $x$ that fails to fit with every single $y$.*

Concretely: the denial of *every number has something you can add to it to get zero* is *some number has nothing that works* — and that inner *for every* is what says **nothing at all** works for that one number.

### Say the rules out loud

They stop looking like symbol-pushing the moment you do.

*Denying "everything has it" gives "something fails it."* If it is not true that every student passed, then some student did not — and you do not have to know which one.

*Denying "something has it" gives "everything fails it."* If it is not true that some student passed, then every student failed.

### A worked example: no largest prime

The procedure is mechanical, which is the point — you never have to be clever here, only careful. Take:
$$
\forall x \in \mathbb{Z}\;\; \exists y \in \mathbb{Z},\;\; \big(y > x \wedge y \text{ is prime}\big).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\wedge$ is "and". So the line reads: for every integer there is a bigger integer that is prime.*

That says there is no largest prime. To deny it: flip both quantifiers, then push the denial through the *and* using De Morgan:
$$
\exists x \in \mathbb{Z}\;\; \forall y \in \mathbb{Z},\;\; \big(y \leq x \vee y \text{ is not prime}\big).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\vee$ is "or". So the line reads: there is some integer such that every integer is either no bigger than it, or not prime.*

Read it back: there is some number past which nothing is prime — that is, a largest prime exists. Every mark in that denial was produced by the two rules of this module, plus De Morgan for *and* \neg q$. Nothing was invented.

<Aside>
Euler conjectured in 1769 that a $k$-th power is never a sum of fewer than $k$ positive $k$-th powers. In 1966 Lander and Parkin refuted the $k = 5$ case with a computer search and published a paper two sentences long, containing $27^5 + 84^5 + 110^5 + 133^5 = 144^5$. Two centuries of a universal claim, ended by one bad apple.
</Aside>
