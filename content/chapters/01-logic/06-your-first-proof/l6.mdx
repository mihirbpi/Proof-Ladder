You already write proofs. This module is about what a proof *is*, and about why the two-column format taught in geometry is scaffolding rather than the destination.

## What a proof is

Module 1.5 proved that $p \Rightarrow q$ is false in exactly one situation: $p$ true, $q$ false. Proving a conditional means ruling that situation out — and the direct way is to **assume $p$ and derive $q$**, after which the bad case cannot arise.

That is a **direct proof**, and the named move is **start from what you're given**.

A **proof** is an argument addressed to a reader. It must be complete enough that a skeptical reader who accepts your assumptions cannot escape your conclusion. Everything else — format, notation, whether you use a table — is presentation.

## The same proof, two ways

Here is a theorem from your course.

> **Theorem.** Vertical angles are congruent.

Specifically: two lines meet at a point, forming $\angle 1$ and $\angle 3$ opposite each other, with $\angle 2$ between them.

**Two-column:**

| Statement | Reason |
|---|---|
| 1. $\angle 1$ and $\angle 2$ form a linear pair | Given (from the figure) |
| 2. $\angle 2$ and $\angle 3$ form a linear pair | Given (from the figure) |
| 3. $m\angle 1 + m\angle 2 = 180°$ | Linear Pair Postulate |
| 4. $m\angle 2 + m\angle 3 = 180°$ | Linear Pair Postulate |
| 5. $m\angle 1 + m\angle 2 = m\angle 2 + m\angle 3$ | Substitution (3, 4) |
| 6. $m\angle 1 = m\angle 3$ | Subtraction Property |
| 7. $\angle 1 \cong \angle 3$ | Definition of congruent angles |

**Paragraph:**

<Proof>
Since $\angle 1$ and $\angle 2$ form a linear pair, $m\angle1 + m\angle2 = 180°$. Likewise $\angle 2$ and $\angle 3$ form a linear pair, so $m\angle2 + m\angle3 = 180°$.

Both sums equal $180°$, so they equal each other:
$$m\angle1 + m\angle2 = m\angle2 + m\angle3.$$
Subtracting $m\angle2$ from both sides gives $m\angle1 = m\angle3$, and equal measures mean the angles are congruent.
</Proof>

**These are the same proof.** Every statement in the table appears in the paragraph, and every reason appears too — carried by the words *since*, *likewise*, *so*, and *gives*. Nothing was dropped and nothing was added.

## Why mathematicians write the paragraph

Three reasons, and none of them is that tables are wrong.

**Reasons are usually obvious and stating them is noise.** "Subtraction Property of Equality" is a real justification, and no working mathematician writes it down, because every reader can supply it. A format that forces you to name every step buries the two or three steps that actually carry the argument.

**Prose can say things the table cannot.** *"This is the step where we use that the lines are distinct."* *"Note that we have not yet assumed the triangle is isosceles."* *"The same argument works for the other pair."* A reason column has nowhere to put any of that.

**Real theorems do not fit in a table.** As soon as a proof needs cases, or a construction, or "repeat the argument with $B$ and $C$ interchanged," the two-column format either breaks or becomes unreadable.

<Warning title="Paragraph proofs are not looser">
The paragraph is not a relaxed version of the table. It carries exactly the same things to check:

- every claim must follow from something already proved;
- every tool used must be one you are entitled to;
- side conditions must be checked.

What changes is only *which* justifications get written down. A paragraph proof that omits a **load-bearing** reason is wrong, exactly as a two-column proof with a blank reason cell is wrong.

The skill you are building is judgement about which reasons carry weight.
</Warning>

## Planning: the Discussion

Mathematicians plan before writing. Math 0's house style keeps the plan visible as a **Discussion** — what we know, what we want, what we'll do. It is scratch work made legible, and published proofs delete it.

> **Proposition.** The base angles of an isosceles triangle are congruent.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$\triangle ABC$ with $\overline{AB}\cong\overline{AC}$. Available: SAS, and the fact that congruent triangles have congruent corresponding parts.</div>
  <div slot="want">$\angle B \cong \angle C$.</div>
  <div slot="how">The angles we want are corresponding parts of *something*. If we can produce two congruent triangles in which $\angle B$ and $\angle C$ correspond, we are done. The trick is that $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ACB$ — the same triangle, matched to itself the other way round — do the job.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Consider the correspondence $A \leftrightarrow A$, $B \leftrightarrow C$, $C \leftrightarrow B$ between $\triangle ABC$ and itself.

Under it, $\overline{AB}$ corresponds to $\overline{AC}$, and these are congruent by hypothesis. The angle at $A$ corresponds to itself, so it is congruent to itself. And $\overline{AC}$ corresponds to $\overline{AB}$, congruent again by hypothesis.

So the correspondence matches side, included angle, side. By SAS, $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle ACB$.

Corresponding parts of congruent triangles are congruent, and $\angle B$ corresponds to $\angle C$. So $\angle B \cong \angle C$.
</Proof>

Notice where the work was: the Discussion found the *idea* — match the triangle to itself the other way round. The proof is short because the Discussion was honest about where the difficulty lay. A two-column version would present the correspondence with no hint of where it came from.

<Warning title="Never assume what you are proving">
Two shapes to avoid:

**Assuming the conclusion.** A proof of "if $x > 1$ then $x^2 > 1$" that begins "suppose $x^2 > 1$" proves the converse.

**Working backwards.** Starting from the goal and manipulating until something true appears is fine as *scratch work* — it belongs in the Discussion — but the written proof must run forwards, from hypothesis to conclusion, with every step valid in that direction.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The $\square$ at the end marks completion. Older texts write *Q.E.D.* — *quod erat demonstrandum*, "which was to be shown."

Both mean the same thing: *I claim the argument is finished, and you should now believe the statement.* It is a small act of accountability, and it is worth taking seriously.
</Aside>
