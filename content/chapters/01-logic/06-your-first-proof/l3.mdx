You know what an if–then claim promises (module 1.5). Now: how do you show that one is actually true?

Here is the claim we will prove. Read it and check a few numbers first.

> **Claim.** If a number is a multiple of $6$, then it is even.

Try $12$: even. Try $18$: even. Try $42$: even. Try $6$: even. It is looking good.

But checking examples is not enough. There are infinitely many multiples of $6$, and you will never finish the list. To be *sure*, you need an argument that covers all of them at once. That argument is called a **proof**.

## Planning first

Mathematicians write a **Discussion** before the proof — a short note about what they have, what they are aiming for, and what route they will take. It is the map, not the trip.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Our number is a multiple of $6$. That means it is $6$ times some whole number — say $6$ groups of something, repeated.</div>
  <div slot="want">To show it is even. Even means it is $2$ times some whole number.</div>
  <div slot="how">Start from what you're given. A group of $6$ is really two groups of $3$. So a pile of $6$s can be re-cut into a pile of $2$s.</div>
</Discussion>

Notice the shape: we wrote down what *even* and *multiple of 6* actually mean. Almost every proof begins by unpacking a definition. If you are stuck, that is usually the reason.

## The proof

<Proof>
**Take any one and follow it.** Pick a multiple of $6$ — any one at all. Do not pick a favorite; pick a nameless one, so that whatever we say about it is true of all of them.

Being a multiple of $6$ means it is a pile of $6$s. Some number of $6$s, all the same size.

Now split every $6$ in the pile into $3 + 3$. Nothing is added and nothing is thrown away — each $6$ has simply become two $3$s.

So the pile is now made entirely of $3$s, and they come in **pairs** — two out of every $6$. Pairs means the $3$s can be matched up with none left over.

A number that splits into matching pairs is even. So our number is even.

And we never said which multiple of $6$ it was. So this works for every single one.
</Proof>

That little square at the end — □ — is the **end-of-proof marker**. It means: the argument is finished and nothing is missing. You will see it for the rest of your mathematical life.

## What made that a proof

Three things, and they are the same three every time.

1. **It started from what you're given.** The first sentence assumed the *if* part. It did not assume the *then* part; that would be cheating.
2. **Every step was allowed.** Rewriting $6$ as $2 \times 3$ is just arithmetic. Regrouping factors is allowed. Nothing snuck in.
3. **It ended on the conclusion.** The last sentence said the thing the claim promised.

A proof built this way — assume the *if*, walk forward, arrive at the *then* — is called a **direct proof**. It is the first and most common kind.

<Warning title="Examples are not proofs">
Checking $12$, $18$, $42$ and $6$ shows the claim has not failed *yet*. It does not show the claim can never fail. One counterexample would sink the claim (module 1.11), but no number of examples can float it. That gap is the reason proofs exist.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Proofs are written in full sentences on purpose. A proof is an explanation addressed to a reader, and a page of bare equations does not explain anything. If you cannot read your proof aloud as English, it is not finished.
</Aside>
