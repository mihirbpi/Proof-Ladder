Single statements are rarely what a theorem asserts. "If $x > 1$ **and** $x$ is an integer, then $x \geq 2$" joins two hypotheses; "$x \leq -1$ **or** $x \geq 1$" describes a solution set in two pieces. This module fixes the two connectives that do the joining.

Throughout, $p$ and $q$ denote statements.

## Conjunction

The **conjunction** $p \wedge q$ ("$p$ and $q$") is true exactly when both are true.

<TruthTable
  variables={["p", "q"]}
  expressions={["p ∧ q"]}
  rows={[
    { p: true,  q: true,  "p ∧ q": true  },
    { p: true,  q: false, "p ∧ q": false },
    { p: false, q: true,  "p ∧ q": false },
    { p: false, q: false, "p ∧ q": false }
  ]}
/>

One true row out of four — conjunction is demanding. Half of a conjunction being true earns nothing: with $p$ = "$12$ is a multiple of $3$" (T) and $q$ = "$12$ is a multiple of $5$" (F), $p \wedge q$ is false.

## Disjunction

The **disjunction** $p \vee q$ ("$p$ or $q$") is true exactly when at least one is true.

<TruthTable
  variables={["p", "q"]}
  expressions={["p ∨ q"]}
  rows={[
    { p: true,  q: true,  "p ∨ q": true  },
    { p: true,  q: false, "p ∨ q": true  },
    { p: false, q: true,  "p ∨ q": true  },
    { p: false, q: false, "p ∨ q": false }
  ]}
/>

Three true rows — and note row 1 in particular. When *both* disjuncts hold, $p \vee q$ is still true.

<Warning title="Mathematical or is inclusive">
Everyday English often uses *or* exclusively: "soup or salad" means one, not both. Mathematics never does.

The convention is not arbitrary. Solving $x^2 \geq 1$ gives $x \leq -1$ **or** $x \geq 1$, and you would not want that statement to become false if some $x$ satisfied both. More sharply: "$x \geq 0$ or $x \leq 0$" is true for every real $x$, including $x = 0$ where both hold. An exclusive reading would make it false at exactly one point, and every theorem quoting it would need a special case.

Exclusive or exists, and it equals $(p \vee q)\wedge\neg(p\wedge q)$. Mathematics uses it rarely and always names it.
</Warning>

## Logical equivalence

Two statements are **logically equivalent**, written $\equiv$, when their truth-table columns agree in **every** row.

This is the key notion, because equivalence licenses substitution: if $p \equiv q$, you may replace one by the other anywhere in a proof.

Quick examples, each checkable in four rows:
$$
p \wedge q \equiv q \wedge p, \qquad
\neg\neg p \equiv p, \qquad
p \vee (p \wedge q) \equiv p.
$$

That last one is the *absorption law*, and it is worth verifying — it is less obvious than the first two, and it reappears for sets in Chapter 2.

The two genuinely surprising equivalences involving $\wedge$ and $\vee$ are De Morgan's laws, and they are all of module 1.4:
$$
\neg(p\wedge q) \equiv \neg p \vee \neg q, \qquad \neg(p \vee q)\equiv \neg p \wedge \neg q.
$$
Notice that negation does not pass through quietly — it *swaps* the connective.

## Contradictions and tautologies

Some columns never change.

<TruthTable
  variables={["p"]}
  expressions={["¬p", "p ∧ ¬p", "p ∨ ¬p"]}
  rows={[
    { p: true,  "¬p": false, "p ∧ ¬p": false, "p ∨ ¬p": true },
    { p: false, "¬p": true,  "p ∧ ¬p": false, "p ∨ ¬p": true }
  ]}
/>

A statement false in every row is a **contradiction**; true in every row, a **tautology**.

Neither depends on what $p$ *says*. "$12$ is prime and $12$ is not prime" is false; so is "the moon is cheese and the moon is not cheese." The shape decides, and that is the first sign that logic is a subject in its own right rather than a commentary on other subjects.

Both have jobs ahead of them:

- $p \wedge \neg p$ powers **proof by contradiction** (module 2.5): if assuming $\neg S$ yields a contradiction, then $S$ holds.
- $p \vee \neg p$ — *excluded middle* — licenses **proof by cases**. Every time you write "either $x$ is rational or it isn't," you are quoting it, and you then owe a proof in each branch.

<Aside>
Tautologies are exactly the valid inference patterns. Writing $p \Rightarrow q$ for *"$p$ implies $q$"* (module 1.5), that $(p\wedge q)\Rightarrow p$ is a tautology *is* the statement that "from $p$ and $q$, infer $p$" is a sound rule. Every rule of inference you use is a tautology wearing different clothes.
</Aside>

## Looking ahead

Chapter 2 defines $S \cap T$ by "$x \in S$ **and** $x \in T$", and $S \cup T$ by "$x \in S$ **or** $x \in T$". Every fact in this module therefore becomes a fact about sets: commutativity of $\wedge$ becomes commutativity of $\cap$, De Morgan for connectives becomes De Morgan for complements, and the absorption law above becomes $S \cup (S\cap T) = S$.

Nothing new will be discovered there. A translation will be performed.
