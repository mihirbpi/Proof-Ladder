Open your geometry notes at any two-column proof and look at the *statement* column. Almost none of the lines are simple. They say things like "$\overline{AB} \cong \overline{CD}$ **and** $\angle A \cong \angle C$", or "the lines are parallel **or** they meet at exactly one point."

Compound statements are what proofs are actually made of. This module fixes what the two joining words mean.

Throughout, $p$ and $q$ stand for statements.

## Conjunction

The **conjunction** $p \wedge q$ — "$p$ and $q$" — is true exactly when both are true.

<TruthTable
  variables={["p", "q"]}
  expressions={["p ∧ q"]}

    { p: true,  q: true,  "p ∧ q": true  },
    { p: true,  q: false, "p ∧ q": false },

    { p: false, q: false, "p ∧ q": false }
  ]}
/>

One true row out of four. Conjunction is demanding, and geometry uses that constantly: the SAS criterion requires side, *and* included angle, *and* side. Two out of three earns nothing.

## Disjunction

The **disjunction** $p \vee q$ — "$p$ or $q$" — is true exactly when at least one is true.

<TruthTable
  variables={["p", "q"]}
  expressions={["p ∨ q"]}
  rows={[
    { p: true,  q: true,  "p ∨ q": true  },
    { p: true,  q: false, "p ∨ q": true  },
    { p: false, q: true,  "p ∨ q": true  },
    { p: false, q: false, "p ∨ q": false }
  ]}
/>

Three true rows — and look hard at row 1. When *both* hold, $p \vee q$ is still true.

<Warning title="Mathematical or is inclusive">
In conversation, "or" often means one but not both — "soup or salad." Mathematics never does.

The convention is not arbitrary. Consider a statement you use in coordinate proofs: for any real $x$, either $x \geq 0$ or $x \leq 0$. That is true for every $x$ — including $x = 0$, where **both** hold. Under an exclusive reading it would be false at exactly one point, and every theorem quoting it would need a special case.

Exclusive or exists, written $\oplus$, and it equals $(p \vee q)\wedge\neg(p \wedge q)$. Mathematics uses it rarely and always names it.
</Warning>

## Logical equivalence

Two statements are **logically equivalent**, written $\equiv$, when their truth-table columns agree in **every** row.

This is the notion that licenses substitution: if $\varphi \equiv \psi$, you may replace one with the other anywhere in a proof and the proof stays valid.

Some quick ones, each checkable in four rows:
$$
p \wedge q \equiv q \wedge p, \qquad \neg\neg p \equiv p, \qquad p \vee (p \wedge q) \equiv p.
$$

The last is the *absorption law*. Verify it — it is less obvious than the others, and it reappears for sets in Chapter 2.

## Contradictions and tautologies

Some columns never change.

<TruthTable
  variables={["p"]}
  expressions={["¬p", "p ∧ ¬p", "p ∨ ¬p"]}
  rows={[
    { p: true,  "¬p": false, "p ∧ ¬p": false, "p ∨ ¬p": true },
    { p: false, "¬p": true,  "p ∧ ¬p": false, "p ∨ ¬p": true }
  ]}
/>

A statement false in every row is a **contradiction**; true in every row, a **tautology**.

Neither depends on what $p$ says. "$\triangle ABC$ is isosceles and $\triangle ABC$ is not isosceles" is false, and so is the same sentence about any figure. The **shape** decides, not the geometry — which is the first sign that logic is a subject of its own.

Both have jobs ahead of them:

- $p \wedge \neg p$ is the engine of **proof by contradiction** (module 2.5). Your geometry course probably calls it *indirect proof*: assume the opposite of what you want, derive an impossibility, conclude.
- $p \vee \neg p$ — the law of the excluded middle — licenses **proof by cases**. Every time a proof says "either the point is inside the triangle or it is not," it is quoting this tautology, and it then owes an argument in each branch.

## Where the two-column format hides this

A two-column proof records one statement per line with one reason beside it. That format quietly assumes each line is a *single* assertion — but lines like

> $\overline{AB}\cong\overline{DE}$ and $\angle B \cong \angle E$ and $\overline{BC}\cong\overline{EF}$ — *Given*

are conjunctions, and the next line applies SAS to all three at once. The format works, but it hides the logical structure inside the punctuation.

Paragraph proofs make the structure visible, which is one reason mathematicians write them. Module 1.6 takes that up properly.

<Aside>
Tautologies are exactly the valid inference patterns. That $(p \wedge q)\Rightarrow p$ is a tautology *is* the statement that "from $p$ and $q$, infer $p$" is a sound rule — which is what you do every time you extract one piece of a *Given*.

Every rule in the reason column of a two-column proof is a tautology or a theorem in disguise.
</Aside>

## Precedence

Unlike arithmetic, the connectives have only a partial convention: $\neg$ binds tightest, then $\wedge$, then $\vee$. So

$$\neg p \wedge q \quad\text{means}\quad (\neg p)\wedge q, \quad\text{not}\quad \neg(p\wedge q).$$

The distinction is not academic. The first says *"$p$ fails and $q$ holds"*; the second says *"they do not both hold"* — and row 4 of a truth table separates them, since with $p$ and $q$ both false the first is F and the second is T.

**When a negation is meant to govern a compound, the parentheses are required.** The same goes for mixing $\wedge$ and $\vee$: most authors parenthesise rather than rely on the convention, and you should too.

## Translating your textbook

The connectives are usually buried in ordinary English, and part of reading a theorem carefully is digging them out.

| Book's phrasing | Structure |
|---|---|
| "a quadrilateral with four congruent sides and four right angles" | $\wedge$ |
| "the lines are parallel or they intersect at exactly one point" | $\vee$ |
| "an angle that is neither acute nor obtuse" | $\neg p \wedge \neg q$ |
| "at least one pair of opposite sides is parallel" | $\vee$ |
| "both pairs of opposite sides are parallel" | $\wedge$ |

The distinction in the last two rows is the entire difference between a trapezoid and a parallelogram, and it is a difference between $\vee$ and $\wedge$.

Notice the third row too: *neither–nor* is a conjunction of negations, and module 1.4 shows it equals the negation of a disjunction. Ordinary English encodes De Morgan's law in a single word.
