Nearly every theorem you will ever meet has the same grammar: *if such-and-such, then so-and-so.* Statements of this form are called **conditionals** and written
$$
p \Rightarrow q.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

The *if* part $p$ is the **hypothesis** and the *then* part $q$ is the **conclusion**.

A conditional is a very particular kind of promise. It promises exactly one thing: whenever $p$ is true, $q$ is true. It says nothing at all when $p$ is false.

## Other ways to write the same thing

The same conditional appears in several disguises, and all of them mean $p \Rightarrow q$:

- *if $p$, then $q$*
- *$p$ implies $q$*
- *$q$ if $p$*
- *$p$ only if $q$*
- *$p$ is sufficient for $q$*
- *$q$ is necessary for $p$*

The last three cause most of the trouble. *$p$ only if $q$* points the arrow the same way as *if $p$ then $q$*, even though the word *if* sits next to $q$. And *necessary* and *sufficient* swap which side they name: $p$ being enough to force $q$ is the same fact as $q$ being unavoidable whenever $p$ holds.

Textbook theorems are stated in all six forms. **The first job on reading one is to identify $p$ and $q$**, because getting them backwards means proving the converse — a different statement, and module 1.7 shows how different.

## A statement can be true because its hypothesis is false

Consider
$$
25 \text{ is a multiple of } 6 \;\Rightarrow\; 25 \text{ is even.}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

Its hypothesis is false, so the whole conditional is vacuously true. We are *not* claiming that $25$ is even. We are claiming that "*if* $25$ were a multiple of $6$, *then* it would be even" — which is technically a true statement about a case that never occurs.

<Warning title="Two things to keep separate">
"The conditional $p \Rightarrow q$ is true" is *not* the same statement as "$p$ is true" or "$q$ is true." A conditional can be true even when nothing on either side is happening.
</Warning>

## A conditional with a variable

Take
$$
x > 1 \;\Rightarrow\; x^2 > 1.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

Now $p$ and $q$ depend on the number $x$ you plug in. Test a few:

- $x = 3$: hypothesis true ($3 > 1$), conclusion true ($9 > 1$). Row 1.
- $x = \tfrac12$: hypothesis false. Conclusion also false. Row 4 — vacuously true, so the conditional itself is still true for this value of $x$.
- $x = -2$: hypothesis false ($-2 \not> 1$). Conclusion true ($4 > 1$). Row 3 — still true.

Can we find any $x$ that falls in row 2? Row 2 would require $x > 1$ and $x^2 \le 1$ at the same time. Squaring a number bigger than $1$ produces a number bigger than $1$, so no such $x$ exists. Therefore the conditional — where $\forall$ reads *for all*, previewed here and treated in §1.10 —
$$
\forall\, x \in \mathbb{R},\; x > 1 \Rightarrow x^2 > 1
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

is true for every real $x$. That is what we will prove in the next module.

## Why the shape matters

Almost every theorem has this shape because it is the shape of *useful* information. You spot a case where the hypothesis holds and immediately know the conclusion holds. Direct proof will be one method for establishing conditionals; the contrapositive (§1.7) and contradiction will be others. All of them start from the same observation: to make $p \Rightarrow q$ true, we only have to rule out row 2 of the table.

That observation is worth restating as a plan, because it is the plan for every proof in the next four modules:

> **Assume $p$. Derive $q$. There is nothing else to do.**

You do not check the rows where $p$ is false — the table already grants those. You do not verify particular values. Assuming the hypothesis is what gives you something concrete to compute with, and the conclusion is the target.

Notice the trade. The claim covers infinitely many values of $x$, and the proof examines none of them individually. **You give up knowing which case you are in, and in exchange you cover all of them at once.**

<Aside>
Everyday speech uses *if–then* to signal cause: "if you drop it, it breaks." Mathematics keeps only the truth-value pattern in the table and discards the causation.

So "if $2+2=5$, then the moon is made of cheese" is a **true statement**. Nothing about the moon follows from it, because the hypothesis never holds — but the conditional itself is true, for exactly the reason a promise about rainy days survives a sunny week.

That gap between the two uses is why the truth table feels strange at first. It is not modeling causation, and it was never trying to.
</Aside>
