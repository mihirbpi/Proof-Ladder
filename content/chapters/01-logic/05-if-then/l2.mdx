A **promise** sounds like this:

> If it rains, I bring the umbrella.

The promise talks about one thing: what happens when it rains.

Think about the four ways a day can go.

<Figure alt="A 2 by 2 grid of the four weather-and-umbrella cases. The only broken case is rain with no umbrella." caption="Four days. One broken promise.">
  <UmbrellaCases />
</Figure>

- It rains and I have the umbrella. **Promise kept.**
- It rains and I do not have the umbrella. **Promise broken.**
- It does not rain and I have the umbrella. **Promise kept.** The promise never said anything about sunny days.
- It does not rain and I do not have the umbrella. **Promise kept.** Same reason.

There is only one **bad case**: it rains and I do not have the umbrella. Every other day, my promise still holds.

If you want to prove somebody broke this kind of promise, you have to find that one bad case. If it never happens, the promise is kept.

<TryIt>
Make your own if-then promise: *if I finish my snack, I get a sticker.* Say the one bad case. It is the only way to break it.
</TryIt>
