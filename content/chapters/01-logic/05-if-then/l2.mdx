A **promise** sounds like this:

> If it rains, I bring the umbrella.

Read it again and notice what it does *not* say. It says nothing at all about sunny days. Not one word.

So think about the four ways a day can go.

<Figure alt="A 2 by 2 grid of the four weather-and-umbrella cases. The only broken case is rain with no umbrella." caption="Four days. Only one soggy disaster.">
  <UmbrellaCases />
</Figure>

- It rains and I have the umbrella. **Promise kept.** Nice and dry.
- It rains and I do not have the umbrella. **Promise broken.** Soaked.
- It does not rain and I have the umbrella. **Promise kept.** I look a little funny carrying it, but I kept my promise.
- It does not rain and I do not have the umbrella. **Promise kept.** Same reason. Sunny days were never part of the deal.

So there is exactly one **bad case**: rain, no umbrella. That single square is the only place a promise can break.

Here is why this matters. If you want to catch somebody breaking a promise like this, do not go hunting everywhere. Go straight to the bad case. If the bad case never happens, the promise is kept — even if it never rains all year.

## Find the bad case

Every if–then promise has exactly one bad case, and you can find it the same way each time. Take the *if* part and make it happen. Take the *then* part and make it not happen. Put them together. That is the bad case.

> If the light is red, we stop.

The *if* part: the light is red. The *then* part, turned around: we do not stop. Bad case: **the light is red and we do not stop.** Nothing else counts. Rolling through a green light is not breaking this promise — the promise never mentioned green lights.

<TryIt>
Make your own if–then promise. Try: *if I finish my peas, I get a sticker.* Now say the one bad case out loud. It is the only way anybody gets in trouble.
</TryIt>
