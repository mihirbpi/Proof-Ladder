Most of the interesting statements in mathematics have a shape:

> If *this* is true, then *that* is true.

Statements of this shape are called **conditionals**, written $p \Rightarrow q$. The *if* part $p$ is the **hypothesis**; the *then* part $q$ is the **conclusion**. The conditional makes exactly one commitment: whenever $p$ is true, $q$ is true too. It says nothing about the cases where $p$ is false.

## The four cases

There are four possible ways the truth values of $p$ and $q$ can line up. Only one of them breaks the conditional.

The second row is the only false row. If $p$ is true but $q$ is false, the promise was broken. Every other row is true.

The bottom two rows are what mathematicians call **vacuously true**. The hypothesis was never met, so nothing was ever promised. It is easier to see with the umbrella example:

If the sky is clear, the promise about rainy days made no claim to keep or break. So we treat it as kept. This is not a technical trick; it is what the promise actually said.

## Numbers, not weather

Take the claim
$$
25 \text{ is a multiple of } 6 \Rightarrow 25 \text{ is even.}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

Is this true? It is. The hypothesis "$25$ is a multiple of $6$" is false, so the conditional is vacuously true. We are not saying $25$ is even (it isn't). We are saying the conditional as a whole didn't ask $25$ to be even, because $25$ never entered the case the conditional was talking about.

Compare with
$$
12 \text{ is a multiple of } 6 \Rightarrow 12 \text{ is even.}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

This one is true because both parts are true — the top row of the table.

And
$$
12 \text{ is a multiple of } 6 \Rightarrow 12 \text{ is odd.}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

This one is false. The hypothesis is true, the conclusion is false: row two.

## Other ways to write the same thing

The same conditional shows up in several disguises, and all of them mean $p \Rightarrow q$:

- *if $p$, then $q$*
- *$p$ implies $q$*
- *$q$ if $p$*
- *$p$ only if $q$*
- *$p$ is sufficient for $q$*
- *$q$ is necessary for $p$*

The last three are the ones that trip people up. *$p$ only if $q$* has the arrow pointing the same way as *if $p$ then $q$*, even though the word *if* sits next to $q$. And "necessary" and "sufficient" swap which side they name: $p$ being enough to force $q$ is the same fact as $q$ being unavoidable whenever $p$ holds.

When a textbook theorem is stated in one of these forms, the first job is to identify $p$ and $q$. Get that backwards and you will prove the wrong statement.

<Warning title="Confusing sentence, careful thinking">
The English sentence "if $p$ then $q$" makes it easy to feel like the conditional says *because $p$*, or *only when $p$*. It does not. It only rules out the one case where $p$ is true and $q$ is false. Everything else is allowed.
</Warning>

## What proving a conditional will look like

If we want to prove *if a number is a multiple of 6, then it is even*, we don't have to check every possible number. We only have to check the one row that could go wrong. That is,

- assume the hypothesis is true — the number *is* a multiple of 6,
- and show the conclusion must be true — the number *has to be* even.

That is the shape of every direct proof you will write. The next module makes it precise.

Notice what this buys you. The claim covers infinitely many numbers, and the proof examines none of them individually. Assuming the hypothesis gives you something to work with — *this number is $6$ times something* — and from there the conclusion follows by algebra that never mentions which number it was. That is the trade at the heart of every proof: you give up knowing the specific case, and in exchange you cover all of them at once.

<Aside>
Everyday speech uses *if–then* to signal cause: "if you drop it, it breaks." Mathematics keeps only the truth-value pattern in the table and throws the causation away.

So "if $2+2=5$, then the moon is made of cheese" is a true statement. Nothing about the moon follows from it, because the hypothesis never holds — but the conditional itself is true, and it is true for the same reason a promise about rainy days survives a sunny week.
</Aside>
