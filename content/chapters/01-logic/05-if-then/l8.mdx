Every theorem in this course, and nearly every theorem you will meet afterwards, has the same shape:

> Whenever this holds, that holds too.

Symbolically, $p \Rightarrow q$ — read *"$p$ implies $q$"*, or *"if $p$ then $q$"* — the **conditional** with **hypothesis** $p$ and **conclusion** $q$. Understanding what the conditional actually means, including its behavior on the cases where the hypothesis does not hold, is the entire point of this module.

Two set symbols also make their first appearance below. $A \subseteq S$ is read *"$A$ is a subset of $S$"*, meaning every element of $A$ is in $S$; and $\emptyset$ denotes the **empty set**, the set with no elements at all. Chapter 2 builds both properly.

## Definition via truth table

$p \Rightarrow q$ is defined to be true in three of the four possible cases, and false in one:

The single false row — hypothesis true, conclusion false — is the only case that would count as a broken promise. Every other case is *true* by definition.

### Vacuous truth

The last two rows, where the hypothesis fails, are said to make the conditional **vacuously true**. The word is not making a claim about what is really true out there: it is a labeling rule that keeps the truth table tidy. If we had chosen instead to leave those cases undefined, we would need three-valued logic and the rest of mathematics would become much clumsier to state.

The umbrella promise is the pedagogical trick that makes vacuous truth land. On a sunny day, no one thinks I broke my promise about rainy days. So on a sunny day, my promise is intact — that is, still true. When the truth table declares row 4 true, it is writing in permanently the same intuition.

## A conditional in mathematics: if $x > 1$ then $x^2 > 1$

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\in$ reads "is in", $\mathbb{R}$ reads the reals.*

Let us pick apart a small theorem you already believe:
$$
\forall\, x \in \mathbb{R},\; x > 1 \Rightarrow x^2 > 1.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces". So the line reads: for every real number $x$, being bigger than $1$ forces its square to be bigger than $1$*

The claim is a if–then claim made about every element. For each individual $x$, we get a conditional $p(x) \Rightarrow q(x)$ where $p(x)$ is "$x > 1$" and $q(x)$ is "$x^2 > 1$." Sample:

| $x$ | $p(x)$ | $q(x)$ | $p(x) \Rightarrow q(x)$ | row |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $3$   | T | T | T | 1 |
| $0.4$ | F | F | T | 4 |
| $-2$  | F | T | T | 3 |
| ??    | T | F | F | 2 |

The claim made about every element is true exactly when the fourth row of the table is *empty* — that is, when no $x$ produces the "hypothesis true, conclusion false" case. Proving the theorem means ruling out that row. That is exactly what a direct proof does, and the next module (§1.1.5) shows how.

<Warning title="Common confusion, worth fixing early">
Note carefully the difference between three statements:

- "$p$ is true."
- "$q$ is true."
- "$p \Rightarrow q$ is true."

The third can hold without either of the first two. For instance, if $p$ is false then $p \Rightarrow q$ is true regardless of $q$. This is not a bug; it is the shape of the definition that makes the whole logical system run.
</Warning>

## The phrasings, and the two that trip people

All of the following denote $p \Rightarrow q$:

$$\text{if } p \text{ then } q \quad\bullet\quad p \text{ implies } q \quad\bullet\quad q \text{ if } p \quad\bullet\quad p \text{ only if } q$$
$$p \text{ is \textbf{sufficient} for } q \quad\bullet\quad q \text{ is \textbf{necessary} for } p \quad\bullet\quad q \text{ whenever } p$$

Two of these invert the visual order and are worth memorizing directly.

**"$p$ only if $q$"** is $p\Rightarrow q$. The *only* signals necessity: $q$ is required for $p$, so $p$ cannot hold without it. Reading it as $q \Rightarrow p$ — which the word order invites — is the standard error.

**"$q$ is necessary for $p$"** is likewise $p\Rightarrow q$, and *"$p$ is sufficient for $q$"* is the same statement read from the other end. An if-and-only-if statement says both roles at once, which is why *necessary and sufficient* means *iff* (§1.9).

The necessary/sufficient vocabulary is worth becoming fluent in now, because calculus states hypotheses that way constantly. *"Continuity is necessary for differentiability"* and *"differentiability is sufficient for continuity"* are the same theorem, and neither says anything about the converse (§1.7: the same statement with its two halves swapped).

## Conditionals with false hypotheses are everywhere

Vacuous truth stops looking like a curiosity as soon as the hypothesis is a condition that can fail to be met.

*"If $\{a_n\}$ converges — settles towards a single value — then it is bounded, never exceeding some fixed size"* is a true theorem, and applying it to a divergent sequence gives nothing — not a false conclusion, simply no information. Row 3 and row 4 of the table are the cases where a theorem is silent, and recognizing when a theorem is silent is as useful as recognizing when it applies.

The pattern is sharper in statements quantified over a set that might be empty:

$$\text{every element of } \{x \in \mathbb{R} : x^2 < 0\} \text{ is rational}$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in". So the line reads: every element of the collection of real numbers whose square is negative is rational — and since no real number has a negative square, that collection is empty, so the claim holds with nothing to check*

is true, and it is true because the set is empty. Chapter 2 uses exactly this to prove $\emptyset\subseteq S$ for every $S$ — a fact that would be false under any other convention, and that is needed constantly.

## Where this shows up next

- **Direct proof** (§1.6). To prove $p \Rightarrow q$, assume $p$ and derive $q$. The whole game reduces to eliminating row 2 of the truth table.
- **Contrapositive.** The truth table for $\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$ is identical to that for $p \Rightarrow q$. So the two statements are logically equivalent — we can prove one by proving the other. Module 1.7 makes this precise.
- **If and only if.** *$p$ if and only if $q$* is the conjunction of $p \Rightarrow q$ and $q \Rightarrow p$. Proving one, therefore, requires two conditional proofs (§1.9).
- **Vacuous statements everywhere.** Any statement of the form "every element of $\emptyset$ satisfies …" is vacuously true. If you spot it in a proof, do not fight the convention; use it.

## Why the vacuous case is the honest choice

The truth table calls *if $p$ then $q$* true whenever $p$ is false, and that decision bothers almost everyone the first time. *"If the moon is made of cheese then $2+2=5$"* comes out **true**, which is not how the words behave in conversation.

The mismatch is real and worth naming rather than explaining away. Ordinary English *if* carries a suggestion of connection — that the first thing has something to do with the second. The arrow of §1.5 carries no such suggestion. It records only a promise about truth values, and a promise whose condition never arises has not been broken.

The reason to accept the trade is that the alternative is worse. Suppose you insisted that an if–then be false when its parts are unconnected. Then *"for every real $x$, if $x > 1$ then $x^2 > 1$"* could no longer be checked one number at a time, because settling the case $x = 0$ would require deciding whether $0 > 1$ is *relevantly* connected to $0 > 1$ — a question with no mathematical content and no agreed answer.

The convention is what lets a universal claim be verified case by case. That is the whole of its justification, and it is enough.

Reading conditionals exactly is what makes the theorems of calculus usable rather than merely memorable.

**Almost every theorem you meet will be a conditional with a checkable hypothesis.** *If the graph has no break here, then …*; *if the curve is smooth on this stretch, then …*. The conclusions are the memorable part and the hypotheses are the part that decides whether you may use the theorem at all.

The routine failure in a first course is applying a theorem whose hypothesis was never checked — and it is a failure to read a conditional, not a failure of calculus. A theorem says **nothing whatever** when its hypothesis is false; it does not say the conclusion fails, it simply declines to speak.

**The habit to build now.** Whenever you meet a theorem, write its hypothesis and its conclusion on separate lines, then ask what the theorem tells you when the hypothesis is false. Usually the answer is *nothing*, and knowing that is what stops you claiming more than you have.

</WhereThisGoes>
