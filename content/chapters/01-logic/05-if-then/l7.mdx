Every theorem in this course, and nearly every theorem you will meet afterwards, has the same shape:

> Whenever this holds, that holds too.

Symbolically, $p \Rightarrow q$: the **conditional** with **hypothesis** $p$ and **conclusion** $q$. Understanding what the conditional actually means — including its behaviour on the cases where the hypothesis does not hold — is the entire point of this module.

## Definition via truth table

$p \Rightarrow q$ is defined to be true in three of the four possible cases, and false in one:

<TruthTable
  variables={["p", "q"]}
  expressions={["p ⇒ q"]}
  rows={[
    { p: true,  q: true,  "p ⇒ q": true  },
    { p: true,  q: false, "p ⇒ q": false },
    { p: false, q: true,  "p ⇒ q": true  },
    { p: false, q: false, "p ⇒ q": true  }
  ]}
/>

The single false row — hypothesis true, conclusion false — is the only case that would count as a broken promise. Every other case is *true* by definition.

The last two rows, where the hypothesis fails, are said to make the conditional **vacuously true**. The word carries no metaphysics: it is a labelling convention that keeps the truth table crisp. If we had chosen instead to leave those cases undefined, we would need three-valued logic and the rest of mathematics would become much clumsier to state.

<Figure alt="Four cases of the umbrella promise: only rain with no umbrella breaks it.">

</Figure>

The umbrella promise is the pedagogical trick that makes vacuous truth land. On a sunny day, no one thinks I broke my promise about rainy days. So on a sunny day, my promise is intact — that is, still true. When the truth table declares row 4 true, it is enshrining the same intuition.

## A conditional in mathematics: $x > 1 \Rightarrow x^2 > 1$

Let us pick apart a small theorem you already believe:
$$
\forall\, x \in \mathbb{R},\; x > 1 \Rightarrow x^2 > 1.
$$

The claim is a universally quantified conditional. For each individual $x$, we get a conditional $p(x) \Rightarrow q(x)$ where $p(x)$ is "$x > 1$" and $q(x)$ is "$x^2 > 1$." Sample:

| $x$ | $p(x)$ | $q(x)$ | $p(x) \Rightarrow q(x)$ | row |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $3$   | T | T | T | 1 |
| $0.4$ | F | F | T | 4 |
| $-2$  | F | T | T | 3 |
| ??    | T | F | F | 2 |

The universally quantified statement is true precisely when the fourth row of the table is *empty* — that is, when no $x$ produces the "hypothesis true, conclusion false" case. Proving the theorem means ruling out that row. That is exactly what a direct proof does, and the next module (§1.1.5) shows how.

<Warning title="Common confusion, worth fixing early">
Note carefully the difference between three statements:

- "$p$ is true."
- "$q$ is true."
- "$p \Rightarrow q$ is true."

The third can hold without either of the first two. In particular, if $p$ is false then $p \Rightarrow q$ is true regardless of $q$. This is not a bug; it is the shape of the definition that makes all of first-order logic run.
</Warning>

## Where this shows up next

- **Direct proof.** To establish $p \Rightarrow q$, assume $p$ and derive $q$. The whole game reduces to eliminating row 2 of the truth table.
- **Contrapositive.** The truth table for $\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$ is identical to that for $p \Rightarrow q$. So the two statements are logically equivalent — we can prove one by proving the other. Module 1.7 makes this precise.
- **Biconditional.** *$p$ if and only if $q$* is the conjunction of $p \Rightarrow q$ and $q \Rightarrow p$. Proving one, therefore, requires two conditional proofs (§1.9).
- **Vacuous statements everywhere.** Any statement of the form "every element of $\emptyset$ satisfies …" is vacuously true. If you spot it in a proof, do not fight the convention; use it.
