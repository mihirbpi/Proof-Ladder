Some claims come in two pieces. They tell you what happens *when* something else is true.

> If a number is a multiple of 6, then it is even.

This is called a **conditional** — a claim of the form *if p then q*. The *if* part is called the **hypothesis**. The *then* part is called the **conclusion**.

The conditional above says: whenever you find yourself looking at a multiple of 6, you can be sure it is even. It does not say anything at all about numbers that are not multiples of 6 — those numbers might be even (like 4), they might be odd (like 5), the promise stays quiet.

## When is a conditional false?

The umbrella promise from before is a good way to see this.

> If it rains, I bring the umbrella.

There is only one way the promise gets broken: it rains, and the umbrella stays home. Every other kind of day — sun with an umbrella, sun without one, rain with the umbrella — the promise stands.

So: a conditional *if p then q* is false in exactly one case — when p is true and q is false. In every other case, it is true.

That includes the two cases where p is false. This surprises people at first: if it is a sunny day, the umbrella promise wasn't tested, so how can it be *true*? The answer is that the promise only spoke about rainy days. On a sunny day, it made no promise to break. We say the conditional is **true by default** when the hypothesis fails.

<Aside>
This "true by default" idea has a fancy name — *vacuously true* — that shows up at higher levels. For now, all it means is: no promise, no problem.
</Aside>

## Back to the numbers

Take our number claim: *if a number is a multiple of 6, then it is even*.

- 12 is a multiple of 6. 12 is even. Case matches, promise kept.
- 18 is a multiple of 6. 18 is even. Case matches, promise kept.
- 5 is not a multiple of 6. The claim never spoke about 5. Promise kept — the case wasn't tested.
- 4 is not a multiple of 6. Same thing.

To *break* this claim, you would have to find a multiple of 6 that is not even. There isn't one — every multiple of 6 is 6 times a whole number, and 6 is already even. So the claim is true.

The whole rest of this chapter is going to be about writing that last sentence carefully.
