Nearly every theorem in your geometry book is an if–then statement.

> *If two sides of a triangle are congruent, then the angles opposite them are congruent.*
>
> *If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, then its diagonals bisect each other.*
>
> *If two parallel lines are cut by a transversal, then alternate interior angles are congruent.*

And every two-column proof you write proves one. This module fixes exactly what such a sentence claims — which turns out to be less than most people assume.

## Definition

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\Rightarrow$ reads "implies".*

A **conditional** is a statement $p \Rightarrow q$. The **hypothesis** is $p$; the **conclusion** is $q$.

The compound statement has its own truth value, separate from those of $p$ and $q$. That is the point to hold onto: $p\Rightarrow q$ can be true while $p$ is false, and asking "is the theorem true?" is a different question from "does the hypothesis hold here?"

## Vacuous truth

Rows 3 and 4 are the ones people resist. When the hypothesis is false, the conditional is **vacuously true**.

$$\text{If } \triangle ABC \text{ has two right angles, then } \overline{AB}\cong\overline{BC}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

True. Not because the conclusion holds, but because no triangle has two right angles, so the promise is never tested.

This is not a technicality invented to fill a table. Module 2.3 uses it to prove $\emptyset\subseteq S$ for every set $S$ — where $\emptyset$ denotes the **empty set**, the set with no elements, and $A \subseteq S$ is read *"$A$ is a subset of $S$"* — and that fact is needed constantly. **A convention that produced anything else would break the mathematics that relies on it.**

<Warning title="A true conditional says nothing about its hypothesis">
*"If a quadrilateral has four congruent sides and four right angles, then its diagonals are congruent"* is true. It does not say that any such quadrilateral exists — though of course one does.

Two separate questions:

1. Is the conditional true? — a question about the implication.
2. Does the hypothesis hold in this figure? — a question about the figure.

Two-column proofs keep these apart with the *Given* line. Everything above the given is the theorem; the given is the claim that this figure satisfies the hypothesis.
</Warning>

## A worked example

Here the real numbers is the standard name for the set of real numbers, $\in$ is read *"is in"*, and $\forall$ is read *"for all"* (module 1.10):

$$\forall x \in\mathbb{R}: \quad x > 1 \Rightarrow x^2 > 1.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

One conditional for each real $x$. Check a few:

- $x = 3$: hypothesis T, conclusion T ($9>1$). **Row 1.**
- $x = 0.4$: hypothesis F, conclusion F ($0.16<1$). **Row 4** — vacuously true.
- $x = -2$: hypothesis F, conclusion T ($4>1$). **Row 3** — also true.

No value produces row 2, and that is what the theorem says. Note that $x = -2$ satisfied the conclusion without the hypothesis; a conditional is entirely untroubled by that, and reading it as though it were is the error module 1.7 names.

## Other phrasings

All of these are $p\Rightarrow q$:

- If $p$, then $q$
- $p$ implies $q$
- $q$ if $p$
- $p$ only if $q$
- $p$ is **sufficient** for $q$
- $q$ is **necessary** for $p$
- $q$ whenever $p$

Two are worth care. *"$p$ only if $q$"* is $p\Rightarrow q$, **not** $q\Rightarrow p$ — the *only* is doing the same work as the word *necessary*. And a bare *"$q$ if $p$"* has the hypothesis after the *if*, so the arrow points the other way from how it reads.

Your book's *necessary and sufficient* phrasing says both directions at once, which is module 1.9's if-and-only-if statement.

## What proving one requires

To prove $p\Rightarrow q$, rule out row 2. The direct route is to **assume $p$ and derive $q$** — after which the bad case cannot arise.

That is exactly what your two-column proofs do. The *Given* is the assumption of $p$; the last line is $q$; the rows between are the derivation. Module 1.6 writes the same thing as a paragraph and argues for why mathematicians prefer that format.

To refute $p\Rightarrow q$, **exhibit row 2**: one case where the hypothesis holds and the conclusion fails. That is a counterexample, and one is enough.

<Warning title="The strange rows are not optional">
Why not leave $p\Rightarrow q$ undefined when $p$ is false?

Because theorems are quantified. *"For every triangle, if it is isosceles then its base angles are congruent"* must have a truth value at **every** triangle, including scalene ones where the hypothesis fails. Leaving those undefined would leave the theorem undefined.

The convention is chosen so that a if–then claim made about every element works out to *"no counterexample exists"* — which is what a theorem should mean.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The four rows generate four related statements: the conditional, its converse $q\Rightarrow p$, its inverse $\neg p\Rightarrow\neg q$, and its contrapositive $\neg q\Rightarrow\neg p$.

Exactly one of the three flips has the same truth value as the original, and module 1.7 identifies it. The consequence for your work is immediate: your book proves theorem-and-converse pairs separately because the converse genuinely does not follow — and it never bothers to prove the contrapositive, because that comes free.
</Aside>
