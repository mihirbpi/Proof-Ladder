*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\Rightarrow$ reads "implies".*

Given a conditional $p \Rightarrow q$, there are three other conditionals you can build from the same two statements by swapping them, negating them, or both. **Flip it around** is the named move, and the point of the module is that only one of the three flips is safe.

| Name | Statement |
|---|---|
| Original | $p \Rightarrow q$ |
| **Converse** | $q \Rightarrow p$ |
| **Inverse** | $\neg p \Rightarrow \neg q$ |
| **Contrapositive** | $\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$ |

Read the columns:

- **The original and the contrapositive are identical** — same value in every row. That is the one equivalence worth memorizing.
- The converse and the inverse are identical to each other too — each is the other's contrapositive — but they differ from the original in rows 2 and 3.

That is the entire content of the module, and it is proved.

## A worked case

Recall the proposition from module 1.6:

> If $x > 1$ then $x^2 > 1$. **(True.)**

**Converse:** if $x^2 > 1$ then $x > 1$. Take $x = -3$. Then $x^2 = 9 > 1$, but $x = -3 \not> 1$. The hypothesis holds and the conclusion fails, so the converse is **false**.

**Inverse:** if $x \leq 1$ then $x^2 \leq 1$. The same $x = -3$: it satisfies $-3 \leq 1$, yet $x^2 = 9 \not\leq 1$. **False.** (Of course — it is the contrapositive of the converse, so it had to fail too.)

**Contrapositive:** if $x^2 \leq 1$ then $x \leq 1$. **True**, and it must be, since the original is.

One number, $x = -3$, killed both the converse and the inverse. That is not luck; it is the equivalence of those two doing its work.

<Warning title="Reading an if-then backwards">
The most common reasoning error in the world is treating a true conditional as though its converse were also true.

*If it rained, the ground is wet.* The ground is wet. Did it rain? Perhaps a sprinkler ran. The conditional gives you no license to conclude $p$ from $q$.

In mathematics this shows up as a proof that proves the wrong direction and stops. If the problem says *prove that $p$ forces $q$* and your argument begins by assuming $q$, you have proved the converse. Check which direction you are traveling before you write the last line.
</Warning>

## Why the contrapositive equivalence is believable

The truth table proves it, but here is the reason it *feels* right. The original says: the situation "$p$ true, $q$ false" never occurs. The contrapositive says: the situation "$q$ false, $p$ true" never occurs. Those are the same forbidden situation, described from opposite ends. Two statements that forbid exactly the same thing are the same statement.

<Aside>
Denying a conditional is a related but different operation, and the result is not another conditional at all. It is the flat claim that the one forbidden row *does* occur: $p$ holds and $q$ fails. This is why disproving "if $p$ then $q$" always means exhibiting a case where $p$ holds and $q$ fails, never producing a rival conditional.
</Aside>

## What this buys you

Because a conditional and its contrapositive are the same statement, proving either one proves both. When the hypothesis is awkward — negative, or about a complicated expression — you may switch to the contrapositive, whose hypothesis is the denial of the original conclusion. Module 1.8 turns this observation into a technique and gives you a rule for deciding when to reach for it.
