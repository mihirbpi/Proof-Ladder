Your geometry course is full of theorem pairs where one direction is proved and the other is proved separately, and full of warnings that a theorem "does not work backwards." This module says exactly which flips are safe.

Given $p \Rightarrow q$:

| Name | Statement |
|---|---|
| Original | $p \Rightarrow q$ |
| **Converse** | $q \Rightarrow p$ |
| **Inverse** | $\neg p \Rightarrow \neg q$ |
| **Contrapositive** | $\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$ |

The named move is **flip it around**.

$$
p \Rightarrow q \;\equiv\; \neg q \Rightarrow \neg p,
\qquad
q \Rightarrow p \;\equiv\; \neg p \Rightarrow \neg q,
$$

and the two pairs disagree in rows 2 and 3.

**Only the contrapositive is equivalent to the original.** The converse and inverse are equivalent to each other — each is the contrapositive of the other — which is why one counterexample usually kills both at once.

## A geometry example

> **Theorem.** If a triangle is equilateral, then it is isosceles. **(True.)**

**Converse:** if a triangle is isosceles, then it is equilateral. Take a triangle with sides $5, 5, 8$. Isosceles ✓, equilateral ✗. **False.**

**Inverse:** if a triangle is not equilateral, then it is not isosceles. The same $5, 5, 8$ triangle: not equilateral ✓, but it *is* isosceles. **False** — necessarily, being the converse's contrapositive.

**Contrapositive:** if a triangle is not isosceles, then it is not equilateral. **True**, and necessarily, since the original is.

One triangle refuted two statements. That economy is a good diagnostic: when you find a counterexample to a converse, check it also refutes the inverse. If it does not, one of your three flips is misformed.

## Why the equivalence is believable

$p \Rightarrow q$ says the situation "$p$ true, $q$ false" never occurs.

$\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$ says "$\neg q$ true, $\neg p$ false" — that is, "$q$ false, $p$ true" — never occurs.

Same forbidden situation, described from opposite ends. Two statements forbidding exactly the same thing are the same statement.

## The theorem-and-converse pattern in your course

Now look at your textbook with this in mind. It keeps stating theorem pairs:

- *If two sides of a triangle are congruent, the angles opposite them are congruent* — and its converse, *if two angles are congruent, the sides opposite them are congruent.*
- *If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, its diagonals bisect each other* — and the converse.
- *If two lines are parallel, alternate interior angles are congruent* — and the converse.

**Each pair needs two proofs.** The book is not being repetitive; it is being honest, because the converse genuinely does not follow. Module 1.9 gives the pair a name — a biconditional — and shows how the two halves usually need different techniques.

And note which pairs your book does *not* state twice. It never proves both "if vertical, then congruent" and its converse — because the converse ("if two angles are congruent, they are vertical") is wildly false.

## Contraposing a compound hypothesis

This is where module 1.4 earns its place.

> If $\overline{AB}\cong\overline{DE}$ **and** $\angle B\cong\angle E$ **and** $\overline{BC}\cong\overline{EF}$, then $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF$.

By De Morgan the contrapositive is:

> If $\triangle ABC \not\cong \triangle DEF$, then $\overline{AB}\not\cong\overline{DE}$ **or** $\angle B\not\cong\angle E$ **or** $\overline{BC}\not\cong\overline{EF}$.

At least one correspondence fails, and you are not told which. That *or* is exactly right — and it is why any proof using this contrapositive will need cases.

<Warning title="Affirming the consequent">
Treating a true conditional as though its converse also held is the most common reasoning error there is.

*If two lines are parallel, they do not intersect.* These two lines do not intersect — are they parallel? In a plane, yes. **In space, no** — they might be skew. The conditional gives no licence to conclude $p$ from $q$, and here the licence genuinely fails.

In algebra it appears as: squaring both sides of an equation and forgetting to check. Squaring is a $\Rightarrow$, not a $\Leftrightarrow$, and the extraneous roots it introduces are converse failures.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Negating a conditional is a different operation from any of these flips:
$$\neg(p \Rightarrow q) \equiv p \wedge \neg q.$$
The negation is not a conditional at all — which is exactly why refuting "if $p$ then $q$" always means exhibiting one case where $p$ holds and $q$ fails, never proposing a rival conditional.
</Aside>

## Necessary and sufficient

If $p \Rightarrow q$, then $p$ is a **sufficient** condition for $q$, and $q$ is a **necessary** condition for $p$. The contrapositive is just the second reading of the same fact — which is why they are equivalent.

Being equilateral is *sufficient* for being isosceles; being isosceles is *necessary* for being equilateral. Module 1.9's biconditional asserts both roles at once.

And because the contrapositive always shares a truth value with the original, you may **prove it instead** whenever it looks easier. Your course already does this — it is what "indirect proof" often amounts to — and module 1.8 makes it a technique with a decision rule.
