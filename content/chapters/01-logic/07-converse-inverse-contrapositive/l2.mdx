Start with a promise:

> If it rains, I bring the umbrella.

There are a few ways to **flip it around**. Let's try all of them and see which ones survive.

## Flip 1: swap the two parts

> If I bring the umbrella, it rains.

Nope. Sunny days happen, and sometimes you carry the umbrella anyway. This flip is a *different* promise, and it can be false even when the first one is true.

## Flip 2: say "not" to both parts

> If it does not rain, I do not bring the umbrella.

Also nope, and for the very same reason. You are allowed to carry an umbrella on a sunny day.

## Flip 3: swap the parts **and** say "not" to both

> If I do not have the umbrella, it is not raining.

Now hold on. Think about this one carefully.

If you are standing there with no umbrella, and it *were* raining, you would have broken your first promise. But you did not break it. So it cannot be raining.

This flip says exactly the same thing as the promise you started with! Same meaning, different words.

<Warning title="Sometimes a flip is true by luck">
Try this one: *If a shape is a square, it has four sides.* Flip 1 gives *if a shape has four sides, it is a square* — and that is false, because rectangles have four sides too. But some promises do flip to something true. "Sometimes true" is not the same as "always true," and only Flip 3 is *always* true.
</Warning>

## The one to remember

Swap the parts and say not to both. That flip always keeps the meaning. The other two do not.

<TryIt>
Say: *If Biscuit is a dog, Biscuit has four legs.* Now do Flip 1, Flip 2, and Flip 3 out loud. Then find a four-legged animal that is not a dog. That one animal breaks Flip 1.
</TryIt>
