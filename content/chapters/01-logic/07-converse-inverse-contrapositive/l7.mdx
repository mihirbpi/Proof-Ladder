Given $p \Rightarrow q$, three further conditionals are obtainable by exchanging the hypothesis and the conclusion, negating both, or doing both. The named move is **flip it around**, and the substantive question is which flips preserve truth.

$$
\underbrace{p \Rightarrow q}_{\text{original}}
\qquad
\underbrace{q \Rightarrow p}_{\text{converse}}
\qquad
\underbrace{\neg p \Rightarrow \neg q}_{\text{inverse}}
\qquad
\underbrace{\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p}_{\text{contrapositive}}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces", $\neg$ is "not". So the line reads: the original, then the same two parts swapped, then both denied, then both swapped and denied.*

Two equivalences and one independence:

$$
p \Rightarrow q \;\equiv\; \neg q \Rightarrow \neg p,
\qquad
q \Rightarrow p \;\equiv\; \neg p \Rightarrow \neg q,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces", $\neg$ is "not", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

and the two pairs disagree in rows 2 and 3, so neither pair forces the other. The converse and inverse are the contrapositives of each other, which is why any counterexample to one is automatically a counterexample to the other.

A proof without the table, since the table generalizes poorly to quantified statements:

<Proof title="Proof of contraposition">
Suppose $p \Rightarrow q$ holds, and suppose $\neg q$. If $p$ were true, then $q$ would follow, contradicting $\neg q$; hence $\neg p$. This establishes $\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$.

Conversely, suppose $\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$ and suppose $p$. If $q$ failed, then $\neg p$ would follow, contradicting $p$; hence $q$. This establishes $p \Rightarrow q$.
</Proof>

Note that both halves used the law of excluded middle (§1.3) implicitly, in the step "if $q$ failed, …, hence $q$." The second half is where the use is essential; see the aside on constructive logic below.

## The anchor example

> **Proposition.** For a real number $x$: $x > 1 \Rightarrow x^2 > 1$. (True, proved in §1.6.)

- **Converse**: $x^2 > 1 \Rightarrow x > 1$. Take $x = -3$: hypothesis true ($9 > 1$), conclusion false. **False.**
- **Inverse**: $x \leq 1 \Rightarrow x^2 \leq 1$. Same witness — the same single refuting value — $x = -3$. **False**, necessarily, being the converse's contrapositive.
- **Contrapositive**: $x^2 \leq 1 \Rightarrow x \leq 1$. **True**, necessarily.

The single witness $x = -3$ refutes two of the four. That economy is a good diagnostic: when you produce a counterexample to a converse, check that it also refutes the inverse. If it doesn't, one of your three statements is misformed.

## Contrapositives of compound hypotheses

This is where §1.4 earns its place. Take:

> **Proposition.** If $x$ is rational and $y$ is irrational, then $x + y \notin \mathbb{Q}$.

The hypothesis is a conjunction, so the contrapositive requires De Morgan:
$$
\neg\big(x \in \mathbb{Q} \wedge y \notin \mathbb{Q}\big) \equiv x \notin \mathbb{Q} \vee y \in \mathbb{Q},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and".*

so the contrapositive reads
$$
x + y \in \mathbb{Q} \;\Rightarrow\; \big(x \notin \mathbb{Q} \;\vee\; y \in \mathbb{Q}\big).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces", $\vee$ is "or".*

A disjunctive conclusion is proved by the standard maneuver: assume one half of the *or* fails and prove the other holds. So: assume $x + y$ rational and $x$ rational; then $y = (x+y) - x$ is a difference of rationals, hence rational — which is the other half of the *or*. Notice that the correct De Morgan negation was essential; the naive negation "$x$ is irrational **and** $y$ rational" is a strictly stronger conclusion, and it is not provable from the hypothesis.

<Warning title="Running the arrow backwards, and its mathematical disguises">
Believing the converse of a true if-then is a mistake old enough to have a name — logicians call it *affirming the consequent*, the consequent being the *then* half. In mathematics it rarely appears so nakedly; it appears as:

- proving the wrong direction of an if-and-only-if statement and stopping (§1.9);
- a chain of manipulations written with $\Leftrightarrow$ where only $\Rightarrow$ holds, e.g. $x = y \Rightarrow x^2 = y^2$, which is not reversible;
- solving an equation by squaring both sides and forgetting to check the solutions — squaring is a $\Rightarrow$, not a $\Leftrightarrow$, and the extraneous roots it introduces are converse failures.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Negation of a conditional is a distinct operation from any of these flips: $\neg(p \Rightarrow q) \equiv p \wedge \neg q$. This is not a conditional at all, which is exactly why refuting a conditional means exhibiting a single case where the hypothesis holds and the conclusion fails — never proposing a rival conditional.
</Aside>

## Necessary and sufficient

The three flips have a vocabulary attached that you will meet in every later course. If $p \Rightarrow q$:

- $p$ is a **sufficient** condition for $q$ — having $p$ suffices to get $q$;
- $q$ is a **necessary** condition for $p$ — you cannot have $p$ without $q$.

The contrapositive is precisely the second reading of the same fact, which is why they are equivalent. The converse asserts the reverse pair of roles, and $p \Leftrightarrow q$ — module 1.9 — asserts both, i.e. that each condition is necessary and sufficient for the other.

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Calculus is full of theorems whose converses fail, and the failures are the interesting part.

**Differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous.** True, and the converse is false: $f(x)=|x|$ is continuous at $0$ and not differentiable there. Weierstrass produced a function continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere, which settles the converse decisively.

**$f$ has a local extremum at an interior point $c$ $\Rightarrow$ $f'(c)=0$** (Fermat's theorem, when $f'(c)$ exists). The converse is false, and $f(x)=x^3$ at $c=0$ is the counterexample every course uses. This is exactly why a critical point requires a *test* rather than a conclusion — the converse's failure is the reason the First and Second Derivative Tests exist.

**Smooth $\Rightarrow$ unbroken.** A curve with a tangent line at a point certainly has no break there. The converse is the tempting one and it is false: $|x|$ is unbroken at $0$ and has no tangent line, because of the corner. The contrapositive, though, is true and free: *a break means no tangent line* — a usable test you get for nothing, once you know contraposition is safe.

</WhereThisGoes>
