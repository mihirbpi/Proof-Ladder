Given a conditional $p \Rightarrow q$, you can build three others by swapping the parts, negating them, or both. The named move is **flip it around**, and the point of the module is that only one flip is safe.

| Name | Statement |
|---|---|
| Original | $p \Rightarrow q$ |
| **Converse** | $q \Rightarrow p$ |
| **Inverse** | $\neg p \Rightarrow \neg q$ |
| **Contrapositive** | $\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$ |

Reading the columns:

$$
p \Rightarrow q \;\equiv\; \neg q \Rightarrow \neg p, \qquad
q \Rightarrow p \;\equiv\; \neg p \Rightarrow \neg q,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces", $\neg$ is "not", $\equiv$ is "says the same as". So the line reads: **a conditional and its contrapositive are the same statement**, and so are the converse and the inverse.*

and the two pairs disagree in rows 2 and 3, so neither pair entails the other.

**Only the contrapositive is equivalent to the original.** The converse and inverse are equivalent to *each other* — each is the contrapositive of the other — which is why a single counterexample usually kills both at once.

## A proof without the table

The table is conclusive but does not generalise to quantified statements, so here is the argument in words.

<Proof>
Suppose $p \Rightarrow q$, and suppose $\neg q$. If $p$ held, then $q$ would follow, contradicting $\neg q$. Hence $\neg p$. This proves $\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$.

Conversely, suppose $\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$, and suppose $p$. If $q$ failed, then $\neg p$ would follow, contradicting $p$. Hence $q$. This proves $p \Rightarrow q$.
</Proof>

## Why the equivalence is believable

$p \Rightarrow q$ asserts that the situation "$p$ true, $q$ false" never occurs.

$\neg q \Rightarrow \neg p$ asserts that "$\neg q$ true, $\neg p$ false" — that is, "$q$ false, $p$ true" — never occurs.

Same forbidden situation, described from opposite ends. Two statements that forbid exactly the same thing are the same statement.

## The anchor example

> **Proposition.** For a real number $x$: if $x > 1$ then $x^2 > 1$. (True — module 1.6.)

**Converse:** $x^2 > 1 \Rightarrow x > 1$. Take $x = -3$: then $x^2 = 9 > 1$, but $-3 \not> 1$. Hypothesis holds, conclusion fails. **False.**

**Inverse:** $x \leq 1 \Rightarrow x^2 \leq 1$. Same $x = -3$: it satisfies $-3 \leq 1$ but $9 \not\leq 1$. **False** — necessarily so, being the contrapositive of the converse.

**Contrapositive:** $x^2 \leq 1 \Rightarrow x \leq 1$. **True**, and necessarily, since the original is.

One number refuted two statements. That economy is a useful check: when you find a counterexample to a converse, confirm it also refutes the inverse. If it doesn't, one of your three flips is misformed.

## Contraposing a compound hypothesis

This is where module 1.4 pays off.

> **Proposition.** If $x$ is rational and $y$ is irrational, then $x + y \notin \mathbb{Q}$.

The hypothesis is a conjunction, so De Morgan is required:
$$
\neg\big(x\in\mathbb{Q} \wedge y \notin \mathbb{Q}\big) \equiv x \notin \mathbb{Q} \vee y \in \mathbb{Q}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and".*

So the contrapositive reads:
$$
x+y \in \mathbb{Q} \Rightarrow \big(x \notin \mathbb{Q} \ \vee\ y \in \mathbb{Q}\big).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces", $\vee$ is "or".*

A disjunctive conclusion is proved by assuming the negation of one disjunct and deriving the other:

<Proof>
Suppose $x + y$ rational, and suppose $x$ rational. Then $y = (x+y) - x$ is a difference of two rationals, hence rational.

So either $x$ is irrational, or $y$ rational — which is the required disjunction. By contraposition, the proposition holds.
</Proof>

Had we mis-negated to "$x$ is irrational **and** $y$ rational", we would have set ourselves a strictly stronger target that is not provable from the hypothesis. Getting De Morgan right was not decoration; it decided whether the proof exists.

<Warning title="Affirming the consequent">
Treating a true conditional as though its converse also held is the most common reasoning error there is.

*If it rained, the ground is wet.* The ground is wet — did it rain? Perhaps a sprinkler ran. The conditional gives no licence to conclude $p$ from $q$.

In mathematics it appears in disguise:

- proving one direction of a biconditional and stopping (module 1.9);
- writing $\Leftrightarrow$ in a chain where only $\Rightarrow$ holds — $x = y \Rightarrow x^2 = y^2$ is not reversible;
- squaring both sides of an equation and not checking the answers. Extraneous roots *are* converse failures.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Negating a conditional is a different operation from any of these flips:
$$\neg(p \Rightarrow q) \equiv p \wedge \neg q.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces", $\neg$ is "not", $\wedge$ is "and", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

The negation is not a conditional at all — which is exactly why refuting "if $p$ then $q$" always means exhibiting one case where $p$ holds and $q$ fails, never proposing a rival conditional.
</Aside>

## Vocabulary you will meet again

If $p \Rightarrow q$, then $p$ is a **sufficient** condition for $q$, and $q$ is a **necessary** condition for $p$. The contrapositive is just the second reading of the same fact, which is why they are equivalent. Module 1.9's biconditional asserts both roles at once.

And because the contrapositive always shares a truth value with the original, you may **prove it instead** whenever it looks easier. That is module 1.8, and it is one of the most useful techniques in the course.
