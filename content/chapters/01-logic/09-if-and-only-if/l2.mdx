Some sentences pack two promises into one. They usually have the words **exactly when** in them.

> You get dessert **exactly when** you eat your peas.

Unpack it. There are two boxes to check.

| Box | The promise |
|---|---|
| ☐ | If you eat your peas, you get dessert. |
| ☐ | If you get dessert, you ate your peas. |

To show the whole sentence is kept, you must **check both boxes**. One is not enough.

## Why one is not enough

Suppose Grandma only kept the first promise. Sam eats peas, Sam gets dessert. Good so far.

<ThingRow items={[{kind:"pea"},{kind:"pea"},{kind:"pea"},{kind:"cookie"}]}
  groups={[3, 1]} groupLabels={["peas eaten", "dessert earned"]} />

But then Sam's little brother gets dessert without touching a single pea! The first box is still checked. The second one is not. Grandma's "exactly when" is broken.

So it really does take both.

## A number one

> A number is even **exactly when** you can split it into two equal piles.

<Proof level="l2">
**Box one.** Say the number is even. Even means you can make pairs with none left over. Give one from each pair to each pile. The piles come out the same. Checked!

**Box two.** Say you can split it into two equal piles. Then put one thing from each pile back together, over and over. You get pairs, with none left over. So it is even. Checked!
</Proof>

Both boxes checked. The whole sentence is true.

<TryIt>
Take 10 crayons. Split into two equal piles — 5 and 5. Now take 9 crayons and try. What is left over? Say which box that breaks.
</TryIt>
