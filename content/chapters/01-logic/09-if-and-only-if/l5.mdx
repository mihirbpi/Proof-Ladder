Module 1.7 showed a conditional and its converse are different claims. Sometimes both hold — and when they do, the pair gets a name.

The **biconditional** $p \Leftrightarrow q$, read "$p$ if and only if $q$", is defined by
$$
p \Leftrightarrow q \;\;\equiv\;\; (p \Rightarrow q) \wedge (q \Rightarrow p).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces", $\wedge$ is "and", $\equiv$ is "says the same as".*

$p \Leftrightarrow q$ is true exactly on the rows where $p$ and $q$ **agree**.

Since the definition is a conjunction, **proving a biconditional means writing two proofs**. The named move is **check both boxes**.

## The theorem

> **Theorem.** For every integer $n$: $n$ is even $\iff$ $n^2$ is even.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Definitions: $n$ even iff $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$; odd iff $n = 2k+1$. Every integer is exactly one of the two.
  <br />Direction (forward) has hypothesis "$n$ even", which unfolds to a usable equation about $n$.
  <br />Direction (backward) has hypothesis "$n^2$ even", which unfolds to $n^2 = 2k$ — no handle on $n$ without inverting a square.</div>
  <div slot="want">Both implications.</div>
  <div slot="how">Prove the easy direction directly. For the hard one, flip it around (module 1.8): the contrapositive of "$n^2$ even $\Rightarrow$ $n$ even" is "$n$ odd $\Rightarrow$ $n^2$ odd", whose hypothesis unfolds usefully.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**(forward) If $n$ is even then $n^2$ is even.**

Suppose $n$ is even, so $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$. Then $n^2 = 4k^2 = 2(2k^2)$, and $2k^2$ is an integer, so $n^2$ is even.

**(backward) If $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even.**

We prove the contrapositive: if $n$ is odd then $n^2$ is odd. Suppose $n = 2k+1$ with $k$ an integer. Then
$$
n^2 = 4k^2+4k+1 = 2(2k^2+2k)+1,
$$
and $2k^2+2k$ is an integer, so $n^2$ is odd. Since every integer is even or odd and not both, contraposition gives $n^2$ even $\Rightarrow$ $n$ even.

Both directions hold, so $n$ is even $\iff$ $n^2$ is even.
</Proof>

The two halves needed **different techniques** — one direct, one by contrapositive. That asymmetry is normal, and it is exactly why a biconditional cannot be proved by one symmetrical argument.

Note also where the (backward) direction leaned: on "every integer is even or odd, not both". That fact is not free — it is the $d=2$ case of the division algorithm, proved in module 4.4.

<Warning title="Label the directions and count them">
Always mark (forward) and (backward), or "forward" and "backward".

Two errors are invisible in unlabelled prose: proving the same direction twice, and proving one and stopping. Both are common, and both look like finished proofs.
</Warning>

## Definitions are biconditionals

Mathematical definitions are written with "if" and always mean "if and only if":

> An integer $n$ is *even* **if** $n = 2k$ for some integer $k$.

means
$$
n \text{ even} \iff \exists k \in \mathbb{Z},\ n = 2k.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when".*

The symbol $\exists$ reads *there exists*; §1.10 introduces it properly.

The consequence is practical: **a definition may be used in both directions**, and virtually every proof uses both, often within four lines of each other. Assuming "$n$ is even" and writing $n = 2k$ uses one; finishing a computation at $2(\text{something integer})$ and declaring the result even uses the other.

Look back at the proof above and you will find each definition unfolded and refolded.

## Chains of equivalences

Sometimes both directions run simultaneously as a chain
$$
p \iff r_1 \iff r_2 \iff \cdots \iff q,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when".*

and this is legitimate **only if every link is genuinely a biconditional**.

<Warning title="Where iff-chains go wrong">
The failure is writing $\iff$ where only $\Rightarrow$ holds:

- $x = y \Rightarrow x^2 = y^2$, but not conversely;
- $a = b \Rightarrow ac = bc$, and conversely **only if** $c \neq 0$;
- $x > 0 \wedge y > 0 \Rightarrow xy > 0$, but not conversely.

Solving equations is where this bites. "Solve $\sqrt{x+2} = x$": squaring gives $x^2 - x - 2 = 0$, so $x \in \{2,-1\}$ — but $x = -1$ fails the original. The squaring step was $\Rightarrow$, not $\iff$, so the final set is a *superset* of the solution set and must be filtered.

Extraneous roots are converse failures (module 1.7), and checking your answers is not busywork — it is discharging the direction you did not prove.
</Warning>

## Proving several statements equivalent

When three or more statements are to be shown pairwise equivalent, proving all $n(n-1)/2$ biconditionals separately is wasteful. Prove a **cycle** instead:
$$
p \Rightarrow q \Rightarrow r \Rightarrow p.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

Three implications rather than six, and every pairwise equivalence follows by going round the cycle. Theorems phrased "the following are equivalent" — they are everywhere in later mathematics — are almost always proved this way, and choosing the cycle order so each individual implication is easy is a real part of the work.

<Aside>
Other phrasings of the same thing: "$p$ is *necessary and sufficient* for $q$" — each implies the other (§1.7); "$p$ exactly when $q$"; "$p$ iff $q$".

And a forward pointer: the theorem proved above is the lemma that makes $\sqrt2$ irrational (module 4.7). There one shows $a^2$ is even and concludes $a$ is even — the (backward) direction, the one that needed the contrapositive.
</Aside>
