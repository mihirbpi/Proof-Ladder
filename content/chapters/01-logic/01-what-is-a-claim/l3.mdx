Before we can prove anything, we need to be careful about what kinds of sentences we are even trying to prove.

A **statement** is a sentence that is either true or false — nothing else. Every statement has one of two **truth values**: true, or false.

Here are statements:

- The cat has four legs.
- $7$ is a prime number.
- $2 + 2 = 5$.
- Every number bigger than 1 has a prime factor.

The first two are true. The third is false. The fourth is also true, though it takes some work to see why. All four are statements.

Here are things that are **not** statements:

- *Is 7 a prime number?* — a question. Questions aren't true or false; they're asking.
- *Factor $x^2 + 2x + 1$.* — a command, not a claim.
- $x + 1$ — not even a full sentence. There's nothing here that could be true or false. What is $x$?

## Open statements

Something interesting: the sentence $x \geq 1$ isn't quite a statement on its own, because it depends on what $x$ is. If $x = 3$, it's true. If $x = 0$, it's false. This kind of sentence is called an **open statement**, and it becomes a real statement once you say what $x$ is (or promise to check for every $x$, which is a story for module 1.10).

## Statements you can't check yet

Sometimes a sentence is a statement — it's definitely either true or false — but nobody knows which. Here is one:

> There are infinitely many pairs of prime numbers that are only 2 apart (like 11 and 13, or 41 and 43).

This is called the **Twin Primes conjecture**. A **conjecture** is a statement that people have guessed is true but nobody has proved yet. Twin Primes has been open for more than 150 years. It's still a statement — it has a definite truth value — we just don't know it.

<Aside>
Some conjectures do eventually get answered. *Fermat's Last Theorem* was a conjecture for 358 years before it was proved in 1994.
</Aside>
