Before we can prove anything, we need to be careful about what kinds of sentences we are even trying to prove.

A **statement** is a sentence that is either true or false — nothing else. Every statement has one of two **truth values**: true, or false.

Here are statements:

- Every square is a rectangle.
- $7$ is a prime number.
- $2 + 2 = 5$.
- Every number bigger than $1$ has a prime factor.

The first two are true. The third is false. The fourth is also true, though it takes some work to see why. All four are statements.

Here are things that are **not** statements:

- *Is $7$ a prime number?* — a question. Questions aren't true or false; they're asking.
- *Draw a rectangle.* — a command, not a claim.
- *the biggest number you can write* — a phrase, not a sentence. It points at something but doesn't claim anything about it.

## Checking now, and checking later

Some statements you can settle on the spot. *$7$ is a prime number* — try dividing by $2$, $3$, $4$, $5$, $6$. Nothing goes in evenly. Done.

Others you cannot settle by checking, no matter how long you keep at it:

> Every number bigger than $1$ has a prime factor.

There is no last number, so there is no point at which you have finished checking. You could test a thousand numbers, a million, and still not have covered them all. Something other than checking is needed, and that something is a **proof** — an argument that covers every case at once. Building those is what the rest of this course is about.

This is the reason mathematics bothers with proof at all. If every statement could be settled by looking, there would be nothing left to do.

## Sentences that need more information

Something interesting: the sentence

> This shape has four equal sides.

isn't quite a statement on its own, because it depends on which shape you mean. Point at a square and it's true. Point at a long thin rectangle and it's false.

A sentence like this becomes a real statement once you say which shape you're talking about — or once you promise to check *every* shape of some kind, which is a story for module 1.10.

The same thing happens with numbers. *It is bigger than ten* is not true and not false until somebody says what *it* is. Sentences with a gap in them are useful — much of the mathematics ahead of you is written that way — but you cannot prove one until the gap is filled.

## Statements you can't check yet

Sometimes a sentence is a statement — it's definitely either true or false — but nobody knows which. Here is one:

> There are infinitely many pairs of prime numbers that are only $2$ apart (like $11$ and $13$, or $41$ and $43$).

This is called the **Twin Primes conjecture**. A **conjecture** is a statement that people have guessed is true but nobody has proved yet. Twin Primes has been open for more than $150$ years. It's still a statement — it has a definite truth value — we just don't know it.

<Warning title="Not knowing is not the same as not true">
"Nobody has proved it" is a fact about *us*. "It is false" is a fact about the mathematics. Those are different, and it's worth keeping them apart.

People have checked Twin Primes for pairs far larger than anyone can write out by hand, and the pairs keep turning up. That is good evidence and it is not a proof, because the numbers do not stop.
</Warning>

## Why any of this matters

Sorting sentences may feel like a strange way to begin. Here is the reason.

A proof is an argument that a statement is true. If the sentence you started with was a question, or a command, or a phrase with a gap in it, then there is nothing for a proof to be about — no truth value for it to prove. Getting the sentence into the shape *this is either true or false* is the first move in every proof you will write, and it is the move people skip.

So the habit to build now is small: before arguing about a sentence, ask whether it is the kind of sentence that could be true or false at all.

<Aside>
Some conjectures do eventually get answered. One says this: take two counting numbers, cube each of them, and add. You will never land on a number that is itself a cube — and the same is true for fourth powers, fifth powers, and every power after that. That guess is called *Fermat's Last Theorem*, and it stayed a guess for $358$ years before anyone proved it, in $1994$.
</Aside>
