Open your geometry notes at any two-column proof. The left-hand column is headed *Statements*, and every line in it is a sentence that is either true or false.

That is not an accident of formatting. It is the requirement that makes proof possible, and this module makes it precise.

## Definition

A **statement** is a declarative sentence with exactly one **truth value**: true (T) or false (F).

*Exactly one.* Not zero — the sentence must assert something. Not two — it must not be true in one reading and false in another.

### Examples, and non-examples

Statements:

- $\angle 1$ and $\angle 3$ are vertical angles. (T or F, depending on the figure)
- The base angles of an isosceles triangle are congruent. (T)
- Every triangle has two right angles. (F)
- $\sqrt2$ is irrational. (T — Chapter 4 proves it)
- Every even integer at least $4$ is a sum of two primes. (truth value unknown, and still a statement)

Not statements:

- *Is $\triangle ABC$ isosceles?* — a question asserts nothing.
- *Draw the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{AB}$.* — a command.
- $x^2+1$ — an expression. No verb, nothing to be true about.
- *The midpoint of $\overline{AB}$* — a noun phrase. It names a point; it does not claim anything about it.

The last two are the ones that catch people. **An expression is not a statement**, and a proof line that consists only of an expression has said nothing.

## Why the two-column format needs this

Each line of a two-column proof is a statement, and the reason beside it is a justification for that statement's truth. The format cannot work otherwise: you cannot justify a question, and you cannot infer anything from a noun phrase.

So when your book writes

> $m\angle 1 + m\angle 2 = 180°$ — *Linear Pair Postulate*

the left side is a statement with a truth value, and the right side is the ground for believing it. **Every proof in the course has this shape**, and Chapter 1 is about the shapes statements themselves can take.

## Open statements

Consider $x^2 \geq 0$.

On its own this is not a statement — its truth depends on what $x$ is, and no $x$ has been supplied. Such a sentence is an **open statement**, with $x$ a *free* variable.

Two ways to close it:

**Substitute.** $3^2 \geq 0$ is a statement (T).

**Quantify.** *For every real $x$, $x^2\geq0$* is a statement (T). Module 1.10 introduces the notation.

Your geometry theorems are all of the second kind. *"The base angles of an isosceles triangle are congruent"* has a suppressed *for every* in front of it — the sentence is about no particular triangle, and reading it as though it were about the one in the diagram is a genuine error.

<Warning title="Ambiguity is not truth-value-lessness">
*"This triangle is large"* is not a statement, because *large* is not defined. It has no truth value.

*"This triangle has an angle greater than $90°$"* is a statement. Once the triangle is fixed, the answer is determined, whether or not anyone has measured it.

Mathematical definitions exist to make the second kind possible. Your book's insistence on defining *isosceles*, *congruent*, and *between* before using them is what guarantees the statements built from those words have truth values at all.
</Warning>

## Conjectures

Some statements have unknown truth value. They are statements anyway.

**Goldbach's Conjecture.** Every even integer at least $4$ is a sum of two primes. Checked by computer past $4\times10^{18}$; unproved.

**Twin Primes Conjecture.** There are infinitely many primes $p$ with $p+2$ also prime. Zhang and Maynard showed in 2013 that infinitely many prime pairs sit within a bounded gap; whether the gap can be $2$ is open.

A **conjecture** is a statement believed true and not yet proved. Some are eventually settled:

- **Fermat's Last Theorem** — for $n > 2$, the equation $a^n + b^n = c^n$ has no solution in positive integers — was conjectured in 1637 and proved by Wiles in 1995, 358 years later.
- **Pólya's conjecture** — that among $1, 2, \ldots, n$ at least half the numbers have an odd number of prime factors — was believed for decades and disproved in 1958 by a counterexample near $10^{361}$.

<Warning title="Unproved is not the same as untrue">
A statement's truth value does not depend on whether anyone has established it. Goldbach's Conjecture is true or it is false, right now, and our ignorance is a fact about us.

This distinction matters for how you read your own work. *"I could not prove it"* and *"it is false"* are entirely different claims, and only the second requires a counterexample.
</Warning>

## Where Chapter 1 goes

Statements can be built from other statements, and each construction is a module:

- **not** (1.2), **and**, **or** (1.3) — the joining words, and De Morgan's laws relating them (1.4)
- **if–then** (1.5) — the shape almost every theorem takes, and the shape your two-column proofs establish
- converse, inverse, contrapositive (1.7–1.8) — which flips are safe, and which are the errors your book warns about
- **if and only if** (1.9) — why definitions run both ways
- **for all**, **there exists** (1.10–1.12) — the quantifiers, and what evidence each demands

Every one of them appears somewhere in your geometry textbook, usually unnamed. Naming them is what lets you predict the shape of a proof before writing it.

<Aside>
There is a limit to how much can be settled.

Gödel proved in 1931 that any consistent axiom system strong enough to describe arithmetic contains statements that are true and unprovable within it. Not merely unproved — unprovable.

Chapter 7 meets the axioms in question. For now the point is narrower: a sentence being a statement, with a definite truth value, does not guarantee any proof of it exists.
</Aside>
