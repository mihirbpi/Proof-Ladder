Mathematics is the business of assigning truth values to statements via proof. Before we can play that game, we need to fix what counts as a statement.

A **statement** is a sentence that is either true or false. Every statement has exactly one **truth value**: T or F.

Statements:

- The number $7$ is prime. (T)
- The number $8$ is prime. (F)
- $2 + 2 = 4$. (T)
- Every even integer greater than $2$ is the sum of two primes. (unknown, but still T or F)

Non-statements:

- *Is $7$ prime?* — a question, not an claim.
- *Factor $x^2 - 1$.* — a command.
- $x + 1$ — an expression, not a sentence. It has no verb, so nothing about it can be true or false.
- *the biggest whole number* — a phrase, not a sentence. It refers to something but doesn't claim anything about it.

## Two or false, never neither

Two commitments are packed into that definition, and both do work later.

**Every statement has a truth value.** Not "true, false, or undecided" — the third option is not on the table. This is the *law of the excluded middle*, and module 1.3 will name it. It is what licenses a proof that works by ruling out one possibility and concluding the other.

**No statement has two.** A sentence cannot be true and false at once. A pair of statements that would force that is a *contradiction*, and Chapter 1 turns contradictions into a proof technique: derive one, and whatever you assumed must have been wrong.

Neither commitment is obvious, and neither is free. Both are assumptions about how mathematical language behaves, and every proof in this course rests on them.

## Open statements

The sentence $x \geq 1$ has a subtle status. If we plug in $x = 3$ we get "$3 \geq 1$", which is a true statement. If we plug in $x = 0$ we get "$0 \geq 1$", which is a false one. So $x \geq 1$ becomes a statement only once we specify $x$. Sentences of this kind are called **open statements**, and the variable in it is one nothing has been said about yet. Modules 1.10–1.12 will show how to turn open statements into ordinary ones with quantifier (§1.10: the *for every* and *there is* words)s like *for all* and *there exists*.

## Unknown but still a statement

The claim
$$
\text{There are infinitely many primes } p \text{ such that } p + 2 \text{ is also prime}
$$
is called the **Twin Primes conjecture**. It has been open since 1849. Nobody knows whether it is true — yet it is a statement, because it has a definite truth value: the universe of natural numbers either does or doesn't contain infinitely many such pairs. A **conjecture** is a statement mathematicians believe true but have not proved.

<Warning title="Truth is not knowledge">
A statement can have a truth value we don't yet know. That's fine. Our not knowing doesn't leave the statement in some third state; it means the mathematical work of settling it hasn't been done.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Some conjectures become theorems. **Fermat's Last Theorem** says that for any whole number $n > 2$, the equation $a^n + b^n = c^n$ has no solution in positive whole numbers. It was a conjecture from 1637 until Andrew Wiles proved it in 1994. Some conjectures turn out to be false — the disproof is called a *counterexample* (module 1.11).
</Aside>

## Checking is not proving

$2 + 2 = 4$ can be settled by counting. Goldbach's conjecture cannot be settled by counting, because there is no last even integer to stop at. Computers have verified it past $4 \times 10^{18}$, and that is evidence, not proof.

The gap between those two situations is the reason this subject exists. A statement about finitely many things can be checked exhaustively. A statement about infinitely many things needs an argument that handles all of them at once — one that never mentions a particular case, and so cannot run out. Modules 1.10 through 1.12 build that argument form directly.

## The mathematician's job

Every theorem is a statement that has been given a truth value by proof. Every unsolved problem is a statement whose truth value is not yet fixed. Chapter 1 is about how the two get connected — how sentences get promoted from "just a statement" to "true statement, and here's why."

Everything in this chapter serves that promotion. Modules 1.2 through 1.4 fix what compound statements mean, so that there is no ambiguity about what you are proving. Modules 1.5 through 1.9 supply the argument shapes. The last three handle statements about infinitely many cases. Nothing here is preliminary — a proof that gets the statement wrong proves the wrong thing, however sound the reasoning after it.
