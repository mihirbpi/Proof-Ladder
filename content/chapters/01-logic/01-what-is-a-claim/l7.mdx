Mathematics is, at its core, the systematic assignment of truth values to statements. Before any of the machinery of proof is worth writing down, we need to be precise about what counts as a "statement" — because a great deal of the discipline consists of not letting the wrong thing sneak into that category.

## Definition

A **statement** is a declarative sentence that is either true or false. Every statement has exactly one **truth value**, denoted T or F. There is no third possibility, though there are three related things worth *not* confusing with a third possibility: (i) our not yet knowing which of T or F holds, (ii) truth values depending on unspecified variables (open statements), and (iii) statements that are neither provable nor disprovable from a given axiom system (Gödel).

Examples:

- $7$ is prime. (T)
- $\sqrt{2}$ is irrational. (T, proved in §4.7 by contradiction.)
- $\pi$ is a root of the polynomial $x^2 - 3$. (F.)
- Every even integer greater than $2$ is the sum of two primes. (unknown to us; T or F in the mathematical universe.)
- The real numbers can be listed in a sequence, one after another. (F, by Cantor's diagonal argument, §3.8.)

Non-statements:

- *Is $7$ prime?* (interrogative)
- *Solve $x^3 = 27$.* (imperative)
- $x + 1$ (expression, no verb)
- The definite description "the largest prime" (a noun phrase whose referent doesn't exist)

## Open statements

The sentence $p(x)$ = "$x^2 \geq 0$" has a free variable and, as written, no fixed truth value. Such sentences are called **open statements** or *predicates*. They become bona fide statements once every variable is either instantiated ($p(3)$ is T) or quantified ($\forall x \in \mathbb{R}, p(x)$ is T).

Everything in Chapter 1 will assume statements are closed unless they explicitly involve $\forall$ or $\exists$.

The distinction is not bookkeeping. Almost every identity you have used in precalculus is an open statement with a suppressed universal quantifier, and the suppression hides a genuine difference:

$$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1 \qquad\text{(true for every } \theta)$$
$$\sin\theta = \tfrac12 \qquad\text{(true for some } \theta)$$

Both look like equations. The first is an **identity** — a universally quantified statement, and a theorem. The second is an **equation to be solved** — an open statement whose solution set you are asked to compute, and which is neither true nor false until $\theta$ is fixed or quantified.

Your algebra course has been quietly teaching you to read the quantifier off the context. Making it explicit is what allows the two to be treated differently: an identity is proved, an equation is solved, and the difference is exactly which quantifier is in front.

<Warning title="An expression is not a statement">
$x + 1$, $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}$, $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2}$, and $\det A$ are all **expressions**. Each denotes a number (when it denotes anything at all); none asserts anything, and none has a truth value.

The corresponding statements attach a verb:

$$\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1, \qquad \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}, \qquad \det A \neq 0.$$

The distinction matters more than it sounds. A line of a proof consisting of an expression has asserted nothing, and cannot be justified by anything or used to justify anything. When a written argument feels stuck, checking that every line is a sentence is worth doing first.

Note also that $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n$ presupposes convergence — the expression may fail to denote, exactly as "the largest prime" does. Statements about it are therefore doing two jobs at once, and Chapter 6 separates them.
</Warning>

## Conjectures

Two live examples:

- **Twin Primes.** $\exists^\infty p$ prime with $p + 2$ also prime. Recent work (Zhang 2013; Maynard 2014) proves prime pairs at bounded gap; the gap $2$ remains open.
- **Goldbach.** $\forall n \in 2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2},\, \exists p, q$ prime with $n = p + q$. Verified computationally to $4 \times 10^{18}$; unproved.

A **conjecture** is a statement believed true but not yet proved. Some conjectures graduate to theorems (Fermat's Last, proved by Wiles 1995); others get disproved (Euler's sum-of-powers conjecture, disproved by Lander and Parkin 1966 with a single counterexample).

<Warning title="Truth is not knowability">
"Nobody has proved $P$" and "$P$ is not true" are different sentences. Muddling them is a common source of student errors. Nothing in the definition of a statement requires that its truth value be presently accessible to us.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Statements about infinitely many cases can't in general be checked by example. Any amount of numerical verification of Goldbach is consistent with the conjecture being false; a proof is qualitatively different information.
</Aside>

## The mathematician's job, restated

- Every theorem is a statement that has been assigned a truth value by proof.
- Every open problem is a statement whose truth value has not yet been assigned by proof.
- Every axiom is a statement whose truth value is assigned by fiat (Chapter 7).

Every other object in the course — negations, conjunctions, quantifiers, proofs, functions, sets — is a tool for constructing more statements out of existing ones, or for establishing their truth values. Fixing the definition here is the necessary first step.

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
The discipline of asking *is this a statement, and what exactly does it assert* is the one that makes the definitions in calculus survivable.

$$\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = L$$

looks like an equation between two numbers. It is not — it is an abbreviation for a three-quantifier statement (module 1.10), and the left side may denote nothing at all. Reading it as an equation is what makes limit laws feel like arithmetic and then fail: $\lim(f\cdot g) = \lim f\cdot\lim g$ holds **only when both limits exist**, a hypothesis invisible in the notation.

The same applies to $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n$. Manipulating a divergent series as though the symbol denoted a number produces the classic absurdities — the alternating series $1-1+1-1+\cdots$ can be "shown" to equal $0$, $1$, and $\frac12$ by three equally plausible regroupings. What fails is not the algebra but the presupposition that the expression denotes.

**In linear algebra**, the corresponding care is about what a symbol denotes: $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ is a statement about a specific $\mathbf{x}$, an open statement about an unknown one, and a universally quantified claim in *"$A$ is invertible iff $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has a unique solution for every $\mathbf{b}$."* Three readings of one string of symbols, distinguished only by the quantifier in front — and getting the wrong one is how the Invertible Matrix Theorem becomes unmemorable.

*You have matrices, determinants and systems; linear algebra builds its vocabulary on top of them. Above: **Invertible Matrix Theorem** — the list of conditions equivalent to a square matrix having an inverse.*
</WhereThisGoes>
