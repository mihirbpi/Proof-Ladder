## Writing a long sum

A sum with a pattern is written with dots in the middle:
$$
1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n-1).
$$

The dots mean *keep going the same way*. The last term tells you where to stop, and the first two or three establish the pattern. Here the terms are the odd numbers, and the $n$th odd number is $2n-1$ — check it at $n=1$, $n=2$, $n=3$.

The one fact about long sums that every induction proof uses:

> The sum up to the $(k+1)$th term **is** the sum up to the $k$th term, plus one more term.

Peeling off that last term is what connects the $(k+1)$-case to the $k$-case.

## The three steps

**1. State $A(n)$ and $n_0$.** Write the claim precisely and say where it starts.

**2. Base case.** Prove $A(n_0)$.

**3. Inductive step.** Prove that $A(k) \Rightarrow A(k+1)$ for every $k \geq n_0$. The assumption $A(k)$ is the **inductive hypothesis**.

Conclude: $A(n)$ for all $n \geq n_0$.

Step 1 is not ceremony. A large share of failed induction proofs fail because $A(n)$ was never written down, so it is unclear what the base case is checking or what the step is transforming.

## Full proof

> **Proposition.** For all $n \geq 1$: $\;1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n-1) = n^2$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The sum for $k+1$ terms is the sum for $k$ terms plus the next odd number, which is $2(k+1)-1 = 2k+1$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$A(n)$ for all $n \geq 1$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Base at $n=1$ is a one-line check. For the step, start from the left-hand side of $A(k+1)$, split off the last term, substitute the inductive hypothesis, and simplify. The algebra is $k^2 + 2k+1 = (k+1)^2$ and nothing else.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**Statement.** Let $A(n)$ be the claim
$$1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + (2n-1) = n^2.$$
We prove $A(n)$ for all $n \geq 1$.

**Base case.** $A(1)$: the sum is just $2(1)-1 = 1$, and $1^2 = 1$. ✓

**Inductive step.** Let $k \geq 1$ and assume $A(k)$:
$$1 + 3 + \cdots + (2k-1) = k^2. \tag{IH}$$

Now take the sum for $k+1$ terms and split off its final term:
$$
\underbrace{1 + 3 + \cdots + (2k-1)}_{\text{the first } k \text{ terms}} \;+\; \underbrace{\big(2(k+1)-1\big)}_{= \,2k+1}.
$$

By (IH) the bracketed part equals $k^2$, so the whole thing is
$$
k^2 + 2k + 1 = (k+1)^2,
$$
which is exactly $A(k+1)$.

By induction, $A(n)$ holds for all $n \geq 1$.
</Proof>

## Why the step is not circular

The inductive step proves the **conditional** $A(k)\Rightarrow A(k+1)$, quantified over all $k$. Written out, what step 3 establishes is
$$
\text{for every } k \geq n_0: \quad A(k) \Rightarrow A(k+1),
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces". So the line reads: for every $k$ from the base value up: if the claim holds at $k$, then it holds at $k+1$.*

and nothing in that asserts $A(k)$ for any particular $k$. Proving a conditional by assuming its hypothesis is module 1.6's *start from what you're given*, the standard method for every conditional in the course.

The base case supplies truth at one point. The step propagates it. Neither is redundant:

- $A(n)$: "$n = n+1$" satisfies the step and fails the base — and is false.
- $A(n)$: "$n < 5$" satisfies the base and fails the step — and is false for $n \geq 5$.

<Warning title="Direction of the argument">
The commonest structural error is starting from the equation $A(k+1)$ and manipulating both sides until something true appears. That proves nothing unless every step is reversible, and students rarely check.

**Correct shape:** begin with the left-hand side of $A(k+1)$, transform it using the inductive hypothesis, and arrive at the right-hand side. The chain of equalities above runs in one direction only.

A related error: forgetting to *use* the inductive hypothesis. If your step never invokes (IH), you have either proved something directly — in which case induction was unnecessary — or made a mistake.
</Warning>

## Why induction is valid

Suppose the base and step both hold but $A(n)$ fails for some $n$. Then the set of counterexamples is a nonempty set of integers $\geq n_0$, so by **the smallest-number principle** it has a smallest member $m$.

$m \neq n_0$, since $A(n_0)$ holds. So $m-1 \geq n_0$, and because $m$ is the smallest one there $A(m-1)$ holds. But the step then gives $A(m)$ — contradicting $m$ being a counterexample.

So no counterexample exists. Induction and the smallest-number principle are two views of one thing, and module 4.4 already used the smallest-member form to prove the division algorithm.

<Aside>
The base case need not be $n=1$. The claim $2^n > n^2$ is false at $n = 2, 3, 4$ and true from $n = 5$ onward, so its base case is $n_0 = 5$ — and the step then only has to work for $k \geq 5$, which it does. Stating $n_0$ explicitly is part of step 1 for exactly this reason.
</Aside>
