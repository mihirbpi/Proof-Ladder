Here is a list of numbers. Look at it and see if you spot something.

$$0, \quad 3, \quad 15, \quad 63, \quad 255$$

Try sharing each one among $3$ friends.

$0$? Nothing to share, everyone gets nothing. Fair!

$3$? One each. Fair.

$15$? Five each. Fair.

$63$? Twenty-one each. Fair!

$255$? Eighty-five each. Fair!

Every single one shares fairly among $3$.

## Where the list came from

Each number is made from the one before it, like this: **make four copies, then add three more**.

Check it. Start with $0$. Four copies of nothing is nothing, and $3$ more is $3$. ✓

Four copies of $3$ is $12$, and $3$ more is $15$. ✓

Four copies of $15$ is $60$, and $3$ more is $63$. ✓

So each number is **four copies of the one before, plus three**.

## The domino argument

<Proof level="l2">
**Push the first one.** The first number is $0$. Does $0$ share fairly among $3$? Yes — everybody gets nothing, and nothing is left over. ✓

**Check the chain.** Suppose some number in the list shares fairly among $3$.

The next number is four copies of that one, plus three.

Four copies of a fair pile is still fair — just give each friend four times their share!

And adding three more is fair too — one extra for each friend.

Fair plus fair is fair. So the next number shares fairly as well. ✓

Both jobs done. So **every** number in the list shares fairly among $3$, forever.
</Proof>

You just proved something about infinitely many numbers, and you only had to think about two things.

<Aside>
The list has a hidden rule. Each number is one less than a power of four: $1-1=0$, $4-1=3$, $16-1=15$, $64-1=63$, $256-1=255$. So what you proved is: **one less than any power of four always shares fairly among three.** That is a real fact of number theory, and you got it with counters.
</Aside>
