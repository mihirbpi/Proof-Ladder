> **Proposition.** $3 \mid 2^{2n}-1$ for all $n \geq 0$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$m \mid p \iff \exists c \in \mathbb{Z},\ p = mc$ (§4.2). Link: $2^{2(k+1)} = 4\cdot2^{2k}$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$A(n)$ for all $n \geq 0$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Unpack (IH) with a fresh cofactor name, solve for $2^{2k}$, substitute, factor out $3$, cite closure.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
*Base.* $2^0 - 1 = 0 = 3\cdot0$.

*Step.* Fix $k \geq 0$ and assume $2^{2k}-1 = 3x$ with $x \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $2^{2k} = 3x+1$. Then
$$
2^{2(k+1)} - 1 = 4\cdot 2^{2k} - 1 = 4(3x+1)-1 = 12x+3 = 3(4x+1),
$$
and $4x+1 \in \mathbb{Z}$ by closure (§4.1). Hence $A(k+1)$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Variable hygiene">
Two unknowns coexist: the induction index $k$ and the cofactor from the divisibility hypothesis. They must have different names. "$2^{2k}-1 = 3k$" identifies them, which is both false and irreparable.

Also note the rearrangement of (IH) into $2^{2k} = 3x+1$ *before* substituting. Solving the hypothesis for the quantity that actually appears in the target is the standard manoeuvre, and skipping it is where these proofs usually stall.
</Warning>

## The general fact

The proposition is the case $a=4$, $m=3$ of:

> **Proposition.** If $a \equiv 1 \pmod m$ then $a^n \equiv 1 \pmod m$ for all $n \geq 0$; equivalently $m \mid a^n - 1$.

<Proof>
*Base.* $a^0 - 1 = 0$.

*Step.* Suppose $a^k - 1 = mx$, i.e. $a^k = mx+1$, and $a = my+1$. Then
$$
a^{k+1} - 1 = a\cdot a^k - 1 = (my+1)(mx+1) - 1 = m(mxy + x + y),
$$
with $mxy+x+y \in \mathbb{Z}$.
</Proof>

More cleanly: congruence is compatible with multiplication (§4.4), so $a \equiv 1$ gives $a^n \equiv 1^n = 1$ by induction on $n$ — the induction now being hidden inside "compatible with multiplication", where it belongs.

This is the shape of most elementary results here. $m \mid a^n - b^n$ whenever $m \mid a - b$, from the factorisation
$$
a^n - b^n = (a-b)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a^{j}b^{n-1-j},
$$
which is itself proved by induction or by telescoping.

## A second, with an external lemma

> **Proposition.** $6 \mid n^3 - n$ for all $n \geq 0$.

<Proof>
*Base.* $0$.

*Step.* Assume $k^3 - k = 6x$. Then
$$
(k+1)^3-(k+1) = (k^3-k) + 3k(k+1) = 6x + 3k(k+1).
$$
Since $k(k+1)$ is a product of consecutive integers it is even (§1.12), say $2y$; hence the total is $6(x+y)$.
</Proof>

The step required an input — evenness of $k(k+1)$ — not obtainable from the inductive hypothesis. **Inductive steps routinely need external lemmas**, and identifying them belongs in the Discussion rather than being discovered mid-proof.

Without induction: $n^3-n = (n-1)n(n+1)$ is a product of three consecutive integers, so it is divisible by $2$ and by $3$, hence by $6$. Shorter and more informative, and it generalises to $k! \mid$ any product of $k$ consecutive integers — a fact about binomial coefficients in disguise.

<Aside>
The relationship between induction and congruence runs both ways. Modular arithmetic makes many of these results one-liners, but the well-definedness of arithmetic on $\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}$ (§3.1's obligation) and the compatibility of $\equiv$ with $+$ and $\times$ are themselves proved by direct computation, and any statement quantified over exponents ultimately rests on an induction.

Euler's theorem $a^{\varphi(m)}\equiv 1 \pmod m$ for $\gcd(a,m)=1$ generalises the proposition above, and its proof is a counting argument rather than an induction — an illustration that the elementary method is not always the one that scales.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
Divisibility statements indexed by an exponent are the elementary end of a large subject. Fermat's little theorem, Euler's theorem, and the structure of $(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^\times$ as an abelian group are the systematic versions, and they replace induction by group theory: $a^{\varphi(m)} = 1$ because the order of an element divides the order of the group, which is Lagrange's theorem. The same pattern recurs throughout mathematics — a family of facts first proved one at a time by induction, then subsumed by identifying the structure that made them true. In cryptography the direction reverses again: RSA correctness *is* Euler's theorem, and its security rests on the difficulty of computing $\varphi(m)$ without the factorisation of $m$.
</WhereThisGoes>
