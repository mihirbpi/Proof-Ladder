Induction is not only for running totals. This module combines it with the factor work of Chapter 4.

## Spot the pattern

Start at $1$ and keep multiplying by $4$. Then subtract $1$ from each answer.

| Multiplications by 4 | You have | Subtract 1 | Does 3 go into it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $1$ | $0$ | yes — zero $3$s |
| $1$ | $4$ | $3$ | yes — one $3$ |
| $2$ | $16$ | $15$ | yes — five $3$s |
| $3$ | $64$ | $63$ | yes — twenty-one $3$s |
| $4$ | $256$ | $255$ | yes — eighty-five $3$s |

Every single one is a multiple of $3$. That is a guess worth proving.

> **Claim.** Start at $1$, multiply by $4$ as many times as you like, then subtract $1$. The answer is always a multiple of $3$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">"Multiple of $3$" means a whole number of copies of $3$, with nothing left over (module 4.2). And each new number is four times the previous one.</div>
  <div slot="want">The claim for every case.</div>
  <div slot="how">Dominoes. The link is that each number is four times the one before, so the next case is built out of the current one — requirement 2 of module 6.4 is satisfied.</div>
</Discussion>

## The proof

<Proof>
**Base case.** With no multiplications you have $1$, and subtracting $1$ gives $0$. Is $0$ a multiple of $3$? Yes — it is zero copies of $3$, and zero is a whole number. ✓

**Inductive step.** Suppose the claim holds for the case you are on. So the number you have, minus $1$, is some whole number of $3$s.

That means the number you have is *a pile of $3$s, plus one extra*.

Now do one more multiplication by $4$. You now have four copies of everything: four copies of the pile of $3$s, and four copies of the extra one.

Four copies of a pile of $3$s is still a pile of $3$s — just four times as many, and four times a whole number is a whole number.

And four copies of the extra one is $4$, which is one $3$ plus one.

So altogether you have a pile of $3$s, plus one extra.

Subtract $1$. The extra goes away, and only $3$s remain.

So the next answer is a multiple of $3$ as well. ✓

Both conditions hold, so the claim is true for every case.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Keep the two counts apart">
The proof talked about *the count of $3$s in your pile* and *how many times you have multiplied by four*. Those are two completely different numbers.

In the table, after three multiplications the count of $3$s is twenty-one. Three and twenty-one are not the same, and a proof that muddles them is a proof of nothing.

This is module 4.3's fresh-names discipline, and it matters more here because two different kinds of count are in play at once.
</Warning>

## Two techniques, working together

Read the proof again and notice that it is two chapters at once.

**From Chapter 6:** the domino structure — base case, then "if this case works, so does the next."

**From Chapter 4:** the factor template — describe what you have as *this many copies of that*, regroup, and confirm the new count is a whole number.

Neither alone would do it. The factor work cannot handle infinitely many cases; the dominoes have nothing to compute with until the factors are unpacked.

<Aside>
Another one to try the same way: **take any counting number, multiply it by itself, multiply by itself again, then subtract the original number. The answer is always a multiple of $6$.**

Check a few first ($2$ gives $6$; $3$ gives $24$; $4$ gives $60$), then run the dominoes.
</Aside>
