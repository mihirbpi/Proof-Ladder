> **Proposition.** $3 \mid 2^{2n}-1$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$.

Data: $2^{2n}-1 = 0, 3, 15, 63, 255, 1023$ for $n = 0,\ldots,5$ — all multiples of $3$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$m \mid p$ unfolds to $\exists c \in \mathbb{Z},\ p = mc$ (module 4.2). The link between cases: $2^{2(k+1)} = 2^{2k+2} = 4\cdot 2^{2k}$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$A(n)$ for all $n \geq 0$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Unpack (IH) into an equation with a **fresh** name for the leftover factor — $k$ is taken by the induction. Solve for $2^{2k}$, substitute into the $(k+1)$-expression, and factor out $3$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $A(n)$ be the statement $3 \mid 2^{2n}-1$.

**Base case.** $2^{0}-1 = 0 = 3\cdot 0$, so $3 \mid 0$ and $A(0)$ holds.

**Inductive step.** Fix $k \geq 0$ and assume $A(k)$: there exists $x \in \mathbb{Z}$ with
$$
2^{2k} - 1 = 3x, \quad\text{hence}\quad 2^{2k} = 3x+1. \tag{IH}
$$

Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
2^{2(k+1)} - 1 &= 2^{2k+2}-1 = 4\cdot 2^{2k} - 1 \\
&= 4(3x+1) - 1 &&\text{by (IH)}\\
&= 12x + 3 = 3(4x+1).
\end{aligned}
$$
Since $4x + 1 \in \mathbb{Z}$ by closure (module 4.1), $3 \mid 2^{2(k+1)}-1$, i.e. $A(k+1)$.

By induction, $A(n)$ holds for all $n \geq 0$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Two kinds of unknown, two names">
The induction variable is $k$. The factor produced by the divisibility hypothesis is a *different* unknown, and must get its own letter.

Writing "$2^{2k}-1 = 3k$" says that the leftover factor equals the index — false, and it wrecks the step. This is module 4.3's fresh-name discipline, and it bites harder here because two unknowns coexist.

Notice also the small maneuver of rearranging (IH) to $2^{2k} = 3x+1$ before substituting. Solving the hypothesis for the quantity that actually appears in the next case is the standard move, and it is what makes the substitution possible.
</Warning>

## Two templates composed

| From | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Chapter 6 | base case, inductive hypothesis, step |
| Chapter 4 | unpack $\mid$ into an equation, do algebra, factor, cite closure |

Neither is enough alone: divisibility cannot reach infinitely many $n$, and induction has nothing to manipulate until $\mid$ is unpacked. Recognizing that a problem calls for two templates at once is the skill this module exercises.

## A second

> **Proposition.** $6 \mid n^3 - n$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}$.

<Proof>
**Base.** $n=0$: $0 - 0 = 0$, and $6\mid 0$. ✓

**Step.** Assume $n^3-n = 6x$ for some $x \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
(k+1)^3 - (k+1) &= k^3+3k^2+3k+1 - k - 1\\
&= (k^3-k) + 3k^2+3k\\
&= 6x + 3k(k+1).
\end{aligned}
$$
Now $k(k+1)$ is a product of consecutive integers, so even (module 1.12), say $k(k+1) = 2y$ with $y \in \mathbb{Z}$. So the expression is $6x + 6y = 6(x+y)$, and $x + y \in \mathbb{Z}$.

So $6 \mid (k+1)^3-(k+1)$.
</Proof>

Note that the step needed an *external* lemma — that consecutive integers have an even product — which was proved by cases back in module 1.12. Inductive steps frequently need such inputs, and identifying them is part of the Discussion.

## The shape all three proofs shared

Look back at what you just did three times over, because the pattern is the whole module.

1. **Unpack the induction hypothesis** into an equation, with a fresh name for the factor it hands you. Never reuse the induction variable for that name — the two have nothing to do with each other, and the collision is the single most common way these proofs go wrong.
2. **Write down what you want** for $k+1$ and start from the expression itself, not from the answer.
3. **Rearrange until the hypothesis appears**, then substitute it in.
4. **Factor out the divisor**, and check that what is left is a whole number, citing closure.

Step 4 is the one students skip, and skipping it is not a formality: divisibility says the *other* factor is a whole number, and a proof that never checks it has not finished.

<Warning title="Give the factor from the hypothesis its own letter">
If the induction is on $k$ and the hypothesis says $3$ divides $2^{2k}-1$, then $2^{2k}-1 = 3x$ for **some** whole number $x$ — and writing $3k$ instead of $3x$ claims that factor equals the index you are inducting on.

It almost never does. At $k = 3$, $2^6 - 1 = 63 = 3 \cdot 21$, and $21$ is not $3$.

A fresh letter costs nothing and prevents a proof that looks fine and proves nothing.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Both results have one-line proofs if you argue with remainders. For the first: $4$ leaves remainder $1$ on division by $3$, so every power of $4$ does too, and $3 \mid 4^n - 1$. For the second: check $n^3$ and $n$ leave the same remainder, taking division by $2$ and by $3$ separately.

Congruence arithmetic (module 4.4) covers many of these inductions. That is worth knowing — but the inductive proofs come first, both historically and pedagogically, and the congruence toolkit is itself justified by inductive arguments.
</Aside>
