Do the same little step over and over, and a pattern shows up. Here is how to find it and then be sure of it.

## Doubling

Start at $1$ and keep doubling:

| Doublings | You have |
|---|---|
| $0$ | $1$ |
| $1$ | $2$ |
| $2$ | $4$ |
| $3$ | $8$ |
| $4$ | $16$ |
| $5$ | $32$ |

## Step one: guess

Look at the two columns. Can you say what you would have after $6$ doublings, without doubling?

$64$. And after $7$? $128$.

Here is the pattern in words:

> Each doubling makes the pile twice as big as the one before.

Check it: after $3$ doublings you have $2$, then $4$, then $8$. ✓

## Step two: check with dominoes

A guess is only a guess (module 6.1). Let's make sure.

<Proof level="l2">
**Push the first one.** Before you double anything at all, you have $1$ counter. ✓

**Check the chain.** Suppose the rule has worked so far, and your pile is twice the one before it.

Now do one more doubling. That means multiplying by $2$ once more.

So now you have $2$ multiplied by itself $k+1$ times. Exactly what the guess says! ✓

Both jobs done. The guess is right, forever.
</Proof>

## The two jobs are different jobs

Notice what just happened. **Guessing** and **proving** are separate skills.

Guessing means staring at the numbers until you see something. That is creative, and there is no recipe for it.

Proving means running the dominoes. That is careful, and there *is* a recipe — module 6.2 gave it to you.

Grown-up mathematicians do both, in that order, all the time.

<Aside>
Try the same two jobs on halving. Start with $64$ and halve: $64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1$. Guess the pattern. Then push the first domino and check the chain.
</Aside>
