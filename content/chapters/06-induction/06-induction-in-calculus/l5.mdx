So far the claims arrived ready-made and you proved them. Real mathematics is not like that. Usually you have to **find** the claim first, and only then prove it.

This module practises both halves. The loop is:

> 1. **Compute cases** until you have data.
> 2. **Isolate the patterns** — often there is more than one, tangled together.
> 3. **Assemble a formula**, and check it against a case you have not used.
> 4. **Prove it** by induction.

Step 4 is what Chapter 6 has been teaching. Steps 1 to 3 are what nobody teaches, and they are most of the work.

## Job one: gather data

Start at $0$ and apply this rule repeatedly: **double it, then add $1$.**

| Steps | Result |
|---|---|
| $0$ | $0$ |
| $1$ | $1$ |
| $2$ | $3$ |
| $3$ | $7$ |
| $4$ | $15$ |
| $5$ | $31$ |

Stare at $0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 31$ and it is hopeless if you try to see it all at once. The differences are $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$ — doubling — which is a clue but not yet a formula.

## Job two: pull the patterns apart

The trick is to put a **simpler sequence beside it** and compare.

| Steps | $0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | $4$ | $5$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Our list | $0$ | $1$ | $3$ | $7$ | $15$ | $31$ |
| Powers of $2$ | $1$ | $2$ | $4$ | $8$ | $16$ | $32$ |

Every entry is **exactly one less**.

## Job three: assemble and check

> **Conjecture.** After $n$ steps, the result is $2^n - 1$.

Check at a value not used to build the guess. At $n = 6$: the rule gives $2\cdot31 + 1 = 63$, and $2^6 - 1 = 63$ ✓.

<Warning title="A checked conjecture is still a conjecture">
Module 6.1's $n^2+n+41$ survived forty checks and was false.

The check above is worth doing — it catches arithmetic slips before you waste an hour proving something wrong — but it is evidence, not proof. **Steps 1 to 3 produce a candidate; only step 4 produces a theorem.**
</Warning>

## Job four: prove it

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Each result comes from the previous one by doubling and adding $1$ — that recursive rule is the link the induction needs (module 6.4). The hypothesis is that the $k$th result is $2^k - 1$.</div>
  <div slot="want">The $(k+1)$st result is $2^{k+1}-1$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Apply the rule once more to the hypothesis and simplify. Doubling $2^k - 1$ gives $2^{k+1} - 2$, and adding $1$ lands exactly one short of $2^{k+1}$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Write $a_n$ for the result after $n$ steps, so $a_0 = 0$ and $a_{n+1} = 2a_n + 1$.

Let $A(n)$ be the statement $a_n = 2^n - 1$, for $n \geq 0$.

**Base case.** $A(0)$: $a_0 = 0$, and $2^0 - 1 = 1 - 1 = 0$ ✓

**Inductive step.** Let $k \geq 0$ and assume $a_k = 2^k - 1$. Applying the rule,
$$
a_{k+1} = 2a_k + 1 = 2\big(2^k - 1\big) + 1 = 2^{k+1} - 2 + 1 = 2^{k+1} - 1,
$$
which is $A(k+1)$.

By induction, $a_n = 2^n - 1$ for every integer $n \geq 0$.
</Proof>

**The recursive rule was the link.** Module 6.4 listed recursive definitions as one of the three places the connection between consecutive cases comes for free, and this is that case: the rule *tells* you how to get from $k$ to $k+1$, so the inductive step writes itself.

## Why the two-list trick works

<Warning title="Pull patterns apart before combining them">
$0, 1, 3, 7, 15, 31$ is unreadable on its own and obvious once the powers of $2$ sit beneath it.

This is the general technique. When data resists, look for a **simpler sequence hiding inside it** — powers, multiples, squares, factorials — and measure your sequence against that one. Differences and ratios are the two standard things to compute.

Here the ratios were roughly $2$, which pointed at powers of $2$, and the comparison then showed a constant offset of $1$. Two patterns, found separately, then combined into $2^n - 1$.
</Warning>

<Aside>
This module is the one that most resembles what research mathematics is actually like.

The published version of a theorem shows step 4 — a clean proof of a clean statement. Steps 1 to 3, where the statement was found, are almost never written down, which makes mathematics look like a subject where correct claims arrive from nowhere and are then verified.

They do not. Somebody computed a table, noticed something, guessed wrong, adjusted, and guessed again. **The proof is the last thing that happens, not the first**, and a course that only ever hands you the statement is showing you half the subject.
</Aside>
