So far the claims came ready-made and you proved them. Real mathematics is not like that: usually you have to **find** the claim first, and only then prove it.

This module practises both halves. The grown-up version of this module uses calculus; the *method* is identical and does not need it.

## Job one: gather data

Start at $1$ and repeatedly multiply by $3$.

| $n$ (times tripled) | Result |
|---|---|
| $0$ | $1$ |
| $1$ | $3$ |
| $2$ | $9$ |
| $3$ | $27$ |
| $4$ | $81$ |
| $5$ | $243$ |

## Job two: find the pattern

The answer is $3$ multiplied by itself $n$ times — written $3^n$.

That was easy because there was only one pattern to spot. Usually there are several tangled together, and you must isolate them one at a time.

## A harder one, with three patterns at once

Start at $1$ and repeatedly apply the rule: **multiply by the step number, then flip the sign.**

| $n$ | Result |
|---|---|
| $0$ | $1$ |
| $1$ | $-1$ |
| $2$ | $2$ |
| $3$ | $-6$ |
| $4$ | $24$ |
| $5$ | $-120$ |

Staring at $1, -1, 2, -6, 24, -120$ is hopeless if you try to see it all at once. So separate the patterns.

**Pattern 1: the sign.** Plus, minus, plus, minus. It alternates with $n$.

**Pattern 2: the size.** $1, 1, 2, 6, 24, 120$. Each is the previous times $n$: $2 = 1\times2$, $6 = 2\times3$, $24 = 6\times4$, $120 = 24\times5$.

That is $1 \times 2 \times 3 \times \cdots \times n$, which has a name: **$n$ factorial**, written $n!$.

**Putting them together:** the $n$th result is $n!$ with a sign that alternates.

Check $n=3$: $3! = 6$, and the sign should be minus. So $-6$. ✓

## Job three: prove it

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Each result is the previous one times $n$, with the sign flipped. And $(k+1)! = (k+1)\times k!$.</div>
  <div slot="want">The formula holds for every case.</div>
  <div slot="how">Dominoes. The base is $n=0$. The step applies the rule once to the $k$th result and checks the answer matches what the formula predicts for $k+1$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**Base case.** At $n=0$ the result is $1$. And $0!$ is defined to be $1$, with a plus sign. ✓

**Inductive step.** Suppose after $k$ steps the result is $k!$ with the appropriate sign.

Apply the rule once more: multiply by $k+1$ and flip the sign.

Multiplying $k!$ by $k+1$ gives $(k+1)!$ — that is exactly what factorial means.

And flipping the sign once is exactly what the alternating pattern predicts for the next step.

So after $k+1$ steps the result is $(k+1)!$ with the right sign, as claimed. ✓
</Proof>

<Warning title="Isolate the patterns before combining them">
The list $1, -1, 2, -6, 24, -120$ is unreadable as a whole and easy once split into *sign* and *size*.

This is the general technique. When data resists, look for two or three independent patterns and find each separately. Then multiply them together.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The list above is not invented. In a few years, when you meet calculus, you will differentiate the function $1/x$ over and over — and the answers will carry exactly this alternating sign and exactly these factorials. The formula is
$$
(-1)^n \, n! \, x^{-(n+1)},
$$
and the proof is the induction you just did, with an extra pattern for the power of $x$.
</Aside>
