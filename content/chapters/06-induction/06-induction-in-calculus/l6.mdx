Some formulas describe what happens when you apply an operation over and over. Finding them means computing a few cases, spotting patterns, and guessing — and then induction turns the guess into a theorem.

This module is about that two-stage process, because the guessing stage has techniques of its own.

## Repeated substitution

Take the operation *"replace $x$ by $\frac{x}{x+1}$"*, and apply it repeatedly starting from $x$.

$$
f(x) = \frac{x}{x+1}
$$

$$
f\big(f(x)\big) = \frac{\frac{x}{x+1}}{\frac{x}{x+1}+1} = \frac{\frac{x}{x+1}}{\frac{x + (x+1)}{x+1}} = \frac{x}{2x+1}
$$

$$
f\big(f(f(x))\big) = \frac{\frac{x}{2x+1}}{\frac{x}{2x+1}+1} = \frac{x}{3x+1}
$$

Three data points:

| times applied | result |
|---|---|
| $1$ | $\dfrac{x}{x+1}$ |
| $2$ | $\dfrac{x}{2x+1}$ |
| $3$ | $\dfrac{x}{3x+1}$ |

**Conjecture:** applying $f$ $n$ times gives $\dfrac{x}{nx+1}$.

Write $f^{(n)}$ for the $n$-fold composition (module 3.7).

## Proving it

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$f^{(k)}(x) = \frac{x}{kx+1}$ by hypothesis, and $f^{(k+1)} = f\circ f^{(k)}$ — apply $f$ once more to the $k$-th result.</div>
  <div slot="want">$f^{(k+1)}(x) = \frac{x}{(k+1)x+1}$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Substitute the hypothesis into $f$ and simplify the compound fraction. The whole step is one algebraic simplification, as in module 6.3.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $A(n)$ be the statement $f^{(n)}(x) = \dfrac{x}{nx+1}$, for $n\geq1$ and all $x$ where the expressions are defined.

**Base case.** $A(1)$: $f^{(1)}(x) = f(x) = \frac{x}{x+1} = \frac{x}{1\cdot x+1}$. ✓

**Inductive step.** Let $k\geq1$ and assume $f^{(k)}(x) = \frac{x}{kx+1}$. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{(k+1)}(x) &= f\big(f^{(k)}(x)\big)\\
&= f\!\left(\frac{x}{kx+1}\right)\\
&= \frac{\frac{x}{kx+1}}{\frac{x}{kx+1}+1}\\
&= \frac{\frac{x}{kx+1}}{\frac{x + (kx+1)}{kx+1}}\\
&= \frac{x}{(k+1)x+1},
\end{aligned}
$$

which is $A(k+1)$.

By induction, $f^{(n)}(x) = \frac{x}{nx+1}$ for every integer $n\geq1$.
</Proof>

**The composition rule supplied the link.** $f^{(k+1)} = f\circ f^{(k)}$ is what connects consecutive cases, and without a rule of that kind there would be no induction to run (module 6.4).

## Reading three patterns at once

Formulas built by repetition usually have several patterns superimposed, and pulling them apart is the skill. Consider:

| $n$ | value |
|---|---|
| $1$ | $-1$ |
| $2$ | $2$ |
| $3$ | $-6$ |
| $4$ | $24$ |
| $5$ | $-120$ |

Three separate things are happening:

- **Sign** alternates, starting negative: that is $(-1)^n$.
- **Size** is $1, 2, 6, 24, 120$ — the factorials $n!$.
- Together: $(-1)^n\,n!$.

**Handle one pattern at a time.** Trying to guess the whole formula at once is much harder than isolating the sign, then the magnitude, then reassembling — and factorials in particular are easy to miss if you are staring at $1,2,6,24$ hoping for a polynomial.

The factorial identity $(k+1)! = (k+1)\cdot k!$ is what makes the induction step work for any formula containing $n!$. It is the factorial's version of the link.

<Warning title="A guess is a guess">
The table above is five data points. Infinitely many formulas agree with it and diverge afterwards — module 6.1's $n^2+n+41$ gave forty confirmations and then failed.

**Conjecture from data, prove by induction.** The table is how you find the statement; the induction is what makes it true. Neither stage replaces the other, and presenting the table as a proof is exactly the error the chapter exists to correct.
</Warning>

## Compound growth

> **Proposition.** For every integer $n\geq0$ and every real $r > -1$:
> $$(1+r)^n \geq 1 + nr.$$

<Proof>
Let $A(n)$ be that statement, for $n\geq0$.

**Base case.** $A(0)$: $(1+r)^0 = 1$ and $1 + 0\cdot r = 1$, so $1\geq1$. ✓

**Inductive step.** Let $k\geq0$ and assume $(1+r)^k \geq 1+kr$.

Since $r > -1$, the factor $1+r$ is positive, so multiplying the inequality by it preserves the direction (module 4.5):
$$(1+r)^{k+1} = (1+r)^k(1+r) \geq (1+kr)(1+r).$$

Expanding the right side:
$$(1+kr)(1+r) = 1 + r + kr + kr^2 = 1 + (k+1)r + kr^2.$$

Since $k \geq 0$ and $r^2\geq0$, the term $kr^2$ is non-negative, so
$$1+(k+1)r+kr^2 \geq 1+(k+1)r.$$

Chaining the inequalities gives $(1+r)^{k+1}\geq 1+(k+1)r$, which is $A(k+1)$.

By induction, the inequality holds for all $n\geq0$.
</Proof>

Two things worth noting. **The hypothesis $r>-1$ was used**, at the step where the inequality was multiplied — drop it and the direction could flip, and the proof would be wrong (module 4.8's point about tracing hypotheses). And **inequalities are chained**, not manipulated as equations: each $\geq$ was justified separately, and the conclusion follows because $\geq$ is transitive.

This result is **Bernoulli's inequality**, and it says an investment compounding at rate $r$ for $n$ periods always beats simple interest — a fact about money that turns out to be a fact about induction.

<Aside>
The example this module is usually built from is one you cannot run yet.

In calculus there is an operation that turns one rule into another, and applying it over and over to something as plain as $y = 1/x$ produces a table with three patterns running down it at once: a sign that flips each row, a factorial climbing, and an exponent stepping down. Spot the three, assemble the guess, prove it by induction — and the induction step uses $(k+1)! = (k+1)\cdot k!$, the identity from earlier in this module, doing the identical job.

So the table you have already worked with is that example with its calculus stripped off. When you meet the operation, the discovery will be pattern-spotting you have practiced, and the proof will be unchanged.
</Aside>
