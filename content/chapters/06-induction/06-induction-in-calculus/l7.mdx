Every result so far arrived pre-stated. This module runs the full loop: compute data, isolate patterns, conjecture, prove.

**This is the one module in the course that borrows from calculus, and it does so on purpose** — the pattern-finding it demonstrates is hard to show convincingly anywhere else. You are not expected to have taken calculus. Everything the module needs is set out here, and none of it is assumed later.

## What a derivative is, in one paragraph

Take a curve and pick a point on it. The curve has a **steepness** there: how sharply it is climbing or falling at that exact spot, which you can see by laying a ruler against the curve so it just grazes it and reading off the slope of the ruler. That number is the **derivative** of the function at that point, and doing it at every point at once turns the original function $f$ into a new function, written $f'$, whose value at $x$ is the steepness of $f$ at $x$.

Two facts are all this module uses.

**First, one rule for computing them.** For a power of $x$, the derivative is found by bringing the exponent down in front and reducing it by one:
$$
\text{the derivative of } x^m \text{ is } m\,x^{m-1}.
$$

*So the line reads: to differentiate a power, multiply by the old exponent and subtract one from it.* This is the **power rule**, and it is one of the first things a calculus course proves. Here it is simply granted — the module is about the induction, not about where the rule comes from.

**Second, you can do it again.** $f'$ is itself a function, so it has a derivative $f''$, and so on. Write $f^{(n)}$ for the result of differentiating $n$ times, with $f^{(0)} = f$.

## The problem

Let $f(x) = 1/x$ on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ — that is, $x^{-1}$. Find $f^{(n)}(x)$: what you get after differentiating $n$ times.

Each row below is the previous one with the power rule applied once.

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
n & f^{(n)}(x)\\\hline
0 & x^{-1}\\
1 & -x^{-2}\\
2 & 2x^{-3}\\
3 & -6x^{-4}\\
4 & 24x^{-5}\\
5 & -120x^{-6}
\end{array}
$$

## Isolating three patterns

The coefficients $1,-1,2,-6,24,-120$ are unreadable whole. Separate them:

- **Sign:** alternates, so $(-1)^n$.
- **Magnitude:** $1,1,2,6,24,120$ — each the previous times $n$, so $n!$.
- **Exponent:** $-1,-2,-3,\ldots$, so $-(n+1)$.

$$
\boxed{f^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^n\,n!\,x^{-(n+1)}.}
$$

<Warning title="Conjecturing is a separate skill">
Isolate patterns one at a time and multiply them together. Attempting to see a single formula in mixed data is unreliable; identifying sign, magnitude, and exponent independently is nearly mechanical.

Then verify the conjecture at a value *not used to form it* — here $n=5$: $(-1)^5\cdot120\cdot x^{-6} = -120x^{-6}$ ✓ — before committing to a proof.
</Warning>

## Proof

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Power rule $\frac{d}{dx}x^m = mx^{m-1}$ for $m \in \mathbb{Z}$, $x \neq 0$; the recursion $(k+1)! = (k+1)k!$; and $f^{(n+1)} = \big(f^{(n)}\big)'$, which is the link making induction applicable (§6.4).</div>
  <div slot="want">$A(n): f^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^n n! x^{-(n+1)}$ for all $n \geq 0$, $x \neq 0$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Base at $n=0$ using $f^{(0)} = f$. Step: apply the power rule to the (IH) expression and reassemble the sign and factorial — the same two patterns that produced the conjecture.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**Base.** $f^{(0)}(x) = f(x) = x^{-1}$, and $(-1)^0\,0!\,x^{-1} = x^{-1}$. ✓

**Step.** Fix $k \geq 0$ and assume
$$f^{(k)}(x) = (-1)^k\,k!\,x^{-(k+1)}.$$
Differentiating, and using that $(-1)^kk!$ is a constant:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{(k+1)}(x) &= \frac{d}{dx}\Big[(-1)^k k!\,x^{-(k+1)}\Big]\\
&= (-1)^k k!\cdot\big(-(k+1)\big)x^{-(k+2)}\\
&= (-1)^{k+1}\,(k+1)\,k!\;x^{-(k+2)}\\
&= (-1)^{k+1}(k+1)!\,x^{-((k+1)+1)},
\end{aligned}
$$
which is $A(k+1)$.

By induction, $A(n)$ holds for all $n \geq 0$.
</Proof>

Two conventions did real work and are worth naming: $f^{(0)} = f$ made the base case meaningful, and $0! = 1$ made it come out right. Both are forced by the respective recursions ($f^{(n+1)} = (f^{(n)})'$ at $n = 0$; $(k+1)! = (k+1)k!$ at $k=0$) rather than chosen for convenience.

## Reading three patterns at once

The derivatives of $1/x$ are

$$-x^{-2},\quad 2x^{-3},\quad -6x^{-4},\quad 24x^{-5},\ \dots$$

and three independent patterns are superimposed:

- **Sign** alternates, starting negative: $(-1)^n$.
- **Coefficient** runs $1, 2, 6, 24$ — the factorials $n!$.
- **Exponent** runs $-2, -3, -4$: $-(n+1)$.

Assembled: $\ \dfrac{d^n}{dx^n}x^{-1} = (-1)^n\,n!\;x^{-(n+1)}$.

**Isolate one pattern at a time.** Guessing the whole formula at once is much harder, and factorials especially are easy to miss if you are staring at $1, 2, 6, 24$ hoping for a polynomial. Checking the ratio of consecutive coefficients — $2/1$, $6/2$, $24/6$ giving $2, 3, 4$ — identifies a factorial immediately.

The identity $(k+1)! = (k+1)\cdot k!$ is what closes the inductive step for any formula containing $n!$, and it is worth having ready.

## Two more standard inductions

**The exponential.** $\dfrac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{ax} = a^n e^{ax}$.

Base: $n=0$ gives $e^{ax}$ on both sides. Step: differentiating $a^ke^{ax}$ gives $a^k\cdot ae^{ax} = a^{k+1}e^{ax}$. One line — and it is the reason exponentials are the functions that survive being differentiated over and over, which is most of why they turn up wherever something grows or decays.

**The sine.** $\dfrac{d^n}{dx^n}\sin x$ cycles with period four: $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $-\sin x$, $-\cos x$, and back. The induction is a four-case argument on the remainder $n$ leaves on division by $4$ — the sorting of §4.4, deciding which entry of the cycle applies.

That period-four cycle is the same one governing the powers of $i$ (§5.3), and the coincidence is not one: Euler's formula relates the two directly, since differentiating $e^{ix}$ multiplies by $i$.

**Both formulas are worth more than they look.** Late in a calculus course you meet a way of approximating a function by a polynomial whose coefficients are built from its repeated derivatives, and a closed form for the $n$th derivative is exactly what that construction needs as input. The approximations for $e^x$, $\sin x$, and $\cos x$ used in §5.7 rest on the two inductions above.

## The general shape

Repeated application of any operation $T$ gives $a_n = T^n(a_0)$, and $a_{n+1} = T(a_n)$ is exactly the link §6.4 requires. When $T$ acts on a suitable family by a simple multiplier — here $x^{-m}\mapsto -mx^{-m-1}$ — the closed form is a telescoping product, and factorials appear whenever the multiplier is linear in $n$.

Standard instances:
$$
\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{ax} = a^ne^{ax}, \qquad
\frac{d^n}{dx^n}\sin x = \sin\!\left(x + \tfrac{n\pi}{2}\right), \qquad
\frac{d^n}{dx^n}\ln x = (-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!\,x^{-n}.
$$
Each is proved by exactly the induction above, and each is *found* by the same three-pattern separation.

<Aside>
Notice how fast the factorial grows once it appears. The $n$th derivative of $\ln x$ carries $(n-1)!$, so by the tenth derivative the coefficient is $362{,}880$ — the function itself is tame, and its repeated derivatives are not.

That growth is why the polynomial approximations of a calculus course divide by $n!$ at every term. Repeated differentiation manufactures a factorial; the division puts it back. The two cancel, and what is left is a coefficient of ordinary size.

**A pattern in the derivatives is therefore worth finding even when no single derivative is wanted.** It is the whole sequence, not the tenth term, that the later toolkit consumes.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Induction on the number of times you have differentiated is the standard proof technique for every formula of this kind, and it is waiting for you a few weeks into a calculus course.

**The formulas that need it.** $\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{ax} = a^ne^{ax}$, $\frac{d^n}{dx^n}\sin x$ cycling with period four, and $\frac{d^n}{dx^n}x^{-1} = (-1)^n n!\,x^{-(n+1)}$. There is also a rule for the $n$th derivative of a product, and the coefficients in it are the same numbers that appear in the expansion of $(a+b)^n$ from Algebra II — proved, again, by induction.

**The polynomial approximations are built from these.** Later in a calculus course you approximate a function near a point by a polynomial whose $n$th coefficient is the $n$th derivative there, divided by $n!$. Writing one down therefore *requires* a closed form for the $n$th derivative, and the standard cases — $e^x$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$ — are formulas found by pattern-spotting and proved by induction.

That is why §5.7's derivation of Euler's formula was available: the three approximations it used were proved by exactly this route.

**The three-pattern discipline is the practical content.** Sign, factorial, and exponent behave independently, and isolating them one at a time is far easier than guessing the whole formula. The factorial identity $(k+1)! = (k+1)\cdot k!$ is what makes the inductive step close, and recognizing $1, 2, 6, 24, 120$ as factorials rather than as a polynomial is a skill worth having.

</WhereThisGoes>
