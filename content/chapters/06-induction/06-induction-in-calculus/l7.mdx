Every result so far arrived pre-stated. This module runs the full loop: compute data, isolate patterns, conjecture, prove.

## The problem

Let $f(x) = 1/x$ on $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$. Find $f^{(n)}(x)$.

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
n & f^{(n)}(x)\\\hline
0 & x^{-1}\\
1 & -x^{-2}\\
2 & 2x^{-3}\\
3 & -6x^{-4}\\
4 & 24x^{-5}\\
5 & -120x^{-6}
\end{array}
$$

## Isolating three patterns

The coefficients $1,-1,2,-6,24,-120$ are unreadable whole. Separate them:

- **Sign:** alternates, so $(-1)^n$.
- **Magnitude:** $1,1,2,6,24,120$ — each the previous times $n$, so $n!$.
- **Exponent:** $-1,-2,-3,\ldots$, so $-(n+1)$.

$$
\boxed{f^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^n\,n!\,x^{-(n+1)}.}
$$

<Warning title="Conjecturing is a separate skill">
Isolate patterns one at a time and multiply them together. Attempting to see a single formula in mixed data is unreliable; identifying sign, magnitude, and exponent independently is nearly mechanical.

Then verify the conjecture at a value *not used to form it* — here $n=5$: $(-1)^5\cdot120\cdot x^{-6} = -120x^{-6}$ ✓ — before committing to a proof.
</Warning>

## Proof

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Power rule $\frac{d}{dx}x^m = mx^{m-1}$ for $m \in \mathbb{Z}$, $x \neq 0$; the recursion $(k+1)! = (k+1)k!$; and $f^{(n+1)} = \big(f^{(n)}\big)'$, which is the link making induction applicable (§6.4).</div>
  <div slot="want">$A(n): f^{(n)}(x) = (-1)^n n! x^{-(n+1)}$ for all $n \geq 0$, $x \neq 0$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Base at $n=0$ using $f^{(0)} = f$. Step: differentiate the (IH) expression by the power rule and reassemble the sign and factorial — the same two patterns that produced the conjecture.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**Base.** $f^{(0)}(x) = f(x) = x^{-1}$, and $(-1)^0\,0!\,x^{-1} = x^{-1}$. ✓

**Step.** Fix $k \geq 0$ and assume
$$f^{(k)}(x) = (-1)^k\,k!\,x^{-(k+1)}.$$
Differentiating, and using that $(-1)^kk!$ is a constant:
$$
\begin{aligned}
f^{(k+1)}(x) &= \frac{d}{dx}\Big[(-1)^k k!\,x^{-(k+1)}\Big]\\
&= (-1)^k k!\cdot\big(-(k+1)\big)x^{-(k+2)}\\
&= (-1)^{k+1}\,(k+1)\,k!\;x^{-(k+2)}\\
&= (-1)^{k+1}(k+1)!\,x^{-((k+1)+1)},
\end{aligned}
$$
which is $A(k+1)$.

By induction, $A(n)$ holds for all $n \geq 0$.
</Proof>

Two conventions did real work and are worth naming: $f^{(0)} = f$ made the base case meaningful, and $0! = 1$ made it come out right. Both are forced by the respective recursions ($f^{(n+1)} = (f^{(n)})'$ at $n = 0$; $(k+1)! = (k+1)k!$ at $k=0$) rather than chosen for convenience.

## Reading three patterns at once

The derivatives of $1/x$ are

$$-x^{-2},\quad 2x^{-3},\quad -6x^{-4},\quad 24x^{-5},\ \dots$$

and three independent patterns are superimposed:

- **Sign** alternates, starting negative: $(-1)^n$.
- **Coefficient** runs $1, 2, 6, 24$ — the factorials $n!$.
- **Exponent** runs $-2, -3, -4$: $-(n+1)$.

Assembled: $\ \dfrac{d^n}{dx^n}x^{-1} = (-1)^n\,n!\;x^{-(n+1)}$.

**Isolate one pattern at a time.** Guessing the whole formula at once is much harder, and factorials in particular are easy to miss if you are staring at $1, 2, 6, 24$ hoping for a polynomial. Checking the ratio of consecutive coefficients — $2/1$, $6/2$, $24/6$ giving $2, 3, 4$ — identifies a factorial immediately.

The identity $(k+1)! = (k+1)\cdot k!$ is what closes the inductive step for any formula containing $n!$, and it is worth having ready.

## Two more standard inductions

**The exponential.** $\dfrac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{ax} = a^n e^{ax}$.

Base: $n=0$ gives $e^{ax}$ on both sides. Step: differentiating $a^ke^{ax}$ gives $a^k\cdot ae^{ax} = a^{k+1}e^{ax}$. One line, and it is the fact that makes the characteristic-equation method for differential equations work.

**The sine.** $\dfrac{d^n}{dx^n}\sin x$ cycles with period four: $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $-\sin x$, $-\cos x$, and back. The induction is a four-case argument on $n \bmod 4$ — the residue classes of §4.4, deciding which entry of the cycle applies.

That period-four cycle is the same one governing the powers of $i$ (§5.3), and the coincidence is not one: Euler's formula relates the two directly, since differentiating $e^{ix}$ multiplies by $i$.

**Both formulas feed Taylor series.** The coefficient of $x^n$ in a Taylor expansion about $0$ is $\frac{f^{(n)}(0)}{n!}$, so a closed form for the $n$th derivative is precisely what writing down a series requires. The series for $e^x$, $\sin x$, and $\cos x$ used in §5.7 rest on the two inductions above.

## The general shape

Repeated application of any operation $T$ gives $a_n = T^n(a_0)$, and $a_{n+1} = T(a_n)$ is exactly the link §6.4 requires. When $T$ acts on a suitable family by a simple multiplier — here $x^{-m}\mapsto -mx^{-m-1}$ — the closed form is a telescoping product, and factorials appear whenever the multiplier is linear in $n$.

Standard instances:
$$
\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{ax} = a^ne^{ax}, \qquad
\frac{d^n}{dx^n}\sin x = \sin\!\left(x + \tfrac{n\pi}{2}\right), \qquad
\frac{d^n}{dx^n}\ln x = (-1)^{n-1}(n-1)!\,x^{-n}.
$$
Each is proved by exactly the induction above, and each is *found* by the same three-pattern separation.

<Aside>
Higher derivatives are what Taylor series consume: $f(x) = \sum_n \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}(x-a)^n$. Applying the result above at $a=1$ gives
$$
\frac1x = \sum_{n\geq0}(-1)^n(x-1)^n,
$$
the geometric series, convergent for $|x-1|<1$. So the factorial in the derivative and the factorial in the Taylor denominator cancel exactly — which is why $1/x$ has such a clean expansion, and a reminder that the $n!$ in Taylor's theorem is there precisely to undo the $n!$ that repeated differentiation produces.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Induction on the order of differentiation is the standard proof technique for every higher-derivative formula, and you will use it within weeks of starting calculus.

**The formulas that need it.** $\frac{d^n}{dx^n}e^{ax} = a^ne^{ax}$, $\frac{d^n}{dx^n}\sin x$ cycling with period four, $\frac{d^n}{dx^n}x^{-1} = (-1)^n n!\,x^{-(n+1)}$, and the **Leibniz rule**
$$(fg)^{(n)} = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk f^{(k)}g^{(n-k)},$$
whose inductive step uses Pascal's identity $\binom nk + \binom{n}{k-1} = \binom{n+1}{k}$ — the same identity that makes the binomial theorem work, and proved the same way.

**Taylor polynomials are built from these.** The coefficient of $(x-a)^n$ in a Taylor series is $\frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!}$, so writing down a Taylor series *requires* a closed form for the $n$th derivative. Every standard series — for $e^x$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $\ln(1+x)$, $(1+x)^\alpha$ — is a formula found by pattern-spotting and proved by induction.

That is why §5.7's derivation of Euler's formula was available: the three series it used were established by exactly this route.

**The three-pattern discipline is the practical content.** Sign, factorial, and exponent behave independently, and isolating them one at a time is far easier than guessing the whole formula. The factorial identity $(k+1)! = (k+1)\cdot k!$ is what makes the inductive step close, and recognising $1, 2, 6, 24, 120$ as factorials rather than as a polynomial is a skill worth having.

</WhereThisGoes>
