> **Theorem.** For finite $S$ with $|S| = n$, $\ |\mathcal{P}(S)| = 2^n$.

## Inductive proof

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Membership of a fixed $a$ partitions $\mathcal{P}(S)$ into two blocks — an exhaustive, disjoint split by excluded middle.</div>
  <div slot="want">$|\mathcal{P}(S)| = 2^{k+1}$ when $|S|=k+1$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Remove $a$. Subsets avoiding $a$ *are* $\mathcal{P}(S\setminus\{a\})$; subsets containing $a$ biject with it via $A \mapsto A\setminus\{a\}$. Add the two counts.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
*Base.* $|\mathcal{P}(\emptyset)| = |\{\emptyset\}| = 1 = 2^0$.

*Step.* Assume the result for sets of size $k$. Let $|S| = k+1$, fix $a \in S$, $S' = S\setminus\{a\}$.

Partition $\mathcal{P}(S) = \mathcal{U}\sqcup\mathcal{V}$ with $\mathcal{U} = \{A : a\notin A\}$, $\mathcal{V} = \{A : a \in A\}$.

$\mathcal{U} = \mathcal{P}(S')$, so $|\mathcal{U}| = 2^k$ by (IH).

$p : \mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{P}(S')$, $A \mapsto A\setminus\{a\}$, has two-sided inverse $B \mapsto B\cup\{a\}$, hence is a bijection (§3.9); so $|\mathcal{V}| = 2^k$.

Thus $|\mathcal{P}(S)| = 2^k+2^k = 2^{k+1}$.
</Proof>

The step transferred a count along a bijection rather than recounting — §3.8's principle in use.

## Bijective proof

<Proof title="Second proof">
For $A \subseteq S$ define its **characteristic function** $\chi_A : S \to \{0,1\}$ by $\chi_A(x) = 1$ iff $x \in A$.

The map $A \mapsto \chi_A$ is a bijection $\mathcal{P}(S)\to\{0,1\}^S$, with inverse $f \mapsto f^{-1}(\{1\})$ — the pre-image, in the sense of §3.3.

A function $S \to \{0,1\}$ is specified by an independent binary choice at each of $n$ points, so $|\{0,1\}^S| = 2^n$.
</Proof>

This explains the notation $2^S$ for the power set: it is $\{0,1\}^S$, and the cardinality formula $|Y^X| = |Y|^{|X|}$ makes $|2^S| = 2^{|S|}$ automatic. The bijection also transports structure — $\chi_{A\cap B} = \chi_A\chi_B$ and $\chi_{\overline A} = 1 - \chi_A$ — so $\mathcal{P}(S)$ and $\{0,1\}^S$ match operation for operation (§2.7), not merely element for element.

<Warning title="Level discipline">
Elements of $\mathcal{P}(S)$ are *sets*. For $S = \{a,b\}$: $a \in S$, $\{a\}\in\mathcal{P}(S)$, $\{a\}\subseteq S$, $\{\{a\}\}\subseteq \mathcal{P}(S)$ — and $a \notin \mathcal{P}(S)$.

Both $\emptyset$ and $S$ belong to $\mathcal{P}(S)$; omitting either destroys the count. And $\mathcal{P}(\emptyset) = \{\emptyset\}$ has one element, not zero — the distinction §2.1 insisted on.
</Warning>

## Refinement by size

Splitting by cardinality gives
$$
2^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk,
$$
since $\binom nk$ counts the $k$-element subsets. The same partition argument used above, applied to $k$-subsets, yields Pascal's rule
$$
\binom{n}{k} = \binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}
$$
— subsets containing $a$ versus not — and hence the binomial theorem by induction. The theorem of this module is the binomial theorem evaluated at $x=y=1$.

## Why the split is legitimate

The inductive step divided the subsets of $S$ into those containing a fixed $a$ and those not, then claimed each group has $2^k$ members. Two conditions had to hold for the final addition, and both were checked.

**Disjoint.** No subset is in both groups, since a subset either contains $a$ or does not — the excluded middle of §1.3, and the reason the two counts do not overlap.

**Exhaustive.** Every subset is in one group, for the same reason.

Adding the sizes of overlapping groups double-counts; adding the sizes of groups with a gap undercounts. Neither check is automatic, and stating them is part of the proof.

**The bijection also needed verifying.** The map $T \mapsto T\cup\{a\}$ from Group 1 to Group 2 is injective because $T$ is recovered by removing $a$, and surjective because every subset containing $a$ arises this way. Only then does "each group has $2^k$ members" follow from the hypothesis, which was a statement about the subsets of a $k$-element set.

**Counting by pairing** — establishing a bijection with something already counted — is more powerful than counting one by one, and it is the only method that survives into the infinite case.

## The infinite case

> **Cantor's theorem.** For every set $S$, there is no surjection $S \to \mathcal{P}(S)$.

<Proof>
Let $f : S \to \mathcal{P}(S)$ and put $D = \{x \in S : x \notin f(x)\} \in \mathcal{P}(S)$.

If $D = f(d)$ for some $d \in S$, then $d \in D \iff d \notin f(d) = D$ — a contradiction. So $D \notin \operatorname{im}(f)$ and $f$ is not surjective.
</Proof>

This is **not** an induction and cannot be made one: no successor structure is available on an arbitrary set. It is a diagonal argument, the same construction as Russell's paradox (§2.1) and Cantor's uncountability proof (§3.8), used constructively rather than destructively.

The finite theorem says $2^n > n$; Cantor's says the strict inequality survives to every cardinal. So there is no largest set and no largest infinity.

<Aside>
$|\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})| = |\mathbb{R}|$, and whether any cardinal lies strictly between $|\mathbb{N}|$ and $|\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})|$ is the continuum hypothesis — and Gödel and Cohen showed the standard axioms of set theory can neither prove nor refute it (§3.8). The finite case is a two-line induction; its infinite analogue is undecidable from the standard axioms. Few contrasts illustrate better that finite combinatorics and set theory are different subjects.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Counting arguments are the everyday application of induction, and the two proofs in this module illustrate the choice this chapter keeps returning to.

**The induction verifies; the choice argument explains.** Building a subset by making one yes/no decision per element gives $2^n$ immediately, and it says *why* the base is $2$. The induction confirms the formula and does not.

The explanatory version also generalises: give each element three options instead of two and the count is $3^n$, with no proof to redo. Rewriting the induction would be real work.

**In calculus, the binomial coefficients are what you will actually need.** $\binom nk$ counts $k$-element subsets of an $n$-set, and Pascal's identity
$$\binom nk + \binom{n}{k-1} = \binom{n+1}{k}$$
has exactly this module's proof: split the subsets of an $(n+1)$-set according to whether they contain a chosen element.

That identity is the inductive step of the binomial theorem, which in turn gives the expansion of $(1+x)^n$, the derivative of $x^n$ from first principles, and the general binomial series $(1+x)^\alpha$ for non-integer $\alpha$ — one of the standard Taylor series.

</WhereThisGoes>
