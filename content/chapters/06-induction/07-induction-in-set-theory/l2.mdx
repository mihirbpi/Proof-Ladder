How many different bags can you pack from a pile of toys? Let's count.

| Toys | Bags you could pack | How many |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | just the empty bag | $1$ |
| $1$ | empty, or the bear | $2$ |
| $2$ | empty, bear, truck, both | $4$ |
| $3$ | (go and count!) | $8$ |

$1, 2, 4, 8$. It doubles every time.

## Why it doubles

Here is the reason, and it is lovely.

Suppose you already know all the bags you can make from your old toys. Now somebody hands you **one new toy**.

Every bag you make now has to answer one question about that new toy: **in or out?**

So take any old bag. You can make *two* new bags from it — one with the new toy dropped in, one without.

Old bags, doubled. That is why the count doubles.

## The domino argument

<Proof level="l2">
**Push the first one.** With $0$ toys, there is exactly $1$ bag — the empty one. ✓

**Check the chain.** Suppose you know how many bags you can make from some pile of toys.

Add one more toy. Every old bag turns into two: with the new toy, and without.

So the count doubles. ✓

Both jobs done. So the count keeps doubling forever.
</Proof>

## Do not forget the empty bag

The empty bag is the one people forget, and leaving it out breaks the pattern.

With $2$ toys you get $4$ bags, and one of them is empty. Drop it and you have $3$ — and $3$ is not double anything nice.

Module 2.1 said an empty box is still a box. This is why it matters.

<Aside>
There is a second way to see it. Line your toys up and, for each one, say "in" or "out." Every list of in-and-out choices gives a different bag.

With $3$ toys: the first toy gives $2$ ways. Each of those splits into $2$ when you decide about the second toy — that is $4$. Each of *those* splits into $2$ again for the third — that is $8$.

Same answer, no dominoes needed. Two proofs of one fact!
</Aside>
