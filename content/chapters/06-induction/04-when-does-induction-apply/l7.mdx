Two requirements, both substantive.

## An index with a successor

The statements must be indexed by $\{n \in \mathbb{Z} : n \geq n_0\}$. What is being used is not merely that the index set is infinite. Two things are needed: **every entry has an immediate next one**, and **every non-empty collection of entries has a smallest member**.

$\mathbb{R}$ has neither, by density (§4.6). So $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ x^2 \geq 0$ is not an induction candidate; it is an arbitrary-element argument with a case split (§1.12).

<Warning title="Look for a hidden integer parameter">
Bernoulli's inequality $\forall x \geq -1,\ (1+x)^n \geq 1+nx$ *is* proved by induction — on $n$, with $x$ fixed but arbitrary throughout. The presence of a real variable is irrelevant; what matters is whether some integer indexes the family.

The other way round, a statement may have an integer index and still resist: see below.
</Warning>

## A usable link

$A(k)$ must materially assist $A(k+1)$. The link is present exactly when the object indexed by $n+1$ is *constructed* from the object indexed by $n$:
$$
\sum^{n+1} = \sum^n + a_{n+1},\quad a^{n+1} = a\cdot a^n,\quad (n+1)! = (n+1)n!,\quad f^{(n+1)} = \big(f^{(n)}\big)'.
$$

**Recursive definition and inductive proof are the same structure viewed from opposite ends.** Chapter 7 makes this exact: $+$ and $\times$ are *defined* by recursion on the successor, and every property of them is *proved* by induction on the same successor. The recursive-definition principle, which licenses definition by recursion, is itself proved from the induction axiom.

Where the link fails, the index does not help. "The $n$th prime is odd" has an integer index and no usable relation between $p_k$ and $p_{k+1}$; assuming $A(k)$ contributes nothing, and no amount of cleverness in the step will fix that.

## Strong induction

> Assume $A(n_0),\ldots,A(k)$; prove $A(k+1)$.

<Proof title="Equivalence with ordinary induction">
Let $B(n)$ be the statement *"$A(m)$ holds for every $m$ from $n_0$ up to $n$"*. The strong hypothesis is exactly $B(k)$, and $B(k)\Rightarrow A(k+1)$ gives $B(k)\Rightarrow B(k+1)$. Ordinary induction on $B$ gives $\forall n\, B(n)$, so $\forall n\, A(n)$.
</Proof>

Indicated when $n+1$ decomposes into cases far below $k$:

> **Existence of prime factorization.** Every $n \geq 2$ is a product of primes. If $n$ is prime, done. Otherwise $n = ab$ with $2 \leq a,b < n$; both have factorizations by the strong hypothesis, and concatenating them factors $n$.

Ordinary induction cannot do this, since $a$ and $b$ bear no relation to $n-1$. Note that *uniqueness* of the factorization is a separate matter requiring Euclid's lemma (§4.3), not induction.

The Fibonacci recurrence $F_{n+1} = F_n + F_{n-1}$ likewise needs two previous cases, so two base cases — an easy and common oversight.

## Choosing what to induct on

A problem often offers several candidate indices, and the choice determines whether the proof is short or impossible.

**A claim about pairs.** For a statement $A(m,n)$, you may induct on $m$ with $n$ fixed, on $n$ with $m$ fixed, or on $m+n$. The last reduces a two-dimensional family to a single chain and is often the only one that works, because each step decreases the total and both coordinates are free to move.

**A claim about a construction.** For polygons, induct on the number of sides; for grids of numbers, on their size; for expressions, on the number of operations. The index should be whatever decreases when you remove one piece.

**When an induction stalls, change the index before changing the algebra.** A step that will not close is usually a sign that the wrong quantity is being decreased, and no amount of manipulation repairs that.

<Warning title="The link must be constructed, not assumed">
The most common failure is asserting a link that does not exist. *"Remove a vertex from an $(n+1)$-gon to get an $n$-gon"* is true for convex polygons and false in general — a non-convex polygon can lose its polygon status under the same operation.

Establishing the link is frequently the whole difficulty of the proof, and a step that begins "plainly the $(k+1)$ case contains the $k$ case" deserves a close look.
</Warning>

## The single principle underneath

Both forms are instances of a single principle.

> **General induction.** Suppose the objects in $S$ come with a notion of *simpler than*, and that no object can be simplified forever — every chain of simplifications stops. If, for every $x$ in $S$, $A$ holding at everything simpler than $x$ forces $A$ at $x$, then $A$ holds throughout $S$.

Note there is **no separate base case**. The simplest objects have nothing below them, so the hypothesis is vacuously satisfied for them (§1.5) and the implication delivers $A$ directly. Ordinary induction is the case where *simpler than* means *the previous integer*; strong induction is the case where it means *any smaller integer*.

**Induction on how a thing is built** is the case where the objects are assembled from smaller ones, and *simpler than* means *is a piece of*. To prove a property of every expression, verify it for the atoms and check that each way of building preserves it. Termination arguments for recursive programs are the same principle: exhibit a quantity that strictly decreases at each call and cannot decrease forever.

The same principle extends past $\mathbb{N}$ to longer collections that still have that smallest-member property, which is how induction reaches beyond the counting numbers.

<Aside>
The no-infinite-descent condition is exactly what rules out circular justification. Set theory assumes a rule of the same shape — you can never have an endless chain of sets each inside the last — and that rule is what forbids a set from being a member of itself.

A practical corollary: to prove termination of a recursive procedure, find a strictly decreasing measure that strictly decreases. There is a famous rule that always finishes despite growing enormously fast, because its arguments decrease in the lexicographic order on $\mathbb{N}^2$ — which also admits no infinite descent, though it does not look like $\mathbb{N}$.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

The two conditions — an integer index and a link between cases — are what determine whether induction is even the right tool, and calculus is full of claims that fail the first.

**Claims about all real numbers are not induction candidates.** *For all $x$, $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$* has infinitely many cases and no chain to run: $\mathbb{R}$ has no smallest member and no next element (§4.6). Attempting induction there is a category error, and the correct technique is a direct proof from the definitions.

**Claims about all $n$ usually are.** Formulas for $\frac{d^n}{dx^n}$, closed forms for $\sum_{j=1}^n$, statements about the $n$th term of a recursively defined sequence, and inequalities like $2^n > n^2$ for $n\geq5$ all carry an integer index and a visible link.

**The link is where the difficulty lives.** For $\frac{d^n}{dx^n}$, the link is *differentiate once more* — take the steepness of the steepness. For a sum, the link is *add the next term*. For a recursively defined sequence, the recursion **is** the link — which is why recursive definitions and induction go together, and why §7.6 defines addition recursively and then proves its properties inductively.

**Strong induction handles irregular links.** Some claims about $n$ need several earlier cases rather than the immediately preceding one. Fibonacci-type recurrences reach back two steps and need two base cases; the fact that every integer above $1$ has a prime factor reaches back an unpredictable distance and needs strong induction.

</WhereThisGoes>
