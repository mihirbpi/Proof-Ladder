> **Proposition.** $\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}(2j-1) = n^2$ for all $n \geq 1$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$\sum_{j=1}^{k+1} = \sum_{j=1}^k + a_{k+1}$ with $a_{k+1} = 2k+1$; and $(k+1)^2 = k^2 + 2k+1$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$A(n)$ for all $n \geq 1$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Standard three steps. The only substantive content is the algebraic identity in the step; everything else is form.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $A(n)$ be $\sum_{j=1}^n(2j-1) = n^2$.

*Base.* $A(1)$: both sides are $1$.

*Step.* Fix $k \geq 1$, assume $A(k)$. Then
$$
\sum_{j=1}^{k+1}(2j-1) = \sum_{j=1}^{k}(2j-1) + (2k+1) \overset{\text{(IH)}}{=} k^2+2k+1 = (k+1)^2.
$$

By induction, $A(n)$ for all $n \geq 1$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Forward direction only">
The step must run from the left-hand side of $A(k+1)$ to its right-hand side. Manipulating both sides of the target — "subtract $2k+1$ from both sides and obtain (IH)" — assumes the conclusion, and is valid only if reversibility is separately established (§1.9). The forward chain has no such obligation.

The related failure is an unused (IH): if the step never invokes it, the argument is not an induction, and is either a direct proof or wrong.
</Warning>

## Three proofs, three kinds of information

**Induction**, above: certifies the identity, explains nothing about why it is true.

**Bijective / geometric.** Odd numbers are the gnomons between consecutive squares: an $(k+1)\times(k+1)$ square minus a $k\times k$ square is $k + k + 1 = 2k+1$ cells. Telescoping,
$$
\sum_{j=1}^{n}(2j-1) = \sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(j^2 - (j-1)^2\big) = n^2 - 0^2 = n^2.
$$
This is a **bijective proof** in the combinatorial sense: it exhibits an explicit decomposition of the $n\times n$ square into the pieces being summed, so the identity becomes a statement that two ways of counting the same set agree.

**Algebraic.** $\sum_{j=1}^n (2j-1) = 2\sum j - n = 2\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2} - n = n^2$, given the triangular-number formula.

The three are not redundant. The telescoping proof generalises immediately: any identity of the form $\sum (f(j)-f(j-1)) = f(n)-f(0)$ is proved the same way, and it is the discrete analogue of the fundamental theorem of calculus. The bijective proof generalises in a different direction, to combinatorics, where "find a bijection" is the preferred form of explanation.

## Power sums

$$
\sum_{j=1}^n j = \frac{n(n+1)}{2},\quad
\sum_{j=1}^n j^2 = \frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6},\quad
\sum_{j=1}^n j^3 = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2.
$$

Each is a routine induction *once stated*, and none is suggested by induction. Finding them is the hard part, and the general answer — **Faulhaber's formula**, expressing $\sum_{j=1}^n j^p$ as a degree-$(p+1)$ polynomial in $n$ with Bernoulli-number coefficients — is a genuinely different kind of result.

Note the third identity: $\sum j^3 = (\sum j)^2$. A striking coincidence, provable by induction in four lines, and again with a wordless proof by dissecting a square into gnomons of cubes.

<Aside>
Telescoping is the discrete fundamental theorem of calculus. Writing $\Delta f(j) = f(j) - f(j-1)$, the statement $\sum_{j=1}^n \Delta f(j) = f(n)-f(0)$ is the exact analogue of $\int_a^b F' = F(b)-F(a)$, and the analogy runs deep: summation by parts mirrors integration by parts, falling factorials play the role of powers, and the finite-difference calculus built on them is the natural setting for both combinatorics and numerical analysis.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
That one theorem admits an inductive, a telescoping, and a bijective proof is characteristic rather than exceptional, and the preference for bijective arguments in combinatorics is principled: a bijection is a *reason*, an induction only a *certificate*. The distinction sharpens in modern work — the Rogers–Ramanujan identities were known analytically for decades before Garsia and Milne produced a bijection, which was regarded as a genuine advance despite proving nothing new. Meanwhile the telescoping viewpoint became a subject of its own: Gosper's algorithm decides whether a given hypergeometric sum telescopes, and the Wilf–Zeilberger method mechanically produces certificates for a vast class of binomial identities — turning the discovery of proofs like the one above into something a computer does.
</WhereThisGoes>
