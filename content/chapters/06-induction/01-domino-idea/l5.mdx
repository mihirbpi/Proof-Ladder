Some claims are really infinitely many claims at once.

> For every integer $n \geq 1$: $\ 1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots + n = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$.

That is a separate statement for each $n$. You could check $n = 1$, then $n=2$, then $n=3$, forever, and never finish. **Induction** is the technique that finishes.

## Dominoes

Stand a row of dominoes on end. To be sure every one falls, you need two things:

1. The **first** one falls.
2. **Whenever** a domino falls, it knocks over the next.

<Figure alt="A row of dominoes, the first three fallen and each tipping into the next, continuing to the right without end." caption="Both conditions together force the whole row; neither alone does.">

</Figure>

Neither alone is enough. Push the first with the dominoes spaced too far apart and only one falls. Space them perfectly and never push, and none falls.

Together they force the lot. And notice you do not have to *watch* them fall — the two conditions guarantee the outcome without any checking.

Now replace "domino $n$ falls" with "statement $A(n)$ is true", and you have induction:

> If $A(1)$ is true, and if $A(k)$ being true always forces $A(k+1)$ to be true, then $A(n)$ is true for every $n \geq 1$.

Module 6.2 turns this into a written template. This module is about why you need it.

## Why not just check?

Because checking cases proves nothing about the cases you did not check, and the danger is not hypothetical.

Consider
$$
f(n) = n^2 + n + 41.
$$

Compute:

| $n$ | $f(n)$ | prime? |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $41$ | ✓ |
| $1$ | $43$ | ✓ |
| $2$ | $47$ | ✓ |
| $3$ | $53$ | ✓ |
| $4$ | $61$ | ✓ |
| $5$ | $71$ | ✓ |

Every one prime. Keep going and it stays prime — for $n = 10$, for $n = 25$, for **every value up to $n = 39$.** Forty consecutive confirmations.

Then:
$$
f(40) = 1600 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41 \times 41.
$$

Not prime.

<Warning title="Forty cases is not a proof, and neither is forty million">
The pattern above fails at the first case anybody would have stopped checking.

And it can be worse. There are statements about integers whose first counterexample is larger than the number of atoms in the observable universe — verified by computer for every case that will ever be tested, and false.

**A universal claim is refuted by one counterexample and established only by an argument covering every case** (module 1.11). Checking is how you *find* things worth proving. It is never how you prove them.

In fairness, $f(40)$ was predictable in hindsight: $f(41) = 41 \cdot 43$ too, since every term of $41^2 + 41 + 41$ carries a factor of $41$. That is an argument, and it is the kind of thing checking cannot give you.
</Warning>

## What induction is not

**It is not circular.** The step does not assume $A(k)$ is true. It proves the *conditional* "if $A(k)$ then $A(k+1)$", which is a statement you can establish without knowing whether $A(k)$ holds — exactly as module 1.5's truth table allows. Module 6.2 returns to this, because it is the objection everyone raises.

**It is not just for sums.** Module 6.4 catalogues what it works on: divisibility, inequalities, counting problems, and statements about objects built in stages.

**It does not work on the rationals.** Induction steps from $n$ to $n+1$, and module 4.6 showed there is no *next* rational to step to. The technique needs a first case and a next case, and only some sets have them.

<Aside>
The domino picture is honest but it hides a real assumption.

Ask why *all* the dominoes fall. Because the first falls, and each knocks the next — but stringing those together into "therefore all of them" is itself a principle, and it is not provable from more basic facts about the integers.

It is an **axiom** of the number system, and Chapter 7 states it as one. That is a genuinely interesting situation: the most useful proof technique in this chapter is not a theorem at all, but part of the definition of what the integers are.

For now, use it. Chapter 7 says where it comes from.
</Aside>
