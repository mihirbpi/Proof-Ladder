Divisibility is existential, so every proof follows one template. What is worth attention is not the template but the two places it can go wrong: the integrality check, and the plausible statements that turn out to be false.

## Template

1. **Unpack** each hypothesis $a \mid b$ as $b = ak$, with a fresh name for the other factor.
2. **Compute.**
3. **Factor** out the target divisor.
4. **Verify** the factor left over is an integer, citing closure (§4.1).

Step 4 is the load-bearing one. In $\mathbb{Q}$ every nonzero element divides every element, so the entire subject rests on the leftover factor being required to be an *integer*.

## The core propositions

> **Proposition.** For $a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{Z}$:
> (i) $a \mid b \wedge b \mid c \Rightarrow a \mid c$;
> (ii) $a \mid c \wedge b \mid d \Rightarrow ab \mid cd$;
> (iii) $a \mid b \wedge a \mid c \Rightarrow a \mid (bx + cy)$ for all $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}$;
> (iv) $a \mid b \vee a \mid c \Rightarrow a \mid bc$.

<Proof>
(i) $b = aj$, $c = bk$ give $c = a(jk)$ with $jk \in \mathbb{Z}$.

(ii) $c = aj$, $d = bk$ give $cd = (ab)(jk)$ with $jk \in \mathbb{Z}$.

(iii) $b = aj$, $c = ak$ give $bx + cy = a(jx + ky)$ with $jx+ky \in \mathbb{Z}$.

(iv) The hypothesis is a disjunction; the two cases are interchanged by swapping the names $b$ and $c$, which leaves the conclusion $a \mid bc$ invariant since $bc = cb$. **Without loss of generality**, assume $a \mid b$; then $b = ak$ and $bc = a(kc)$ with $kc \in \mathbb{Z}$.
</Proof>

Statement (iii) deserves emphasis: it says the set $\{n : a \mid n\} = a\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under integer linear combinations, i.e. is closed under adding its own members and under multiplying by anything outside. That single structural fact drives the Euclidean algorithm and, with it, **Bézout's identity**: for any $a,b$ there are integers $x,y$ with $ax + by = \gcd(a,b)$. Everything downstream rests on it.

<Warning title="Two disciplines">
**Fresh names.** From $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, the two leftover factors have nothing to do with each other; reusing one letter asserts they are equal and proves a weaker statement.

**WLOG has a license.** It is legitimate exactly when the omitted case follows from the treated one by a relabeling under which hypotheses and conclusion are invariant — and you must say what the relabeling is. Asserted without justification, WLOG is how a case gets silently dropped, and it is the most common structural defect in submitted proofs.
</Warning>

## The false converse, and its repair

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\nmid$ reads "does not divide".*

$$
a \mid bc \;\not\Rightarrow\; a \mid b \ \vee\ a\mid c.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces", $\vee$ is "or", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

Counterexample: $4 \mid 12 = 2\cdot 6$, yet $4 \nmid 2$ and $4 \nmid 6$. The divisor $4 = 2^2$ draws one factor of $2$ from each of $b, c$.

> **Euclid's lemma.** If $p$ is prime and $p \mid bc$, then $p \mid b$ or $p \mid c$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$p$ prime, $p \mid bc$. Suppose $p \nmid b$ — we must reach $p \mid c$. Since $p$ is prime and does not divide $b$, we have $\gcd(p,b) = 1$, as the only divisors of $p$ are $1$ and $p$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$p \mid c$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Bézout: $\gcd(p,b) = 1$ gives integers $x,y$ with $px + by = 1$. Multiply by $c$ and apply (iii) to the two terms, each of which $p$ divides.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose $p \mid bc$ and $p \nmid b$. The divisors of $p$ are $\pm1, \pm p$, and $p \nmid b$, so $\gcd(p,b) = 1$. By Bézout's identity there exist $x,y \in \mathbb{Z}$ with
$$px + by = 1.$$
Multiplying by $c$:
$$pcx + bcy = c.$$
Now $p \mid pcx$ trivially, and $p \mid bcy$ since $p \mid bc$. By (iii), $p$ divides the linear combination $pcx + bcy = c$.

Hence $p \mid c$, and the lemma is proved.
</Proof>

Note that Euclid's lemma is *not* a triviality about primes; its proof consumes Bézout, which consumes the division algorithm (§4.4). The chain is
$$
\text{division algorithm} \Rightarrow \text{Euclidean algorithm} \Rightarrow \text{Bézout} \Rightarrow \text{Euclid's lemma} \Rightarrow \text{unique factorisation}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

Each arrow is short; the sequence is the whole of elementary number theory's foundation, and it is worth being able to recite.

<Aside>
Unique factorisation is often taught as obvious. It is not. Existence of a factorisation follows by strong induction (Chapter 6); *uniqueness* is exactly where Euclid's lemma is spent, and in number systems where that lemma fails, uniqueness fails with it. In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, $2$ is irreducible but $2 \mid 6 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ while dividing neither factor — so $2$ is not prime there, and $6$ has two genuinely different factorisations.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

The linear-combination theorem — *if $a$ divides $b$ and $c$, then $a$ divides $bx+cy$* — has a shape you will meet again immediately, and recognizing it saves relearning.

**In calculus, linearity of the derivative and the integral is the same fact.**
Writing $\int$ for the integral — the operation you are meeting this year as the reverse of differentiation:
$$\frac{d}{dx}\big(af + bg\big) = af' + bg', \qquad \int (af+bg) = a\int f + b\int g.$$

So a property preserved by differentiation is preserved by any combination of functions having it — which is why, having differentiated $x^n$, you can differentiate every polynomial without further work.

**The same template runs on polynomials.** Everything in this module used only three facts about $\mathbb{Z}$: you can add, you can multiply, and the product of two things stays in the system. Polynomials satisfy all three, so the divisibility template transfers verbatim.

Say $d(x)$ divides $p(x)$ when $p(x) = d(x)q(x)$ for some polynomial $q(x)$ — the same definition, with polynomials in place of integers. Unpack, compute, factor, and check the leftover factor is a polynomial, and you have proved that if $d$ divides both $p$ and $r$ then it divides $p \cdot a + r \cdot b$ for any polynomials $a$ and $b$.

The Factor Theorem you met in precalculus is one instance: $(x - c)$ divides $p(x)$ exactly when $p(c) = 0$. Long division of polynomials is the division algorithm of §4.4 in the same disguise, remainder and all.

</WhereThisGoes>
