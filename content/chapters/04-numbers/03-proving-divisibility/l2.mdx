We say $3$ **goes into** $12$ because $12$ counters share into groups of $3$ with nothing left over.

Now here is a fact worth proving.

> If $2$ goes into a number, and that number goes into another number, then $2$ goes into that one too.

Sounds fussy. Let's do it with counters and it will be obvious.

## Try it

$24$ counters. Does $4$ go into $24$? Yes — six groups of $4$.

Does $24$ go into $72$? Yes — three groups of $24$.

So does $4$ go into $72$?

<Proof level="l2">
Start with $72$ counters. Share them into $3$ groups of $24$.

Now take one group of $24$. We know it splits into $6$ groups of $4$.

So do that to all three groups. Each one becomes $6$ little groups of $4$.

Now count the little groups. $6$ and $6$ and $6$. That is $18$ little groups.

And every one has exactly $4$ counters, with nothing left over.

So $4$ goes into $72$. □
</Proof>

## What we actually did

Read the proof back and notice the shape. It has three steps, and they will be the same three steps every time.

1. **Start from what you're given.** We were told about groups of $24$, so we made groups of $24$.
2. **Break each one down further.** Each group of $24$ became six groups of $4$.
3. **Count what you ended up with.** Little groups of $4$, and nothing left over.

That is the whole trick. Take what you are given, break it down, count.

<Aside>
Watch out for one thing. Say you make $2$ groups of $6$, which is $12$ counters. Now $4$ goes into $12$ — three groups of $4$.

But $4$ does not go into $2$, and $4$ does not go into $6$! It sneaked in by taking a bit from each group.
</Aside>
