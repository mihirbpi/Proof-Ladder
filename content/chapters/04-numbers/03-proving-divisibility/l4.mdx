Divisibility statements are existential (module 4.2), so their proofs all have the same shape. This module makes the shape explicit and then works four examples with it.

## The template

> **1. Unpack.** Convert each hypothesis $a \mid b$ into $b = ak$, with a **fresh name** for each leftover factor.
> **2. Compute.** Substitute and rearrange.
> **3. Factor.** Extract the target divisor.
> **4. Verify.** State that the factor left over is an integer, citing closure (module 4.1).

Step 4 is the one students omit and graders look for. The definition of $\mid$ demands an integer witness (§1.12); producing an expression without checking its type proves nothing.

## Divisibility passes along a chain

> **Proposition.** For $a,b,c \in \mathbb{Z}$: if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$ then $a \mid c$.

<Proof>
Suppose $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$. Then there exist $j, k \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $b = aj$ and $c = bk$.

Substituting, $c = (aj)k = a(jk)$.

Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under multiplication, $jk \in \mathbb{Z}$. So $a \mid c$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Fresh names for fresh witnesses">
Writing $b = ak$ and $c = bk$ with the same $k$ says that the two leftover factors are the same number — a hypothesis nobody supplied. The resulting argument proves a strictly weaker statement while appearing to prove the intended one, which is why this error survives casual checking.
</Warning>

## Products

> **Proposition.** If $a \mid c$ and $b \mid d$ then $ab \mid cd$.

<Proof>
There exist $j, k \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $c = aj$ and $d = bk$. Then
$$cd = (aj)(bk) = (ab)(jk),$$
using commutativity and associativity of multiplication. Since $jk \in \mathbb{Z}$ by closure, $ab \mid cd$.
</Proof>

## Linear combinations

> **Proposition.** If $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$ then $a \mid (bx + cy)$ for all $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$b = aj$, $c = ak$ with $j,k \in \mathbb{Z}$; and $x, y$ any two integers.</div>
  <div slot="want">An integer factor left over for $bx + cy$ over $a$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Substitute and factor out $a$ using distributivity. The leftover factor $jx + ky$ is an integer by closure under both operations.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Take any $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$. Since $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$, there are $j,k \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $b = aj$, $c = ak$. Then
$$bx + cy = ajx + aky = a(jx + ky).$$
As $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under multiplication and addition, $jx + ky \in \mathbb{Z}$. So $a \mid (bx+cy)$.
</Proof>

Taking $x = y = 1$ gives $a \mid (b+c)$; taking $x = 1, y = -1$ gives $a \mid (b - c)$. This one proposition covers a family of corollaries, and it is the engine of the Euclidean algorithm.

## When an *or* in the hypothesis costs you only one case

> **Proposition.** If $a \mid b$ or $a \mid c$, then $a \mid bc$.

The hypothesis is an *or*, so module 1.12 demands cases. But look at the two cases before writing them out: they are the same argument with $b$ and $c$ swapped.

<Proof>
Suppose first that $a \mid b$. Then $b = ak$ for some integer $k$, so
$$bc = (ak)c = a(kc),$$
and $kc$ is an integer by closure. So $a \mid bc$.

Now suppose instead that $a \mid c$. Swap the names $b$ and $c$ everywhere in the paragraph above and it still reads correctly, because $bc$ and $cb$ are the same number. So that case needs no new work.
</Proof>

**Why the second case was free, exactly.** Two conditions had to hold, and both were checked rather than assumed:

- the *hypothesis* turns into the other case's hypothesis when you swap the letters, and
- the *conclusion* is unchanged by the swap — here because $bc = cb$.

When both hold, one case really does cover the other. When they do not, skipping a case is skipping a case, and that is the most common way a hole gets into a proof quietly.

Mathematicians have a stock phrase for this move — you will meet it in a later course, and this course names it at the next level up. What matters now is the habit: say *why* the two cases are the same, in one sentence, every time you lean on it.

## Turning it around gives something false

> **Claim?** If $a \mid bc$ then $a \mid b$ or $a \mid c$.

**False.** Take $a = 4$, $b = 2$, $c = 6$: $4 \mid 12$ but $4 \nmid 2$ and $4 \nmid 6$.

The mechanism: $4 = 2^2$, and $12$ collects one factor of $2$ from each of $b$ and $c$. A composite divisor can be assembled from pieces spread across the two factors.

<Aside>
The claim becomes true when $a$ is prime — a prime has no proper factorization to spread out. That is **Euclid's lemma**: if $p$ is prime and $p \mid bc$ then $p \mid b$ or $p \mid c$. It is not obvious. The standard proof goes through a result called *Bézout's identity*: for any two whole numbers there are multiples of them that add to their greatest common factor — for instance $2\cdot 12 + (-1)\cdot 21 = 3$. That result is a descendant of the linear-combination proposition above. Euclid's lemma is in turn what makes prime factorization unique — and the uniqueness proof is exactly where it gets used.
</Aside>
