Module 4.2 gave you a definition you can compute with: one number **goes into** another when the second is a whole number of copies of the first. This module turns that definition into a routine you can follow every time.

## The template

Every proof about factors has four steps. Learn them once.

1. **Unpack.** Turn each thing you are given into a description with a count: *this many copies of that*.
2. **Regroup.** Split the copies, or bundle them, until the factor you want appears.
3. **Pull it out.** Say what the new copies are copies of.
4. **Check the count.** Say out loud that the new count is a whole number, and why.

That last step is the one people skip, and it is not optional. The definition promised a *whole* number of copies, so you owe the reader that check.

## Going into passes along a chain

> **Claim.** If a first number goes into a second, and the second goes into a third, then the first goes into the third.

Try it with numbers before proving it. $4$ goes into $24$ (six copies). $24$ goes into $72$ (three copies). Does $4$ go into $72$? Yes — eighteen copies.

And notice: $18 = 6 \times 3$. That is not a coincidence, and spotting it is most of the proof.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The second number is some whole number of copies of the first. The third is some whole number of copies of the second. Two different counts — so keep them apart.</div>
  <div slot="want">The third number is a whole number of copies of the first.</div>
  <div slot="how">Replace each copy of the second number by what it is really made of, and count the pieces.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Take any three numbers where the first goes into the second and the second goes into the third. Follow them.

The third number is some whole number of copies of the second. Call that **the outer count**.

Each of those copies is itself some whole number of copies of the first. Call that **the inner count**.

Now break every copy of the second number into its pieces. Nothing is added and nothing is lost — you have just described the same pile more finely.

How many pieces are there altogether? The outer count, repeated the inner count of times. That is the outer count multiplied by the inner count.

Both counts are whole numbers, and multiplying whole numbers gives a whole number (module 4.1). So the total is a whole number of copies of the first number.

That is exactly what it means for the first number to go into the third.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Two counts, two names">
The proof used **two different counts** and gave them two different names. That matters.

If you called them both "the count", you would be claiming the two numbers are the same — and nobody told you that. In the example, the counts were $6$ and $3$, which are plainly different.

Whenever a proof unpacks two things, give each unpacked piece its own name.
</Warning>

## Going into survives adding

> **Claim.** If a number goes into two other numbers, it goes into their sum.

Check it: $5$ goes into $20$ and into $35$. Does $5$ go into $55$? Yes — eleven copies. And $11 = 4 + 7$.

<Proof>
Take any number that goes into two others, and follow them.

The first of the two is some whole number of copies. The second is some whole number of copies as well — a different count.

Now push the two piles together. Nothing is broken up; you have simply put them side by side.

The total number of copies is the first count plus the second count.

Adding two whole numbers gives a whole number (module 4.1), so the total count is a whole number.

So the sum is a whole number of copies, which means the number goes into the sum.
</Proof>

Same four steps. Unpack, regroup, pull out, check the count.

## Something that looks true and is not

> **Claim?** If a number goes into a product, then it goes into one of the two things being multiplied.

This feels reasonable. It is false.

Take $4$, and the product $2 \times 6 = 12$. Does $4$ go into $12$? Yes — three copies. ✓

Does $4$ go into $2$? No. Does $4$ go into $6$? No. ✗

One counterexample and the claim is dead (module 1.11).

What went wrong? $4$ is two $2$s. The $12$ had one $2$ coming from the $2$ and another coming from the $6$. Neither piece had enough on its own — $4$ got in by borrowing from both.

<Aside>
The claim becomes true when the number doing the dividing is **prime**. A prime cannot be split into smaller factors, so it has nothing to spread across the two pieces — it has to land entirely in one of them.

That fact is called Euclid's lemma, and it turns out to be the reason every counting number breaks into primes in exactly one way.
</Aside>

## The habit

Whenever you see a claim about factors, ask: *what am I given, and how many copies of what is each thing?* Name the counts, keep them apart, and the regrouping almost always finishes itself.
