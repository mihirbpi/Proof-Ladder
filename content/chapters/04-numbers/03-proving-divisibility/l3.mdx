Module 4.2 gave you a definition you can compute with: one number **divides** another when the second is a whole number of copies of the first. This module turns that definition into a routine you can follow every time.

## The template

Every proof about factors has four steps. Learn them once.

1. **Unpack.** Turn each thing you are given into a description with a count: *this many copies of that*.
2. **Regroup.** Split the copies, or bundle them, until the factor you want appears.
3. **Pull it out.** Say what the new copies are copies of.
4. **Check the count.** Say out loud that the new count is a whole number, and why.

That last step is the one people skip, and it is not optional. The definition promised a *whole* number of copies, so you owe the reader that check.

## Going into passes along a chain

> **Claim.** If a first number divides a second, and the second divides a third, then the first divides the third.

Try numbers first. $4$ divides $24$ (six copies); $24$ divides $72$ (three copies); and $4$ divides $72$ — eighteen copies. Notice $18 = 6\times3$. Not a coincidence, and spotting it is most of the proof.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The second number is some whole number of copies of the first. The third is some whole number of copies of the second. Two different counts — so keep them apart.</div>
  <div slot="want">The third number is a whole number of copies of the first.</div>
  <div slot="how">Replace each copy of the second number by what it is really made of, and count the pieces.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Take any three numbers where the first divides the second and the second divides the third. Follow them.

The third number is some whole number of copies of the second — call that **the outer count**. Each of those copies is itself some whole number of copies of the first — **the inner count**.

Now break every copy of the second number into its pieces. Nothing is added and nothing is lost; you have described the same pile more finely.

How many pieces altogether? The outer count, repeated the inner count of times — the two counts multiplied.

Multiplying whole numbers gives a whole number (module 4.1), so the total is a whole number of copies of the first number. That is exactly what it means for the first to divide the third.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Two counts, two names">
The proof used **two different counts** and gave them two different names.

Call them both "the count" and you are claiming the two are the same — which nobody told you. In the example they were $6$ and $3$.

Whenever a proof unpacks two things, give each piece its own name.
</Warning>

## Going into survives adding

> **Claim.** If a number divides two other numbers, it divides their sum.

Check it: $5$ divides $20$ and into $35$. Does $5$ divide $55$? Yes — eleven copies. And $11 = 4 + 7$.

<Proof>
Take any number that divides two others, and follow them.

Each of the two is some whole number of copies — two different counts.

Push the two piles together. Nothing is broken up; you have put them side by side, so the total is the first count plus the second.

Adding whole numbers gives a whole number (module 4.1). So the sum is a whole number of copies, which means the number divides the sum.
</Proof>

Same four steps. Unpack, regroup, pull out, check the count.

## Something that looks true and is not

> **Claim?** If a number divides a product, then it divides one of the two things being multiplied.

This feels reasonable. It is false.

Take $4$, and the product $2 \times 6 = 12$. Does $4$ divide $12$? Yes — three copies. ✓

Does $4$ divide $2$? No. Does $4$ divide $6$? No. ✗

One counterexample and the claim is dead (module 1.11).

What went wrong? $4$ is two $2$s, and the $12$ got one $2$ from each side. Neither piece had enough alone — $4$ got in by borrowing from both.

<Aside>
The claim becomes true when the number doing the dividing is **prime**. A prime cannot be split into smaller factors, so it has nothing to spread across the two pieces — it has to land entirely in one of them.

That fact is called Euclid's lemma, and it turns out to be the reason every counting number breaks into primes in exactly one way.
</Aside>

## The habit

Whenever you see a claim about factors, ask: *what am I given, and how many copies of what is each thing?* Name the counts, keep them apart, and the regrouping almost always finishes itself.
