Module 4.2 ended with four steps. Here they are as a template, and it handles essentially every divisibility proof you will meet.

> 1. **Unpack.** Turn each hypothesis $a \mid b$ into an equation $b = a k$, with a **fresh letter** for each.
> 2. **Compute.** Substitute into the expression you care about.
> 3. **Factor.** Pull the divisor out front.
> 4. **Cite closure.** Say that the factor left over is an integer, and why.

Step 4 is the one people skip, and it is not optional: the definition promised an *integer* witness — the element exhibited to settle an existence claim (module 1.12).

## The main theorem

> **Theorem.** If $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$, then $a \mid (bx + cy)$ for all integers $x, y$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Two hypotheses, each existential, so two witnesses — and they need <strong>different names</strong>, since nothing says the two quotients agree.</div>
  <div slot="want">An integer $j$ with $bx + cy = aj$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Substitute both, then factor $a$ out of the whole expression. Closure handles the rest.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$. Then there are integers $k$ and $m$ with
$$
b = ak, \qquad c = am.
$$
Let $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$ be arbitrary. Then
$$
bx + cy = (ak)x + (am)y = a(kx + my).
$$
Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under multiplication and addition, $kx + my \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Then $bx + cy = aj$ with $j = kx+my \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $a \mid (bx+cy)$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Two hypotheses, two letters">
Writing $b = ak$ and $c = ak$ claims $b = c$. Nobody said that.

With $a = 3$, $b = 6$, $c = 9$: the witnesses are $2$ and $3$, plainly different. A proof that forces them equal proves a much narrower statement and is simply wrong.

**Every unpacking gets its own fresh letter.** This rule returns in Chapter 6 with a sharper edge, because there the induction variable has already taken the obvious name.
</Warning>

## What it buys

The theorem is stated in a general form on purpose, and the special cases fall out by choosing $x$ and $y$:

| Choice | Conclusion |
|---|---|
| $x = y = 1$ | $a \mid (b+c)$ |
| $x = 1, y = -1$ | $a \mid (b-c)$ |
| $y = 0$ | $a \mid bx$ — any multiple of $b$ |

**One proof, three corollaries, and no extra work.** Proving the general statement was no harder than proving $a \mid (b+c)$ alone, which is a good reason to look for the general version first.

## A plausible statement that is false

> **Claim?** If $a \mid bc$, then $a \mid b$ or $a \mid c$.

It sounds reasonable. It is false.

<Proof>
Take $a = 4$, $b = 2$, $c = 6$.

Then $bc = 12$ and $4 \mid 12$ ✓.

But $4 \nmid 2$ and $4 \nmid 6$ ✗.
</Proof>

One counterexample, and the claim is dead (module 1.11).

**What went wrong is worth understanding.** $4 = 2 \times 2$, and the product $12$ received one factor of $2$ from $b$ and another from $c$. Neither had enough on its own; $4$ got in by borrowing from both.

That diagnosis suggests the repair. If $a$ cannot be split across the two factors, the argument cannot fail — and an integer that cannot be split is a **prime**.

> **Euclid's lemma.** If $p$ is prime and $p \mid bc$, then $p \mid b$ or $p \mid c$.

This is true, and genuinely harder than anything else in the module — it is not provable by the four-step template. It matters because it is the reason every integer factors into primes in exactly one way.

<Warning title="Plausible is not proved">
The false claim above has the same *shape* as several true ones in this module. Nothing about its appearance marks it as different.

This is why the template ends with a closure check rather than a feeling. When you cannot complete step 4 — when you cannot produce the integer — that is evidence the statement may be false, and the next move is to hunt for a counterexample rather than to try harder.

Both directions are useful. Failing to prove something is not proof that it is false, but it is a reason to look.
</Warning>

## Without loss of generality

One phrase you will meet. Suppose a claim is symmetric in $b$ and $c$ — swapping them gives the same statement. Then a proof may say **without loss of generality**, assume $b \leq c$, and handle only that case.

This is legitimate **only** when the symmetry is real: the other case must follow by relabeling, with no new argument. Used honestly it halves the work; used carelessly it hides a case you never handled.

The test is to ask whether swapping the names really does turn your proof into a proof of the other case. If you cannot say yes immediately, write both.

<Aside>
The theorem above is the engine behind the divisibility rules you learned by rote.

Why does $3 \mid n$ exactly when $3$ divides the digit sum? Write $n = 100a + 10b + c$. Then
$$
n - (a+b+c) = 99a + 9b = 3(33a + 3b),
$$
so $3$ divides the difference. Since $3$ divides the difference, the theorem says $3$ divides $n$ exactly when it divides $a + b + c$ — take $x = 1$, $y = -1$ and read it both ways.

The rule for $9$ is the same computation, and the rule for $11$ uses alternating signs for the same reason.
</Aside>
