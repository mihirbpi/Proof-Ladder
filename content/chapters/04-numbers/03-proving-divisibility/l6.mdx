Every proof in this module has the same skeleton. Once you have seen it three times you can write the fourth without thinking, which is exactly what practice is for.

> **The template.** Unpack each hypothesis into an equation. Do algebra until the target form $b = a\cdot(\text{something})$ appears. Check the something is an integer, citing closure.

The named move is **start from what you're given**.

## Transitivity

> **Proposition.** If $a \mid b$ and $b\mid c$, then $a \mid c$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Two existential hypotheses, so two witnesses — and they need different letters.</div>
  <div slot="want">An integer $m$ with $c = am$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Substitute one equation into the other. The witness will be a product, and closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ under multiplication finishes it.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Since $a\mid b$, there is an integer $j$ with $b = aj$.

Since $b \mid c$, there is an integer $k$ with $c = bk$.

Substituting,
$$c = bk = (aj)k = a(jk),$$
using associativity of multiplication.

Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under multiplication, $jk\in\mathbb{Z}$. Hence $a \mid c$.
</Proof>

Compare this with transitivity of $\subseteq$ from module 2.3, and with transitivity of congruence in your geometry course. **Same shape, three subjects** — chain two facts, and the middle object drops out.

## Linear combinations

> **Proposition.** If $a\mid b$ and $a\mid c$, then $a \mid (bx + cy)$ for all integers $x, y$.

<Proof>
Let $x, y$ be arbitrary integers.

Since $a\mid b$, there is $j\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $b = aj$; since $a\mid c$, there is $k\in\mathbb{Z}$ with $c = ak$.

Then
$$bx + cy = ajx + aky = a(jx+ky),$$
by the distributive law.

Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under multiplication and addition, $jx+ky\in\mathbb{Z}$. Hence $a\mid(bx+cy)$.
</Proof>

This one proposition contains a family of results. Take $x=y=1$: $a \mid (b+c)$. Take $x=1, y=-1$: $a\mid(b-c)$. Take $y=0$: $a \mid bx$.

**Proving the general statement once is cheaper than proving four special cases**, and recognising that a batch of exercises are instances of one theorem is a skill worth developing early.

## What does not hold

<Warning title="Divisibility does not split across products">
$$a \mid bc \quad\text{does not imply}\quad a\mid b \ \text{ or } \ a \mid c.$$

**Counterexample.** $4 \mid 12$ and $12 = 2\cdot 6$, but $4\nmid 2$ and $4 \nmid 6$.

The factor $4$ was assembled from a $2$ in each — neither factor carried it alone.

The implication **does** hold when $a$ is prime, and that is a theorem (Euclid's lemma) rather than an obvious fact. Its proof is genuinely harder than anything in this module, and the $\sqrt2$ argument in module 4.7 depends on it.

Two more failures worth knowing: $a\mid c$ and $b \mid c$ do not give $ab\mid c$ (take $2\mid 12$, $6\mid12$, $12\mid 12$ — that one works; but $4\mid 12$ and $6\mid 12$ while $24\nmid12$). And $a \mid (b+c)$ does not give $a\mid b$ or $a \mid c$: $5 \mid (2+3)$.
</Warning>

## Antisymmetry

> **Proposition.** If $a\mid b$ and $b\mid a$ with $a,b$ positive, then $a = b$.

<Proof>
There are integers $j, k$ with $b = aj$ and $a = bk$.

Substituting, $a = (aj)k = a(jk)$. Since $a$ is positive, it is non-zero, so we may cancel: $jk = 1$.

The only integer factorisations of $1$ are $1\cdot1$ and $(-1)(-1)$. Since $a$ and $b$ are positive, $j$ and $k$ are positive, so $j = k = 1$.

Hence $b = a\cdot 1 = a$.
</Proof>

The step *"the only integer factorisations of $1$"* is where the proof lives, and it is a fact about $\mathbb{Z}$ specifically — over the rationals, $jk=1$ has infinitely many solutions, and the proof would collapse.

Together with reflexivity and transitivity, this makes $\mid$ a **partial order** on the positive integers, exactly like $\subseteq$ from module 2.3 — and with the same feature: $4$ and $6$ are incomparable, since neither divides the other.

## Without loss of generality

Some proofs have cases that are genuinely identical up to renaming. Writing both is waste, and there is a standard phrase for skipping one.

> **Proposition.** For integers $a$ and $b$, if $ab$ is odd then both are odd.

<Proof>
We prove the contrapositive: if $a$ or $b$ is even, then $ab$ is even.

**Without loss of generality**, assume $a$ is even — the other case follows by exchanging the roles of $a$ and $b$, which is legitimate because multiplication is commutative.

Then $a = 2j$ for some integer $j$, so $ab = 2(jb)$ with $jb \in\mathbb{Z}$. Hence $ab$ is even.
</Proof>

<Warning title="WLOG requires a stated symmetry">
The phrase is a promise that the omitted case is the written one with letters swapped, and it is only valid when a genuine symmetry exists. Here, commutativity of multiplication supplies it, and the proof says so.

Using WLOG on cases that are not symmetric is a way of skipping the hard half. If you cannot name the symmetry in one clause, write both cases out.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The linear-combination proposition is more important than it looks.

The set $\{bx + cy : x, y \in\mathbb{Z}\}$ of all integer combinations of $b$ and $c$ turns out to be exactly the set of multiples of $\gcd(b,c)$. So the proposition says: any common divisor of $b$ and $c$ divides the gcd.

That result — Bézout's identity — is what the Euclidean algorithm computes, and it is the foundation of the number theory behind public-key cryptography. The three-line proof above is the first step of a genuinely deep theorem.
</Aside>

## Checklist

When your divisibility proof stalls, work through these:

1. Did you unpack **every** hypothesis into an equation?
2. Did you use **different letters** for different witnesses?
3. Have you written the goal in the form $b = a\cdot(\ ?\ )$, so you know what to aim at?
4. Did you verify the witness is an integer, and say which closure property?
5. Did you assume something that only holds for primes?

Most stalls are item 3. Writing the target form before doing algebra tells you what the algebra is for.
