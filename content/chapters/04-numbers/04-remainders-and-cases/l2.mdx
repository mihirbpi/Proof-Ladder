Share some counters among $3$ friends and see what is **left over**.

| Counters | Each friend gets | Left over |
|---|---|---|
| $9$ | $3$ | $0$ |
| $10$ | $3$ | $1$ |
| $11$ | $3$ | $2$ |
| $12$ | $4$ | $0$ |
| $13$ | $4$ | $1$ |
| $14$ | $4$ | $2$ |
| $15$ | $5$ | $0$ |

Look down the last column. $0, 1, 2, 0, 1, 2, 0$. Round and round forever.

## Why it never reaches 3

Suppose you had $3$ left over. Then you could give one more to each friend! So it would not be left over any more.

That is the rule:

> **What is left over is always smaller than the number of friends.**

Three friends? Leftovers are $0$, $1$, or $2$. Never $3$ or more.

Five friends? Leftovers are $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$, or $4$.

## Why this is so useful

Because it means there are only a *few kinds* of number.

If you are sharing among $3$, then every number in the world is one of exactly three kinds:

- the ones with $0$ left over,
- the ones with $1$ left over,
- the ones with $2$ left over.

That is all of them. Nothing is missed.

So if you want to prove something about *every* number, you only have to check three things. That move is called **splitting into cases**, and you met it in module 1.12.

<Warning title="You must check them all">
Splitting into cases only works if your cases cover everything. If you check the $0$-left-over numbers and the $1$-left-over numbers and stop, the $2$-left-over ones are still out there, unchecked. A gap is a gap.
</Warning>

Pick any number under 20. Share it among 4 friends and say the leftover. Now find a number with each of the leftovers 0, 1, 2, and 3.
