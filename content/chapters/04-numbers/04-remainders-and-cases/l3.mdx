Module 4.2 told you what it means for $3$ to go into a number. This module is about what happens when it doesn't — and that turns out to be surprisingly useful.

## Remainders

Share $17$ among $3$ people. Each gets $5$, and $2$ are left over.

That $2$ is the **remainder**, and you have been computing remainders since you first did long division.

The key fact is that the remainder is always **smaller** than what you divided by. Sharing among $3$, you can never have $3$ left over — you would just give one more to each person.

> Share any counting number among $3$. The leftover is $0$, $1$, or $2$. Nothing else is possible.

And $3$ goes into the number exactly when the remainder is $0$.

## Three kinds of number

So every counting number is exactly one of three kinds:

| Kind | Examples |
|---|---|
| remainder $0$ — a multiple of $3$ | $0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15$ |
| remainder $1$ | $1, 4, 7, 10, 13, 16$ |
| remainder $2$ | $2, 5, 8, 11, 14, 17$ |

Every number appears in exactly one row. Nothing is missing and nothing is counted twice.

This is why **splitting into cases** works so well here. To prove something about every counting number, you check three rows. Three finite checks, and you have covered infinitely many numbers.

## The showcase proof

> **Claim.** Take any counting number that $3$ does **not** go into. Multiply it by itself and subtract $1$. Then $3$ goes into the answer.

Check a few first.

$4$: not a multiple of $3$. And $4 \times 4 - 1 = 15$, which is five $3$s. ✓
$5$: not a multiple of $3$. And $5 \times 5 - 1 = 24$, which is eight $3$s. ✓
$7$: $7 \times 7 - 1 = 48$, which is sixteen $3$s. ✓

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">We are told $3$ does not go into our number. That is a *not* statement, and not-statements give us nothing to compute with — until we remember remainders. Not a multiple of $3$ means the remainder is $1$ or $2$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$3$ goes into (the number times itself, minus one).</div>
  <div slot="how">Split into cases. There are only two left, and in each one we can say exactly what the number looks like and just multiply it out.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Take any counting number that $3$ does not go into, and follow it.

Sharing it among $3$ leaves a remainder of $0$, $1$, or $2$. The remainder cannot be $0$, because that would mean $3$ goes into it. So two cases remain.

**Case 1: the remainder is $1$.** Then the number is some whole number of $3$s, plus one extra.

Multiply it by itself. You get: (that many $3$s) times itself, plus two copies of (that many $3$s), plus one. The first two chunks are built entirely out of $3$s. So the whole answer is a pile of $3$s plus one extra.

Now subtract $1$. The extra goes away, and what is left is made only of $3$s.

So $3$ goes into it. ✓

**Case 2: the remainder is $2$.** Then the number is some whole number of $3$s, plus two extra.

Multiply it by itself. You get: (that many $3$s) times itself, plus four copies of (that many $3$s), plus four. The first two chunks are made of $3$s, and $4$ is one $3$ plus one.

So altogether it is a pile of $3$s, plus one extra.

Subtract $1$, and again only $3$s are left.

So $3$ goes into it. ✓

The two cases cover everything, and both give the conclusion. So the claim holds for every counting number that $3$ does not go into.
</Proof>

## Look at how much that used

That one proof pulled in almost everything you have learned:

- **Remainders** (this module) turned an unusable *not* into two usable descriptions.
- **Splitting into cases** (module 1.12) let two calculations cover all numbers.
- **Closure** (module 4.1) justified "a pile of $3$s stays a pile of $3$s when you regroup it."
- **Going into as a hunt for a count** (module 4.2) told us what to produce.

That is what a real proof looks like: not one clever idea, but four ordinary tools used in the right order.

<Warning title="Say why the missing case is missing">
The proof listed all three possible remainders and then explained why remainder $0$ was impossible.

Do not leave that out. A reader who does not see the exclusion will wonder whether you simply forgot a case — and forgetting a case is the most common way a case proof goes wrong.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The trick works in reverse too. If you can show a number is a pile of $3$s plus one extra, you have *proved* that $3$ does not go into it — because only remainder $0$ means it does.

Describing a number by its remainder is often the fastest way to prove something is *not* a multiple.
</Aside>
