Module 4.2 told you what it means for $3$ to divide a number. This module is about what happens when it doesn't — and that turns out to be surprisingly useful.

## Remainders

Divide $17$ by $3$. You get $5$, with $2$ left over. We write
$$
17 = 3 \times 5 + 2.
$$
That $2$ is the **remainder**.

The key fact is that the remainder is always smaller than what you divided by. Sharing among $3$, you can never have $3$ left over — you would just give one more to each.

> When you divide any whole number $n$ by $3$, you can write
> $$n = 3k + r$$
> where $k$ is a whole number and $r$ is $0$, $1$, or $2$.

And $3 \mid n$ exactly when $r = 0$.

## Three kinds of number

So every whole number is exactly one of three kinds:

| Kind | Looks like | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| remainder $0$ | $3k$ | $0, 3, 6, 9, 12$ |
| remainder $1$ | $3k+1$ | $1, 4, 7, 10, 13$ |
| remainder $2$ | $3k+2$ | $2, 5, 8, 11, 14$ |

Every number appears in exactly one row. Nothing is missing and nothing is counted twice.

This is why **splitting into cases** works so well here. To prove something about every whole number, you check three rows. Three finite checks, and you have covered infinitely many numbers.

## The showcase proof

> **Claim.** For any whole number $a$: if $3 \nmid a$, then $3 \mid (a \times a - 1)$.

Check a few first. $a = 4$: not a multiple of $3$, and $16 - 1 = 15 = 3 \times 5$. ✓ $a = 5$: $25 - 1 = 24 = 3 \times 8$. ✓

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$3 \nmid a$. That is a *not* statement, and not-statements give us nothing to compute with — until we remember remainders. Not divisible by $3$ means the remainder is $1$ or $2$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$a \times a - 1$ is a multiple of $3$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Split into cases: remainder $1$, and remainder $2$. In each case write $a$ in that form, compute $a \times a - 1$, and pull out a $3$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose $3 \nmid a$. By the remainder rule, $a = 3k$ or $a = 3k+1$ or $a = 3k+2$ for some whole number $k$ — and the first is ruled out, since it would make $3 \mid a$. So two cases remain.

**Case 1: $a = 3k+1$.** Then
$$a \times a - 1 = (3k+1)(3k+1) - 1 = 9k^2 + 6k + 1 - 1 = 9k^2 + 6k = 3(3k^2 + 2k).$$
Since $3k^2 + 2k$ is a whole number, $3 \mid (a\times a - 1)$.

**Case 2: $a = 3k+2$.** Then
$$a \times a - 1 = (3k+2)(3k+2) - 1 = 9k^2 + 12k + 4 - 1 = 9k^2 + 12k + 3 = 3(3k^2 + 4k + 1).$$
Since $3k^2 + 4k + 1$ is a whole number, $3 \mid (a \times a - 1)$.

The two cases cover everything, and both give the conclusion. So the claim holds.
</Proof>

## Look at how much that used

That one proof pulled in almost everything you have learned:

- **Remainders** (this module) turned an unusable *not* into two usable forms.
- **Splitting into cases** (module 1.12) let two calculations cover all numbers.
- **Closure** (module 4.1) justified "$3k^2 + 2k$ is a whole number."
- **Divisibility as a hunt for a witness** (module 4.2) told us what to produce.

That is what a real proof looks like: not one clever idea, but four ordinary tools used in the right order.

<Warning title="Remainder 0 has to be excluded, and you have to say so">
The proof listed three possible forms and then explained why the first was impossible. Do not leave that out. A reader who does not see the exclusion will wonder whether you forgot a case.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The trick works in reverse too. If you can write a number as $3k + 1$, you have *proved* it is not a multiple of $3$ — because it has remainder $1$, and only remainder $0$ means divisible. Writing a number in remainder form is often the fastest way to prove something is not divisible.
</Aside>
