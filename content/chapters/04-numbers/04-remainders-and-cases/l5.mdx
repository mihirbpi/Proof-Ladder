Module 4.2 defined $m \mid n$. This module unpacks the other case — what it means when $m$ does **not** divide $n$ — and the answer turns "not divisible" from a dead end into a finite list of possibilities.

## The division algorithm

> **Theorem.** For integers $m$ and $n > 0$, there are unique integers $q$ and $r$ with
> $$ m = nq + r, \qquad 0 \leq r < n. $$

$r$ is the **remainder**. This is long division, stated as a theorem.

The condition $0 \leq r < n$ is the whole content. Without it the statement is trivial — $m = n \cdot 0 + m$ always works — and useless. Pinning $r$ into a window of exactly $n$ values is what makes the theorem worth having.

Note that $r = 0$ is exactly the case $n \mid m$. So the theorem covers divisibility and non-divisibility in one statement, and it says:

> **Not divisible by $n$ means the remainder is one of $1, 2, \ldots, n-1$.**

Finitely many possibilities. That is what makes the next technique work.

## Split into cases

Every integer leaves exactly one remainder on division by $n$. So the integers fall into $n$ groups, with **no overlap and no gap**, and a claim about all integers can be proved by checking each group.

The named move is **split into cases**, and it carries one thing to check: the cases must be exhaustive. The division algorithm is what guarantees they are.

For $n = 2$ this recovers something familiar. Every integer is $2q$ or $2q+1$ — even or odd, nothing else. **That split into two is a theorem, not a definition**, and this is its proof.

## A worked case proof

> **Proposition.** For every integer $m$, the number $m^2$ has remainder $0$ or $1$ on division by $3$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">By the division algorithm, $m$ is $3q$, $3q+1$, or $3q+2$ for some integer $q$ — three cases, and they cover everything.</div>
  <div slot="want">In each case, $m^2$ written as a multiple of $3$ plus $0$ or $1$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Square each form and regroup. The work is three short expansions, and the answer never lands on remainder $2$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $m \in \mathbb{Z}$ be arbitrary. By the division algorithm with $n = 3$, exactly one of the following holds.

*Case 1: $m = 3q$.* Then $m^2 = 9q^2 = 3(3q^2)$, remainder $0$.

*Case 2: $m = 3q+1$.* Then
$$
m^2 = 9q^2 + 6q + 1 = 3(3q^2+2q) + 1,
$$
remainder $1$.

*Case 3: $m = 3q+2$.* Then
$$
m^2 = 9q^2 + 12q + 4 = 3(3q^2+4q+1) + 1,
$$
remainder $1$.

In each case the factor left over is an integer by closure, and the cases are exhaustive. So $m^2$ leaves remainder $0$ or $1$.
</Proof>

**No square leaves remainder $2$ on division by $3$.** That is a real and slightly surprising fact, and it comes with a use: it lets you rule things out.

Is $3\,000\,000\,002$ a perfect square? Its digit sum is $5$, so it leaves remainder $2$ on division by $3$ (module 4.3's Aside). By the proposition, it cannot be a square — and you never had to take a square root.

## Choosing the modulus

> **Proposition.** For every integer $n$, $\ 3 \mid (n^3 - n)$.

<Proof>
Let $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ be arbitrary, and note $n^3 - n = n(n-1)(n+1)$.

*Case 1: $n = 3q$.* The first factor is divisible by $3$.

*Case 2: $n = 3q+1$.* Then $n - 1 = 3q$, and the second factor is divisible by $3$.

*Case 3: $n = 3q+2$.* Then $n + 1 = 3q+3 = 3(q+1)$, and the third factor is divisible by $3$.

In each case one factor is a multiple of $3$, so the product is. The cases are exhaustive.
</Proof>

Splitting by $3$ was a **choice**, and it was the right one because the conclusion was about $3$. Splitting into even and odd would have produced two cases and no progress. **Match the modulus to the divisor in the goal.**

<Warning title="Exhaustive, and preferably disjoint">
Two conditions on a case split, and only the first is mandatory.

**Exhaustive** — every element must fall into at least one case. This is required, and it is what the division algorithm supplies. Leave a case out and the proof has a hole exactly where that case lived.

**Disjoint** — no element falls into two. This is not required for correctness, since proving the conclusion twice for the same element does no harm, but overlapping cases usually signal a split that has not been thought through.

The commonest failure is a split that looks exhaustive and is not: *positive, negative* misses zero; *rational, irrational* is fine for reals and misses complex numbers.
</Warning>

<Aside>
This module is the reason "check small cases" sometimes becomes a proof and usually does not.

Checking $m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots, 100$ proves nothing about $m = 101$. But checking the three *remainder classes* mod $3$ proves the statement for every integer, because every integer is in one of them.

The difference is that remainder classes are finitely many and integers are not. Splitting into cases works when the cases are a finite partition of an infinite set — and finding such a partition is often the entire idea of a proof.
</Aside>
