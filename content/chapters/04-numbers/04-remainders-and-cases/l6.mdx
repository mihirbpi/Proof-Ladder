Module 4.2 left a gap. Proving $a\mid b$ is easy — exhibit the witness. Proving $a\nmid b$ means ruling out every integer $k$, and there are infinitely many.

Division with remainder closes the gap by replacing infinitely many candidates with finitely many cases.

## The division algorithm

> **Theorem.** For any integer $m$ and any positive integer $n$, there exist **unique** integers $q$ and $r$ with
> $$m = nq + r \quad\text{and}\quad 0 \leq r < n.$$

$q$ is the **quotient** and $r$ the **remainder**. The name "algorithm" is historical; it is a theorem, and its existence half is proved by induction in Chapter 6.

Note the two halves — existence and uniqueness, module 1.10's $\exists!$. Uniqueness is what makes "the remainder" a legitimate phrase, and it is what makes the case split below exclusive rather than merely exhaustive.

The constraint $0\le r<n$ is not decoration. Without it, $17 = 5\cdot 2 + 7$ and $17 = 5\cdot 3 + 2$ are both true, and neither $q$ nor $r$ would be determined.

## Divisibility restated

$$
n \mid m \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \text{the remainder of } m \text{ on division by } n \text{ is } 0.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: $n$ divides $m$ exactly when dividing $m$ by $n$ leaves nothing over*

So $n \nmid m$ means the remainder is one of $1, 2, \dots, n-1$ — **finitely many possibilities**, each of which can be examined. The infinite search is gone.

## Sorting the integers by remainder

Every integer falls into exactly one of $n$ groups according to the remainder it leaves on division by $n$. For $n = 3$:

$$\mathbb{Z} = \{3k\} \ \cup\ \{3k+1\}\ \cup\ \{3k+2\}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\cup$ is "pooled with". So the line reads: every integer is a multiple of $3$, or one more than a multiple of $3$, or two more — three families, with nothing left out and no overlap*

Three sets, pairwise disjoint, covering everything — a **partition** in the sense of module 2.4. Exhaustive from existence, exclusive from uniqueness.

**That is exactly the license proof by cases needs** (module 1.12). Splitting on the remainder is not a rough guide; it is a complete method, guaranteed by the theorem.

The named move is **split into cases**.

## Choosing what to divide by

The number of cases is yours to pick, and picking well is most of the work.

> **Proposition.** For every integer $n$, $n^2 + n$ is even.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The conclusion concerns divisibility by $2$, so split on the remainder on division by $2$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$n^2+n = 2\cdot(\text{integer})$ in each case.</div>
  <div slot="how">Substitute the case form and factor out a $2$. Two cases, and both should be short — if one runs long, you probably split on the wrong number.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $n$ be an arbitrary integer. By the division algorithm with divisor $2$, either $n = 2k$ or $n = 2k+1$ for some integer $k$.

**Case 1: $n = 2k$.** Then
$$n^2 + n = 4k^2 + 2k = 2(2k^2+k),$$
and $2k^2+k\in\mathbb{Z}$ by closure.

**Case 2: $n = 2k+1$.** Then
$$n^2 + n = (4k^2+4k+1) + (2k+1) = 4k^2+6k+2 = 2(2k^2+3k+1),$$
and $2k^2+3k+1\in\mathbb{Z}$ by closure.

The cases are exhaustive, so $n^2+n$ is even for every integer $n$.
</Proof>

Now a harder one, where the number you split on is not the number in the conclusion.

> **Proposition.** For every integer $n$, $3 \mid (n^3 - n)$.

<Proof>
Pick any $n$ you like. By the division algorithm, $n = 3k$, $n = 3k+1$, or $n = 3k+2$.

**Case 1: $n = 3k$.** Then $n^3 - n = 27k^3 - 3k = 3(9k^3-k)$.

**Case 2: $n = 3k+1$.** Then $n^3 = 27k^3 + 27k^2+9k+1$, so
$$n^3 - n = 27k^3+27k^2+9k+1 - 3k - 1 = 3(9k^3+9k^2+2k).$$

**Case 3: $n = 3k+2$.** Then $n^3 = 27k^3+54k^2+36k+8$, so
$$n^3 - n = 27k^3+54k^2+36k+8-3k-2 = 3(9k^3+18k^2+11k+2).$$

Each bracket is an integer by closure, and the cases are exhaustive. So $3\mid(n^3-n)$.
</Proof>

**Three cases because the conclusion was about $3$.** Splitting on division by $2$ would have produced two cases neither of which resolved, and splitting on the wrong number is the usual reason a case proof fails to close.

<Warning title="Exhaustive, not merely plausible">
The claim that the cases cover everything is a thing to check, and the division algorithm is what discharges it. Say so.

A proof of "$3\mid(n^3-n)$" that checks $n=1,2,3,4,5$ and stops has not proved anything — module 1.10's warning. Cases are not examples. What makes a case argument valid is the theorem guaranteeing there are no others.
</Warning>

## Reading the pattern backwards

The correspondence runs both directions:

$$m = 3k+1 \text{ for some } k \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad m \text{ leaves remainder } 1 \text{ on division by } 3 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 3\nmid m.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", $\exists!$ is "there is exactly one", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when". So the line reads: leaving remainder $1$ on division by $3$ is the same as being one more than a multiple of $3$.*

So writing an integer in the form $3k+1$ **proves** it is not divisible by $3$, by uniqueness of the remainder. This is how non-divisibility gets proved without an infinite search, and it is the tool module 4.7 will need.

## Choosing what to divide by, in practice

There is a way to pick that number before doing any algebra: **look at what the conclusion is about.**

To prove $n^2+n$ is even, the conclusion mentions $2$, so split on division by $2$. To prove $3\mid(n^3-n)$, split on division by $3$. To prove $4\mid(n^2-1)$ for odd $n$, the hypothesis already restricts you to the odd group, and writing $n = 2k+1$ is the whole setup.

Sometimes the right number to split on is larger than the number in the conclusion. To prove $24 \mid (n^2-1)$ for $n$ sharing no factor with $6$, splitting on division by $6$ gives the useful cases, because the hypothesis is itself a statement about remainders on division by $6$.

**When a case proof produces cases that will not close, you probably split on the wrong number.** Try a different one before trying harder algebra.

## Refuting divisibility

The division algorithm also settles non-divisibility in one line.

> **Claim.** $3\nmid 100$.
>
> **Proof.** $100 = 3\cdot33+1$, and $0\leq1<3$. By uniqueness of the remainder, the remainder of $100$ on division by $3$ is $1$, not $0$. So $3\nmid100$. $\square$

Compare with what module 4.2 left you: ruling out every integer $k$ with $100 = 3k$. The division algorithm replaced an infinite search with an arithmetic fact, and **uniqueness is the part doing the work** — without it, finding *one* representation with non-zero remainder would not exclude another with remainder zero.

That is why the theorem was stated with $\exists!$ rather than $\exists$.

## Where geometry meets this

Regular polygons supply a natural application. A regular $n$-gon has rotational symmetry through $360°/n$, and its rotations compose by adding step counts and keeping the remainder on division by $n$ — rotating by three steps then two steps gives five steps, and if $n=4$ that is one step.

The symmetries of a regular $n$-gon are therefore built from exactly the remainder classes of this module. Composing rotations *is* addition of remainders, which is why a clock returns to $3$ after twelve hours and why the same arithmetic governs both.

<Aside>
Later courses introduce a symbol meaning "$a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder on division by $n$", and it turns the case proofs above into one-liners.

For $3\mid(n^3-n)$: every $n$ is $\equiv 0$, $1$, or $2$, and cubing gives $0^3\equiv0$, $1^3\equiv1$, $2^3 = 8\equiv 2$. In each case $n^3\equiv n$, so $n^3-n\equiv0$.

The three cases became three multiplications, because congruences can be added and multiplied like equations. Fermat's little theorem generalizes this: for any prime $p$ and any $n$, $p \mid (n^p - n)$.
</Aside>
