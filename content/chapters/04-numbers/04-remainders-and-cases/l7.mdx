> **Theorem (Division algorithm).** For $m \in \mathbb{Z}$, $n \in \mathbb{Z}_{>0}$, there exist **unique** $q, r \in \mathbb{Z}$ with
> $$m = nq + r, \qquad 0 \leq r < n.$$

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$n > 0$. Consider $S = \{m - nk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\} \cap \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$ — *the non-negative numbers you can reach by subtracting whole multiples of $n$ from $m$*.</div>
  <div slot="want">Existence and uniqueness of $(q,r)$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Existence: $S$ is nonempty (take $k$ very negative) and consists of non-negative integers, so because every non-empty collection of non-negative integers has a smallest member — it has a smallest member $r$, and being smallest forces $r < n$. Uniqueness: two representations differ by a multiple of $n$ smaller than $n$ in absolute value, so by $0$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
*Existence.* Let $S = \{m - nk \mid k \in \mathbb{Z}\}\cap \mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}$. Taking $k = -|m|$ gives $m + n|m| \geq m + |m| \geq 0$, so $S \neq \emptyset$. Every non-empty collection of non-negative integers has a smallest member, so $S$ has a smallest member $r = m - nq$ for some $q \in \mathbb{Z}$, and $r \geq 0$.

If $r \geq n$ then $r - n = m - n(q+1) \geq 0$ lies in $S$ and is smaller than $r$, contradicting the fact that $r$ was the smallest. So $0 \leq r < n$.

*Uniqueness.* Suppose $m = nq_1 + r_1 = nq_2 + r_2$ with $0 \leq r_1, r_2 < n$. Then $n(q_1 - q_2) = r_2 - r_1$, so $n \mid (r_2 - r_1)$. But $|r_2 - r_1| < n$, and the only multiple of $n$ of absolute value less than $n$ is $0$. So $r_1 = r_2$, and then $n(q_1-q_2)=0$ with $n \neq 0$ gives $q_1 = q_2$.
</Proof>

The proof rests on one fact — **every non-empty set of non-negative integers has a smallest member** — which is equivalent to induction (Chapter 6) and is the property distinguishing $\mathbb{Z}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$. Note also that uniqueness used the fact that a product of non-zero integers is never zero (§4.1).

## Non-divisibility becomes a finite disjunction

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\nmid$ reads "does not divide", $\int$ reads "the integral of".*

$$
n \nmid m \iff \exists r \in \{1,\ldots,n-1\},\ \exists q \in \mathbb{Z},\ m = nq + r.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\nmid$ is "does not divide".*

A negative hypothesis, useless as such, becomes finitely many positive ones. This is the whole method: **residues turn negation into cases**.

Writing $a \equiv b \pmod n$ for $n \mid (a-b)$, the residue classes $[0], \ldots, [n-1]$ partition $\mathbb{Z}$, and congruence is compatible with $+$ and $\times$ — so one may compute with representatives, which is what the case analyzes below do.

## The showcase proposition

> **Proposition.** For all $a \in \mathbb{Z}$: $3 \nmid a^2 - 1 \Rightarrow 3 \mid a$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">A negative hypothesis about the composite quantity $a^2 - 1$, with a conclusion about $a$. Both of §1.8's indicators fire.</div>
  <div slot="want">$3 \mid a$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Contrapose to $3 \nmid a \Rightarrow 3 \mid a^2 - 1$, then convert the still-negative hypothesis into the two cases $a \equiv 1$ and $a \equiv 2 \pmod 3$ and compute.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
We prove the contrapositive (§1.7). Suppose $3 \nmid a$. By the division algorithm $a = 3k+r$ with $r \in \{0,1,2\}$, and $r = 0$ is excluded since it gives $3 \mid a$.

If $r=1$: $a^2 - 1 = 9k^2+6k = 3(3k^2+2k)$.
If $r=2$: $a^2 - 1 = 9k^2+12k+3 = 3(3k^2+4k+1)$.

In both cases the factor left over is an integer by closure, so $3 \mid a^2-1$. The cases are exhaustive, so the contrapositive (swap the two halves and deny both, §1.7) holds, and so does the proposition.
</Proof>

The proof composes five separate tools — contraposition (§1.8), the division algorithm, case analysis (§1.12), closure (§4.1), and divisibility-as-existence (§4.2). None is clever; the skill is selection and order.

<Warning title="Case discipline">
State the exhaustive list, then eliminate the impossible ones explicitly. An unmentioned excluded case is indistinguishable, to a reader, from a forgotten one — and forgotten cases are the commonest structural defect in this style of proof.
</Warning>

## Residues as an obstruction

> **Proposition.** $a^2 \equiv 0$ or $1 \pmod 3$ for every $a \in \mathbb{Z}$; never $2$.

<Proof>
With $a = 3k+r$, $r \in \{0,1,2\}$: $a^2$ is $9k^2$, then $9k^2+6k+1$, then $9k^2+12k+4$, leaving remainders $0$, $1$, $1$.
</Proof>

Consequences are immediate and sharp: no integer of the form $3n+2$ is a perfect square; the equation $a^2+b^2 = 3c^2$ has only the all-zero solution (reduce mod 3 and use that a sum of two elements of $\{0,1\}$ is $0$ only when both are); and mod $4$, the same technique shows $a^2+b^2 = 4^k(8m+7)$ is unsolvable, which is the negative half of the three-squares theorem.

Squares mod $8$ lie in $\{0,1,4\}$; cubes mod $9$ lie in $\{0,1,8\}$; fourth powers mod $16$ lie in $\{0,1\}$. **Reduce modulo a well-chosen $n$** is the standard first move against a Diophantine equation, and the art is picking $n$.

<Aside>
This method proves non-existence, never existence. An equation solvable modulo every $n$ may still have no integer solutions — the failure of the *Hasse principle*, first exhibited by Selmer's curve $3x^3+4y^3+5z^3=0$, which has not obvious solutions modulo every $n$ and none in $\mathbb{Z}$. Congruence obstructions are necessary conditions, not sufficient ones.
</Aside>

Division with remainder has a polynomial twin you already use, and the parallel is exact.

**Polynomial division.** For polynomials $f$ and $g \neq 0$ there exist unique $q$ and $r$ with
$$f = gq + r, \qquad \deg r < \deg g,$$
where the degree plays the role the size $|r| < n$ played here. Uniqueness is what makes "the remainder" a legitimate phrase, exactly as above.

The consequences are the ones you know. The **Remainder Theorem** says the remainder on dividing by $x - a$ is $f(a)$ — a one-line corollary, since $r$ is constant and evaluating at $a$ kills the $gq$ term. The **Factor Theorem** follows: $(x-a)$ divides $f$ exactly when $f(a) = 0$, which is the polynomial statement of *remainder zero means divides*.

Partial fractions is division with remainder plus factoring, and it is the reason every rational function can be integrated: reduce until the numerator has smaller degree, decompose, and integrate the pieces.

**Proof by cases is the technique behind every piecewise argument in calculus.** Absolute values force it — $\int |x|\,dx$, $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{|x|}{x}$, and every one-sided limit at a corner — and the thing to check is the one this module states: the cases must be exhaustive, and saying so is part of the proof.

</WhereThisGoes>
