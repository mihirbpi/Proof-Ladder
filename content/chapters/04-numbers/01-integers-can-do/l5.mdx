Chapters 1 to 3 built the machinery. This chapter points it at the number systems, starting with the one you know best.

A set is **closed** under an operation when applying that operation to elements of the set always produces an element of the set. *You cannot get out.*

## What ℤ can do

For all $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$:

$$
a + b \in \mathbb{Z}, \qquad -a \in \mathbb{Z}, \qquad ab \in \mathbb{Z},
$$
$$
a(b+c) = ab + ac.
$$

Closure under addition, additive inverses, and multiplication, plus the **distributive law**. Subtraction comes free, since $a - b = a + (-b)$.

These look too obvious to state, and stating them is the point. **From here on, every proof that needs "and that is an integer" cites closure**, and you have now seen the list of what may be cited. Module 4.3's divisibility proofs end with exactly this step, every time.

## What ℤ cannot do

$$
\frac13 \notin \mathbb{Z}.
$$

$\mathbb{Z}$ is **not** closed under division. And the failure is not only at $3$ — it is almost total.

> **Proposition.** The only integers with integer reciprocals are $1$ and $-1$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$a$ has an integer reciprocal means: there is $b \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $ab = 1$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$a = 1$ or $a = -1$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Both $a$ and $b$ are integers, so both have absolute value at least $1$ unless zero. Their product being $1$ leaves very little room.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $ab = 1$.

Neither is zero, since $0 \cdot b = 0 \neq 1$. So $|a| \geq 1$ and $|b| \geq 1$.

Then $1 = |ab| = |a|\,|b| \geq |a| \cdot 1 = |a|$, so $|a| \leq 1$. Combined with $|a| \geq 1$, we get $|a| = 1$.

Hence $a = 1$ or $a = -1$. (Both work: $1\cdot1 = 1$ and $(-1)(-1) = 1$.)
</Proof>

The step doing the work is that there are **no integers strictly between $0$ and $1$**. That fact is what separates $\mathbb{Z}$ from $\mathbb{Q}$, and module 4.6 shows just how differently the rationals behave.

## The word for this

A number system closed under $+$, $-$, and $\times$, with the distributive law, is called a **ring**.

That is all the word means here: **a label for a system with these rules.** It is worth having because $\mathbb{Z}$ is not the only such system, and recognising the pattern saves work.

You have met two others:

- **Polynomials.** Add, subtract, multiply two polynomials and you get a polynomial. Divide and you generally do not.
- **Square grids of numbers** — rows and columns of them, called *matrices*, which Algebra II introduces along with rules for adding and multiplying. Same story: closed under $+$, $-$, $\times$, and division mostly unavailable.

The parallel with $\mathbb{Z}$ is exact, including the failure. Most of those grids cannot be undone by another one; most integers have no reciprocal; most polynomials have no polynomial reciprocal. **A theorem proved from the ring rules alone holds in all three at once**, and that is why the label earns its keep rather than being vocabulary for its own sake.

Module 4.5 adds the missing rule and gets a **field**.

<Warning title="Closure is a claim about every case">
"$\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under addition" is a universal statement:
$$
\forall a, b \in \mathbb{Z},\ a + b \in \mathbb{Z}.
$$

So checking examples does not prove it, and one counterexample would destroy it — module 1.11's asymmetry.

Watch the direction carefully. To show a set is **not** closed, exhibit one escape: $\mathbb{Z}$ is not closed under division because $1 \div 3 \notin \mathbb{Z}$, and that single instance is a complete disproof.

To show a set **is** closed takes a general argument. Module 4.5 gives several for $\mathbb{Q}$, and each is a short computation with an arbitrary element rather than a check of cases.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Closure is why the number systems were built in the order they were, and each extension repairs one failure.

$\mathbb{N}$ is not closed under subtraction — $3 - 5$ escapes. Adding negatives gives $\mathbb{Z}$.

$\mathbb{Z}$ is not closed under division. Adding reciprocals gives $\mathbb{Q}$.

$\mathbb{Q}$ has gaps: no rational squares to $2$ (module 4.7). Filling them gives $\mathbb{R}$.

$\mathbb{R}$ is not closed under square roots of negatives. Adding them gives $\mathbb{C}$, and Chapter 5 does it.

$$
\mathbb{N} \subseteq \mathbb{Z} \subseteq \mathbb{Q} \subseteq \mathbb{R} \subseteq \mathbb{C}
$$

**Each step exists because the previous system could not do something.** The chain is not a list of facts to memorise; it is a record of four problems and their solutions.
</Aside>
