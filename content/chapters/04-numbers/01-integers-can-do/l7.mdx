Every integer proof you have written invokes properties of $\mathbb{Z}$ that were never stated. Stating them serves two purposes: it closes the gaps, and it reveals that the properties, not the integers, are what the proofs are really about.

## The axioms

An **axiom** is a rule assumed at the start rather than proved from something earlier; §7.1 examines what that really commits you to. For now, take the following as the rules of arithmetic and watch how much comes out of them.

$(\mathbb{Z}, +, \cdot)$ satisfies, for all $a,b,c$:

**Additive.** Associativity $(a+b)+c = a+(b+c)$; commutativity $a+b=b+a$; identity $a + 0 = a$; inverses $a + (-a) = 0$.

**Multiplicative.** Associativity $(ab)c = a(bc)$; commutativity $ab = ba$; identity $a\cdot 1 = a$.

**Linking.** Distributivity $a(b+c) = ab+ac$.

A structure satisfying all of these is a **system obeying the standard arithmetic rules with identity**. Closure is implicit in calling $+$ and $\cdot$ operations that take two integers and return one, $\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$ — which, note, is a statement about functions in the sense of Chapter 3.

Facts that look primitive are in fact derived, and deriving them is worth doing:

<Proof title="Proof that a · 0 = 0">
$a\cdot 0 = a\cdot(0+0) = a\cdot 0 + a\cdot 0$ by distributivity. Adding $-(a\cdot 0)$ to both sides gives $0 = a\cdot 0$.
</Proof>

Similarly $(-a)b = -(ab)$ and $(-a)(-b) = ab$ follow from the axioms alone. "A negative times a negative is a positive" is a *theorem*, not a convention — a fact worth knowing, since it is usually taught as a rule to memorize.

## Units

$u \in \mathbb{Z}$ is a **unit** if $uv = 1$ for some $v \in \mathbb{Z}$.

> **Proposition.** $\mathbb{Z}^\times = \{1, -1\}$.

<Proof>
Let $uv = 1$. Then $u, v \neq 0$, so $|u|, |v| \geq 1$, and $|u||v| = |uv| = 1$. If $|u| \geq 2$ then $|u||v| \geq 2$, contradiction. So $|u| = 1$, i.e. $u = \pm 1$; and both are units, being self-inverse.
</Proof>

The argument used the order on $\mathbb{Z}$ and the fact that no integer lies strictly between $0$ and $1$ — the discreteness of $\mathbb{Z}$, not an arithmetic one. This is worth noticing: some properties of $\mathbb{Z}$ are algebraic and some are order-theoretic, and separating them is what makes the general theory possible.

## A product of non-zero integers is never zero

$\mathbb{Z}$ satisfies more than the arithmetic rules:
$$
ab = 0 \Rightarrow a = 0 \text{ or } b = 0,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

In words: **a product can only be zero if one of the factors is zero.** Equivalently, cancellation holds: $ab = ac$ with $a \neq 0$ gives $b = c$.

<Warning title="Cancellation is not free">
In $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$, $3 \cdot 4 = 0$ with both factors nonzero, and $3 \cdot 4 = 3 \cdot 8 = 0$ while $4 \neq 8$. So canceling a common factor is illegal there.

Every proof that divides both sides by something is using cancellation, so the domain property. In $\mathbb{Z}$ this is safe. In systems where a product of nonzeros can vanish it is not, and knowing which you are in is the difference between a proof and an error.
</Warning>

## Consequences of the axioms alone

The $a\cdot0=0$ proof above used nothing about zero except that it is the additive identity. Two more in the same spirit, each a fact people treat as undefined starting word (§7.1: a word deliberately left undefined at the start):

> **Proposition.** $(-a)b = -(ab)$.

$ab + (-a)b = (a + (-a))b = 0\cdot b = 0$ by distributivity and the previous result, so $(-a)b$ is the additive inverse of $ab$. The "rule of signs" is a theorem.

> **Proposition.** $(-a)(-b) = ab$.

Apply the previous proposition twice. That negative times negative is positive is not a convention adopted for convenience — it is forced, and any system in which it failed would violate distributivity.

Each proof used only the table. That is what makes them theorems about *every* system obeying the standard arithmetic rules, including matrices and polynomials, at once — and it is why a single page of algebra can replace a separate treatment of each system.

The converse (the same statement with its two halves swapped, §1.7) habit is worth building too: when a familiar identity **fails** in a new system, the failure identifies which axiom is missing. Matrices lose commutativity of multiplication, and with it $(A+B)^2 = A^2+2AB+B^2$; $\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}$ loses the domain property, and with it cancellation. In both cases the broken identity points straight at the absent row of the table.

## Why the abstraction pays

The axioms are satisfied by structures with nothing else in common:

| System | Which elements have reciprocals | Can a product of two nonzeros be zero? |
|---|---|---|
| the integers | only $1$ and $-1$ | no |
| the rationals, reals and complex numbers | every nonzero one — §4.5 shows this is what makes division work | no |
| polynomials with integer coefficients | only $1$ and $-1$ | no |
| square grids of real numbers | the ones that can be undone | yes, and the order of multiplication matters |
| the hours on an $n$-hour clock | those sharing no factor with $n$ | yes, unless $n$ is prime |
| continuous functions on the line (unbroken graphs) | those never equal to zero | yes |

Anything proved from the axioms alone holds in every row. That move — prove it once from the rules, then use it everywhere the rules hold — is what the later algebra courses are built on, and it is why the vocabulary is worth acquiring before you need it.

<Aside>
The three number systems of Chapter 4 stack, each adding one thing: the integers obey the arithmetic rules, the fractions add reciprocals, and the reals add the no-gaps property of §4.6. Each addition buys theorems and costs generality. $\mathbb{Z}$ additionally has properties $\mathbb{Q}$ lacks — its numbers come in separate steps, any collection of them going down stops somewhere, and it has a division algorithm — which is why number theory is a subject about $\mathbb{Z}$ and not about $\mathbb{Q}$.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

The list of properties in this module is the checklist you will run against every new system in the courses ahead — and the interesting entries are the ones that fail.

**Polynomials obey this same checklist.** You can add, subtract and multiply two polynomials and get a polynomial; you cannot always divide one by another and stay in the family. That is exactly the pattern $\mathbb{Z}$ has. The parallel is close enough that polynomial long division, the Factor Theorem, and the Remainder Theorem are the polynomial versions of §4.2–§4.4, with degree playing the role of size.

**In calculus, the arithmetic rules are what make the derivative rules provable.** The rule that the derivative — the number saying how steeply a graph is rising at a point, which is what a first calculus course is built to compute — of a sum is the sum of the derivatives is distributivity in disguise; and the product rule is a statement about what differentiating does to a product. The surprise there — that it is *not* simply $f'g'$ — is the first sign that taking a derivative does not pass through multiplication the way it passes through addition.

**And the numbers you multiply by have to be checked first.** When the courses ahead set up a new kind of object, the first question they ask is which *numbers* you are allowed to multiply by — and the answer is a system with every property listed in this module, which is §4.5. The setup is stated as a list of rules, exactly like the list you just read.
