Every proof you write about integers uses facts about $\mathbb{Z}$ that you have never stated. This module states them, because a proof that cites an unstated fact is a proof with a hole in it.

$$
\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \ldots\}.
$$

## Closure

$S$ is **closed** under a binary operation when applying it to elements of $S$ always yields an element of $S$.

For all $a, b \in \mathbb{Z}$:

| Property | Statement |
|---|---|
| closed under addition | $a + b \in \mathbb{Z}$ |
| closed under additive inverses | $-a \in \mathbb{Z}$ |
| closed under multiplication | $ab \in \mathbb{Z}$ |
| distributive law | $a(b+c) = ab + ac$ |

Closure under subtraction follows: $a - b = a + (-b)$, an integer by the first two.

These are exactly the facts you have been invoking silently. When a proof says "$n = 2(2k^2)$, and $2k^2 \in \mathbb{Z}$, so $n$ is even", the step "$2k^2 \in \mathbb{Z}$" is closure under multiplication used twice. From now on, cite it.

## Failure of multiplicative inverses

$\mathbb{Z}$ is **not** closed under division. Sharper: an integer $n$ is a **unit** if there is an integer $m$ with $nm = 1$.

> **Proposition.** The only units in $\mathbb{Z}$ are $1$ and $-1$.

<Proof>
Suppose $n, m \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $nm = 1$. Then $n \neq 0$, since $0 \cdot m = 0 \neq 1$.

Since $nm = 1 \neq 0$, both $n$ and $m$ are nonzero, so $|n| \geq 1$ and $|m| \geq 1$. Taking absolute values, $|n|\,|m| = 1$.

If $|n| \geq 2$ then $|n|\,|m| \geq 2 \cdot 1 = 2 > 1$, a contradiction. Hence $|n| = 1$, so $n \in \{1, -1\}$.

Conversely $1 \cdot 1 = 1$ and $(-1)(-1) = 1$, so both are units.
</Proof>

Two units out of infinitely many elements. This single deficiency is what motivates everything else in the chapter: module 4.5 constructs $\mathbb{Q}$, where every nonzero element is a unit.

<Warning title="Closure is a universal claim">
$6 \div 2 = 3 \in \mathbb{Z}$, so division sometimes stays inside. Closure requires *always*, so a single counterexample refutes it (module 1.11). $7 \div 2 \notin \mathbb{Z}$ is that counterexample.
</Warning>

## One list of rules, many systems

Collect what we have: add, subtract and multiply and you never leave; order and grouping do not matter; $0$ and $1$ change nothing; and multiplication distributes over addition.

**That same list is obeyed by systems with nothing else in common.**

- **Polynomials** — expressions like $x^2 + 3x - 1$, which you take up in Algebra I. Add two or multiply two and you get another one; distribution holds.
- **Grids of numbers** — rows and columns of them, called *matrices* in Algebra II, which gives rules for adding and multiplying them. Same closures, one twist: the order of multiplication matters.
- **Clock arithmetic.** On a twelve-hour clock, add or multiply and keep the remainder on division by $12$. Closed, distributive, finite.

So a proof using **only** those rules is not really about the integers. It proves the same thing for every system obeying the list, all at once, for no extra work.

Hence the most useful habit in this chapter: when you finish a proof, ask which rules you actually leaned on. The fewer you used, the more places the result holds.

<Aside>
The integers have one property the list above does not mention, and it matters more than it looks: **if a product is $0$, one of the factors is $0$.**

That fails on the twelve-hour clock, where $3 \times 4$ lands on $0$ with neither factor zero. And its failure is exactly why cancelling is safe with integers and unsafe on the clock: from $ab = ac$ with $a \neq 0$ you may conclude $b = c$ over the integers, and you may not on the clock.

Later courses give the list of rules a name, and this extra property a name as well. The names are not the point. Noticing which rules a proof leans on is.
</Aside>

## What is still missing

One thing is conspicuously absent from the list: **division**.

You can add, subtract and multiply integers all day and never leave the integers. Divide, and you fall out almost immediately — $1 \div 3$ is not an integer. Module 4.5 repairs exactly that, and the repair is what fractions are for.

Knowing which system you are working in tells you which moves are legal — in particular, whether you are allowed to divide at all.
