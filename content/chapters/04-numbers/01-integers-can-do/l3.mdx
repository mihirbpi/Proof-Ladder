You have been using the **counting numbers** — $0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots$ — for years. This chapter asks a question nobody usually asks: *what rules do they actually obey?*

The point is not to doubt arithmetic. It is that once you list the rules, you can check whether other things obey them too — and that turns out to be how a great deal of mathematics gets organized.

## Closed

A set is **closed** under an operation when doing that operation to members of the set always lands you back inside the set.

**Adding.** Take any two counting numbers and add them. Do you ever land outside the counting numbers?

$7 + 5 = 12$. Inside. $0 + 9 = 9$. Inside. $438 + 62 = 500$. Inside.

You never escape. Adding can only make counting numbers bigger, and bigger counting numbers are still counting numbers. So the counting numbers are **closed under adding**.

**Multiplying.** Same story.

$6 \times 7 = 42$. Inside. $9 \times 0 = 0$. Inside.

Closed again. Multiplying is repeated adding, so if adding cannot get you out, neither can multiplying.

## The distributive law

There is a third rule, and it is the one that ties adding and multiplying together.

$$
3 \times (4 + 5) = 3 \times 4 + 3 \times 5.
$$

Check it: the left side is $3 \times 9 = 27$. The right side is $12 + 15 = 27$. ✓

This is the **distributive law**, and you have used it since you first learned to multiply — it is why $3 \times 12$ can be done as $3 \times 10 + 3 \times 2$.

It holds for *any* three counting numbers, and it is worth seeing why with a picture. Draw a rectangle $3$ tall and $9$ wide. Now cut it after the fourth column. You have a $3$-by-$4$ rectangle and a $3$-by-$5$ rectangle, and together they are the whole thing. Same squares, counted two ways.

## What the counting numbers cannot do

Now the gaps — and there are two.

**Subtracting can throw you out.**

$9 - 4 = 5$. Fine. But $4 - 9$? There is no counting number that answers that. You have run off the end of the number line.

**Dividing can throw you out.**

$12 \div 4 = 3$. Fine. But $7 \div 2$? Not a counting number either — you have to cut something in half.

<Warning title="Closed means always">
$9 - 4$ and $12 \div 4$ both work out fine. So subtracting and dividing *sometimes* stay inside.

**Closed** means it works *every* time, with no exceptions. And one escape is enough to break it — that is one bad apple, from module 1.11.
</Warning>

## Where new numbers come from

Those two gaps are exactly where new numbers get invented.

The counting numbers could not answer $7 \div 2$. So people built the **fractions**, and now $7 \div 2 = \tfrac72$ has an answer.

The counting numbers could not answer $4 - 9$. So people built numbers below zero, which you will meet next year.

<Proof>
Here is a small thing worth proving about the gap. Which counting numbers have a counting-number *reciprocal* — that is, something you can multiply them by to get $1$?

Take any counting number bigger than $1$ — say $5$. To get $1$ you would need to multiply by something smaller than $1$, because multiplying $5$ by $1$ or more only gets bigger.

But there is no counting number between $0$ and $1$. And $0$ times anything is $0$, never $1$.

So the only counting number with a counting-number reciprocal is $1$ itself, since $1 \times 1 = 1$.

One number out of infinitely many. The counting numbers are almost entirely without division.
</Proof>

<Aside>
A number system closed under adding and multiplying and obeying the distributive law has a name. Mathematicians call it a *ring*. The word is worth knowing because the same rules are obeyed by things that are not numbers at all — including shapes you can rotate, and clock arithmetic. Anything you prove using only these rules is automatically true for all of them at once.
</Aside>
