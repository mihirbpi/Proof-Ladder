You have been using the **integers** — the whole numbers together with their negatives — for years. This chapter asks a question nobody usually asks: *what rules do they actually obey?*

$$
\mathbb{Z} = \{\ldots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}
$$

The point is not to doubt arithmetic. It is that once you list the rules, you can check whether other things obey them too — and that turns out to be how a great deal of mathematics gets organised.

## Closed

A set is **closed** under an operation when performing that operation on members of the set always lands you back inside the set.

$\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under **addition**: add any two integers and you get an integer. Try to escape — you cannot.

$\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under **opposites**: the opposite of $7$ is $-7$, and the opposite of $-7$ is $7$. Both integers.

$\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under **multiplication**: $(-3) \times 4 = -12$, an integer.

These three facts get used constantly, and usually without being mentioned. Every time a proof ends "and since $2k^2$ is an integer, the number is even," it is closure being cited.

## The distributive law

There is a fourth rule, and it is the one that ties adding and multiplying together:
$$
a \times (b + c) = a \times b + a \times c.
$$

Check it: $3 \times (4 + 5) = 3 \times 9 = 27$, and $3 \times 4 + 3 \times 5 = 12 + 15 = 27$. ✓

This is the **distributive law**. You have used it since you first learned to multiply — it is why $3 \times 12$ can be done as $3 \times 10 + 3 \times 2$.

## What the integers cannot do

Now the gap. Is $\mathbb{Z}$ closed under **division**?

No. $7 \div 2$ is not an integer.

A sharper way to say it: which integers have an integer *reciprocal*? That is, for which $n$ is there an integer $m$ with $n \times m = 1$?

<Proof>
Suppose $n$ and $m$ are integers with $n \times m = 1$.

Neither can be $0$, since $0$ times anything is $0$, not $1$.

If $n$ were $2$ or bigger, then $m$ would have to be $\tfrac{1}{n}$, which sits strictly between $0$ and $1$ — and there are no integers strictly between $0$ and $1$.

The same argument rules out $n$ being $-2$ or smaller.

That leaves only $n = 1$ (with $m = 1$) and $n = -1$ (with $m = -1$).
</Proof>

So out of infinitely many integers, exactly **two** have integer reciprocals. The integers are almost entirely without division.

<Warning title="Closed means always">
$6 \div 2 = 3$ is an integer, so division *sometimes* works. Closure requires it to work *every* time. One escape — $7 \div 2$ — settles the matter, by one bad apple (module 1.11).
</Warning>

## Where this is going

That missing division is a hole, and the rest of this chapter is about it.

<Aside>
A number system with these four rules — closed under $+$, opposites, and $\times$, and obeying the distributive law — has a name. Mathematicians call it a *ring*. The word is worth knowing because the same rules are obeyed by things that are not numbers at all: polynomials, and grids of numbers, and clock arithmetic. Anything you prove using only these rules is automatically true for all of them at once.
</Aside>
