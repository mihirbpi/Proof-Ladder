Module 4.5 showed the fractions are beautifully behaved: add, multiply, or divide two of them and you always land on another fraction.

What about the numbers left over — the ones on the number line that are *not* fractions, like the diagonal length from module 4.7?

They are a mess. And the mess is instructive.

## Closure fails

**Multiplying.** The diagonal length is not a fraction. But multiply it by itself and you get $2$ — and $2$ is a fraction, since $2 = \tfrac21$.

So two not-fractions multiplied together landed back among the fractions. The not-fractions are **not closed under multiplying**.

**Adding.** Take the diagonal length, and take the number you need to add to it to reach $3$. Neither is a fraction. But added together they give $3$, which is.

Not closed under adding either.

**And there is no $1$ and no $0$.** Both are fractions, so neither is even in the collection. There is not much of a number system left.

<Warning title="Not closed, but not always broken">
Some pairs of not-fractions *do* add to a not-fraction. So "not closed" does not mean it always fails — it means it fails at least once.

That is exactly what module 4.1 said closure means, and one bad case is enough (module 1.11). You can never predict which way a particular pair will go without doing the work.
</Warning>

## What does survive

Not everything falls apart. Here are two things that are always true, and both are proved the same way: **suppose not**, and use the fact that fractions stay fractions.

> **Claim 1.** Double a not-fraction and you still have a not-fraction.

<Proof>
Take any not-fraction and follow it. Suppose not — suppose its double *is* a fraction.

Halving is multiplying by $\tfrac12$, which is a fraction. And multiplying two fractions gives a fraction (module 4.5).

So halving that double gives a fraction.

But halving the double gets you back to the number you started with. So the number you started with is a fraction.

That contradicts what we were told. So the double must not be a fraction after all.
</Proof>

> **Claim 2.** Multiply a not-fraction by any fraction except zero, and you still have a not-fraction.

<Proof>
Take any not-fraction and any fraction that is not zero, and follow them. Suppose not — suppose the product *is* a fraction.

Since our fraction is not zero, it has a reciprocal, and that reciprocal is a fraction (module 4.5).

Multiply the product by that reciprocal. Multiplying two fractions gives a fraction, so the answer is a fraction.

But multiplying by the reciprocal undoes the original multiplication — so the answer is the number we started with.

So the not-fraction is a fraction. Contradiction. The product must not be a fraction.
</Proof>

Claim 1 is really Claim 2 with the fraction being $2$. Noticing that is worth more than the two separate proofs — one general result covering both is usually better mathematics than two specific ones.

## The pattern

Look at both proofs. They have the identical shape:

1. Suppose the thing you built *is* a fraction.
2. Undo what you did, using an operation that keeps fractions as fractions.
3. Land back on the number you started with, proving it is a fraction.
4. Contradiction.

The engine is always the same: **fractions are closed, so anything you can build out of fractions is a fraction.** Being a not-fraction is a statement about *not* being reachable that way — and "suppose not" is the natural tool for a claim about what cannot happen.

<Aside>
There is a famous puzzle hiding here. Can a not-fraction raised to the power of a not-fraction ever come out as a fraction?

It can — and the proof is startling, because it shows such a pair exists without ever telling you which pair it is. You will meet it in a few years, and it is worth the wait.
</Aside>
