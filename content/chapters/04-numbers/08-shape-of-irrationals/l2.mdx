Module 4.7 introduced the diagonal of a square — a length that is a **not-a-piece number**. Let us give it a short name and call it $d$.

In module 4.1 you learned that whole numbers are *closed*: add two and you get another whole number, every time.

Are the not-a-piece numbers closed? Let's find out.

## Walking out and back

Take a strip of paper as long as $d$. Lay it on the floor and walk its length. You are standing at a strange spot — no piece lands there.

Now turn around and walk the very same length back.

Where are you? **Right where you started.** At zero.

And zero is not strange at all. Zero is the most ordinary number there is.

So two strange lengths, put together, gave a perfectly ordinary answer. **Not closed.**

## Making a square

Try again a different way. Take four strips of length $d$ and build a square with them.

How much room is inside? Module 4.7 said this square holds exactly **two** of the little squares with sides of $1$.

So two strange lengths made a square worth exactly $2$ — and $2$ is a perfectly ordinary whole number.

**Not closed** that way either.

<Warning title="The not-a-piece numbers are a messy group">
The whole numbers are tidy — you cannot escape them by adding. The pieces are even tidier.

The not-a-piece numbers are not tidy at all. You can escape them in one move, going either way. They do not even contain $0$ or $1$, since both of those are perfectly ordinary.
</Warning>

## But something does survive

Not everything falls apart. Here is one thing that always works.

If $d$ is a not-a-piece number, then **double** $d$ is a not-a-piece number too.

Why? Think about it backwards. Suppose double $d$ *were* an ordinary piece. Then you could fold it in half — and half of a piece is always a piece — and you would get $d$ back.

But $d$ is not a piece. So double $d$ cannot be one either.

That is **suppose not** again (module 2.5), used in three sentences.

<Aside>
The same story works for tripling, and for taking a third, and for stretching by any ordinary piece you like. If you stretch a strange length by an ordinary amount, it stays strange.

It is only when you put *two strange lengths together* that things can turn ordinary.
</Aside>

<TryIt>
Cut a strip of paper any length you like. Walk its length, then turn and walk back. Where do you end up? Now fold the strip in half and check that half of it is still a real length.
</TryIt>
