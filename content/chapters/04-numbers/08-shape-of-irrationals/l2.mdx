Module 4.7 introduced the diagonal of a square — a length that is a **not-a-fraction**. Let us give it a short name and call it $d$.

In module 4.1 you learned that whole numbers are *closed*: add two and you get another whole number, every time.

Are the not-a-fractions closed? Let's find out.

## Adding

Take $d$. Now take the opposite of $d$, which is $-d$. Both are not-a-fractions.

Add them:
$$d + (-d) = 0.$$

Zero! And zero is $\tfrac01$, which is definitely a fraction.

So two not-a-fractions added up to a fraction. **Not closed.**

## Multiplying

Try again with multiplying. Take $d$ and $d$.

Now, $d$ is the number whose square is $2$. So
$$d \times d = 2.$$

And $2$ is $\tfrac21$, a perfectly good fraction!

So two not-a-fractions multiplied to a fraction. **Not closed either.**

<Warning title="The not-a-fractions are a messy group">
The whole numbers are tidy — you cannot escape them by adding or multiplying. The fractions are even tidier.

The not-a-fractions are not tidy at all. You can escape them in one move, going either way. They do not even contain $0$ or $1$, since both of those are fractions.
</Warning>

## But something does survive

Not everything falls apart. Here is one thing that always works.

If $d$ is a not-a-fraction, then so is $-d$.

Why? Think about it backwards. Suppose $-d$ *were* a fraction. Then you could take its opposite and get $d$ — and the opposite of a fraction is always a fraction. So $d$ would be a fraction too.

But $d$ is not. So $-d$ cannot be a fraction either.

That is **suppose not** again (module 2.5), used in three sentences.

<Aside>
Same story with doubling. If $d$ is a not-a-fraction, so is $2 \times d$. Because if $2d$ were a fraction, you could halve it — and half of a fraction is a fraction — and you would get $d$ back, which is impossible.
</Aside>
