## Closure fails, in every way

Let $\mathbb{I} = \mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ denote the irrationals.

**Not closed under addition.** $\sqrt2$ and $-\sqrt2$ are irrational, and $\sqrt2 + (-\sqrt2) = 0$ is rational.

**Not closed under multiplication.** $\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2 = 2$.

**Not closed under subtraction or division.** $\sqrt2 - \sqrt2 = 0$; $\sqrt2/\sqrt2 = 1$.

Each is a single counterexample refuting a universal claim — module 1.11's rule, and it takes one line each.

<Warning title="A counterexample refutes; it does not describe">
Showing $\sqrt2 + (-\sqrt2)$ is rational does **not** mean sums of irrationals are usually rational. $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$ is irrational, as is $\sqrt2+\pi$.

The counterexample refutes the *universal* claim "every such sum is irrational" and says nothing about the general case. Both outcomes are possible, which is exactly what "not closed" means.

Confusing "not always" with "never" is a De Morgan error (module 1.4): $\neg\forall$ is $\exists\neg$, not $\forall\neg$.
</Warning>

So the irrationals are not a self-contained number system — they are not closed under anything. It is a set — the complement of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ — and that is all.

## What does hold

The stable facts all take the same form, and all are proved the same way: assume the result is rational, and use closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ to contradict something.

> **Proposition.** If $a$ is rational and $b$ is irrational, then $a + b$ is irrational.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$a\in\mathbb{Q}$, $b\notin\mathbb{Q}$. Available: $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under subtraction.</div>
  <div slot="want">$a+b\notin\mathbb{Q}$ — a negative claim, with nothing to build from.</div>
  <div slot="how">Suppose not. Then $a+b$ is rational, and so is $a$; subtracting recovers $b$ inside $\mathbb{Q}$, contradicting the hypothesis. The move is to use closure to manufacture the forbidden conclusion.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose not: suppose $a+b$ is rational. Write $s = a+b$, so $s\in\mathbb{Q}$.

Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under subtraction and both $s$ and $a$ are rational,
$$s - a \in \mathbb{Q}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\in$ is "is in".*

But $s - a = (a+b)-a = b$. So $b\in\mathbb{Q}$, contradicting that $b$ is irrational.

So $a+b$ is irrational.
</Proof>

> **Proposition.** If $a$ is a **non-zero** rational and $b$ is irrational, then $ab$ is irrational.

<Proof>
Suppose not: suppose $ab$ is rational, say $ab = p\in\mathbb{Q}$.

Since $a\neq0$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under division by non-zero rationals, $\frac pa\in\mathbb{Q}$.

But $\frac pa = \frac{ab}{a} = b$. So $b$ is rational — a contradiction.

So $ab$ is irrational.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Why a ≠ 0 is required">
Drop it and the statement is false: $0\cdot\sqrt2 = 0$, which is rational.

The proof shows exactly where the hypothesis is used — the division step. **When a proof needs a hypothesis, deleting the hypothesis produces a counterexample at exactly that step**, and tracing that connection is a good way to check you have understood an argument.
</Warning>

## A family of corollaries

Both propositions cover a range of cases at once:

- $-b$ is irrational when $b$ is — take $a = -1$ in the second.
- $b/2$, $3b$, $b+7$, $\frac{2b}{5}-1$ are all irrational when $b$ is.
- $5\sqrt2 - 3$ is irrational, applying both.

**One theorem, many instances** — the same economy as module 4.3's linear combinations, and the same reason to prove the general statement first.

## Sums of two irrationals

Nothing general holds, and both outcomes occur:

$$\sqrt2 + \sqrt2 = 2\sqrt2 \quad\text{irrational}$$
$$\sqrt2 + (2-\sqrt2) = 2 \quad\text{rational}$$

The method breaks down for a locatable reason. Assuming $\sqrt2+\sqrt3 = q$ rational and subtracting gives $\sqrt3 = q - \sqrt2$, and the right side is a *combination* of a rational and an irrational rather than a rational — so nothing is contradicted.

Proving $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ irrational is possible and needs a different idea: square it to get $5 + 2\sqrt6$, then apply the results above plus the irrationality of $\sqrt6$. Try it; the two propositions of this module do all the work once the squaring is done.

## Both sets are crowded, and only one is self-contained

Module 4.6 showed the rationals and irrationals are thoroughly interleaved: every interval contains infinitely many of each.

So the difference between them is **not** positional. It is algebraic — one is closed under the operations and the other is not — and it is a matter of size, since module 3.8's argument shows the irrationals are strictly more numerous.

That is three genuinely different ways two subsets of the line can differ: where they sit, how they behave under arithmetic, and how many there are. Keeping the three apart is worth the effort, because informal talk about "how much of the line" a set occupies runs them together.

<Aside>
The irrationals split further.

An **algebraic** number is a root of a polynomial with integer coefficients. $\sqrt2$ is one — it satisfies $x^2 - 2 = 0$ — and so is $\sqrt[3]{7}$, and so is every rational.

A **transcendental** number is not the root of any such polynomial. $\pi$ and $e$ are transcendental, which is far harder to prove than irrationality: Hermite settled $e$ in 1873, Lindemann settled $\pi$ in 1882.

Lindemann's result closed a problem that had been open since the ancient world. **Squaring the circle** — constructing, with compass and straightedge, a square of the same area as a given circle — requires constructing $\sqrt\pi$, and compass-and-straightedge constructions can only produce certain algebraic numbers. $\pi$ being transcendental proves the construction impossible.

Two thousand years of failed attempts, ended by a theorem about numbers rather than a theorem about figures.
</Aside>
