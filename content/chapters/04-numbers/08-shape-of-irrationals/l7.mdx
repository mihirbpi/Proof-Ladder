## Failures

$0, 1 \in \mathbb{Q}$, so the irrationals contain neither identity. They are closed under neither operation:
$$
\sqrt2 + (-\sqrt2) = 0, \qquad \sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2 = 2.
$$

The general statement: **the complement of a subfield is never a subfield**, since it omits $0$ and $1$. So the question was answered before it was asked; what remains is which weaker properties survive.

<Warning title="No rule of thumb">
$\sqrt2+\sqrt3 \notin \mathbb{Q}$ but $\sqrt2 + (2-\sqrt2) = 2 \in \mathbb{Q}$. Sums and products of irrationals may be either, and deciding requires work — even $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ needs an argument (square twice, or use the rational root theorem on its minimal polynomial $x^4-10x^2+1$).

Statements like "irrational plus irrational is irrational" are false, and their negations are equally false.
</Warning>

## Stability under rational operations

> **Proposition.** Let $a \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$, $r \in \mathbb{Q}$. Then $r + a$ is irrational, and if $r \neq 0$ so is $ra$. In particular $-a$ and (since $a \neq 0$) $1/a$ are irrational.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$a \notin \mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under all four operations.</div>
  <div slot="want">Negative statements about constructed quantities.</div>
  <div slot="how">Contradiction (§2.5): assume the construction is rational and use closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ to recover $a$. All four cases are the same three lines with a different operation.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose $r + a \in \mathbb{Q}$. Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under subtraction, $(r+a) - r = a \in \mathbb{Q}$ — contradiction.

Suppose $r \neq 0$ and $ra \in \mathbb{Q}$. Then $r^{-1} \in \mathbb{Q}$, and by closure under multiplication $r^{-1}(ra) = a \in \mathbb{Q}$ — contradiction.

Taking $r = -1$ gives $-a$; and $a \neq 0$ since $0 \in \mathbb{Q}$, so applying the argument to $1/a$ (whose inverse is $a$) gives the last claim.
</Proof>

Each proof is the same three steps: assume rational, apply closure to recover $a$, contradict. Irrationality is *non-membership*, so the only available handle is what membership would force — which is why every theorem in this module is a contradiction proof.

## The structural statement

Group the reals into families: put $x$ and $y$ in the same family exactly when $x-y$ is rational. This is an equivalence relation (§7.2), so the families partition $\mathbb{R}$. One family is $\mathbb{Q}$ itself; every other is a shifted copy $a+\mathbb{Q}$. The proposition says exactly:

- $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ is everything outside the single family $\mathbb{Q}$;
- adding a rational moves you **within** a family, so preserves irrationality;
- multiplying by a non-zero rational permutes the families, so also preserves irrationality;
- adding two irrationals combines two families neither of which is $\mathbb{Q}$ — and the result may land back in $\mathbb{Q}$, which is exactly why no theorem is available.

Viewed this way, none of the results is surprising. The failures and the stabilities are both consequences of $\mathbb{Q}$ being a closed sub-collection and a subfield, not facts about $\sqrt2$.

## Why the method stalls on sums of two irrationals

The stability proofs all worked the same way: assume the result is rational, and use closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ to recover the irrational hypothesis inside $\mathbb{Q}$.

Attempt the same on $\sqrt2 + \sqrt3$. Suppose it equals a rational $q$. Then $\sqrt3 = q - \sqrt2$, and the right side is a rational minus an irrational — which is irrational, by the results above, and therefore consistent with $\sqrt3$ being irrational. **Nothing is contradicted**, and the method genuinely stops.

The repair is to square. From $\sqrt2+\sqrt3 = q$,
$$q^2 = 2 + 2\sqrt6 + 3 = 5 + 2\sqrt6,$$
so $\sqrt6 = \frac{q^2-5}{2}$, which is rational by closure. But $6$ is not a perfect square, so $\sqrt6$ is irrational by §4.7 — contradiction.

Two lessons. **Squaring converted a sum of two irrationals into one**, which the stability results can then handle. And **a stalled proof is information**: the point where the argument fails identifies what extra input is needed, which here was the irrationality of $\sqrt6$ rather than any new technique.

## Size, and a non-constructive classic

The irrationals are the *larger* part of the line in every sense: the fractions can be written out in a list and the reals cannot (§3.8), so the irrationals cannot be listed either — and in the sense of length, the fractions take up none of the line while the irrationals take up all of it. A "random" real is irrational with probability $1$, and indeed transcendental with probability $1$.

> **Proposition.** There exist irrational $a, b$ with $a^b \in \mathbb{Q}$.

<Proof>
Consider $t = \sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$. Either $t \in \mathbb{Q}$ or not.

If $t \in \mathbb{Q}$, take $a = b = \sqrt2$; both are irrational (§4.7) and $a^b = t \in \mathbb{Q}$.

If $t \notin \mathbb{Q}$, take $a = t$ and $b = \sqrt2$; both are irrational, and
$$a^b = \left(\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}\right)^{\sqrt2} = \sqrt2^{\,2} = 2 \in \mathbb{Q}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\in$ is "is in".*

In either case such a pair exists.
</Proof>

The proof proves existence without identifying a witness, because it never decides which branch holds. It is the standard illustration of non-constructive reasoning (§1.12), and a constructivist rejects it — the excluded middle applied to an undecided statement is doing all the work.

<Aside>
As it happens $\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$ *is* irrational, indeed transcendental, by the Gelfond–Schneider theorem (1934): $\alpha^\beta$ is transcendental whenever $\alpha$ is algebraic, $\alpha \neq 0,1$, and $\beta$ is algebraic irrational. So the second branch is the true one. But the point of the proof above is exactly that it needs none of that — which is why an elementary argument can reach a conclusion whose direct verification requires a deep theorem.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

The lesson generalizes past the irrationals: **the complement of a well-behaved set is usually not well-behaved**, and expecting closure to survive complementation is a mistake worth naming.

Confusing the two is a real error: $W^\perp$ and $\mathbb{R}^n\setminus W$ are different sets, and only one of them supports the machinery.

**In calculus, the corresponding fact is that the non-differentiable functions are not closed under anything.** The sum of two nowhere-differentiable functions can be differentiable, exactly as $\sqrt2 + (2-\sqrt2)$ is rational. And the "rational plus irrational is irrational" result has a direct analogue: **differentiable plus non-differentiable is non-differentiable**, proved the same way — assume the sum is differentiable, subtract off the differentiable part, and contradict.

That proof is worth doing once. It is four lines, it uses only closure of the differentiable functions under subtraction, and it is the reason $x^2 + |x|$ is not differentiable at $0$ without any computation.

**The pattern across the chapter:** identify what a set is closed under, and let that dictate the proof. Closure gives you constructions; its absence gives you counterexamples, and knowing which you are dealing with before starting saves the wasted attempt.

</WhereThisGoes>
