$\mathbb{Q}$ is a field; $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ is not, and cataloguing exactly how it fails is more informative than the failure itself.

## Failures

$0, 1 \in \mathbb{Q}$, so the irrationals contain neither identity. They are closed under neither operation:
$$
\sqrt2 + (-\sqrt2) = 0, \qquad \sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2 = 2.
$$

The general statement: **the complement of a subfield is never a subfield**, since it omits $0$ and $1$. So the question was answered before it was asked; what remains is which weaker properties survive.

<Warning title="No rule of thumb">
$\sqrt2+\sqrt3 \notin \mathbb{Q}$ but $\sqrt2 + (2-\sqrt2) = 2 \in \mathbb{Q}$. Sums and products of irrationals may be either, and deciding requires work — even $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ needs an argument (square twice, or use the rational root theorem on its minimal polynomial $x^4-10x^2+1$).

Statements like "irrational plus irrational is irrational" are false, and their negations are equally false.
</Warning>

## Stability under rational operations

> **Proposition.** Let $a \in \mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$, $r \in \mathbb{Q}$. Then $r + a$ is irrational, and if $r \neq 0$ so is $ra$. In particular $-a$ and (since $a \neq 0$) $1/a$ are irrational.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$a \notin \mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under the field operations.</div>
  <div slot="want">Negative statements about constructed quantities.</div>
  <div slot="how">Contradiction (§2.5): assume the construction is rational and use closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ to recover $a$. All four cases are the same three lines with a different operation.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose $r + a \in \mathbb{Q}$. Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under subtraction, $(r+a) - r = a \in \mathbb{Q}$ — contradiction.

Suppose $r \neq 0$ and $ra \in \mathbb{Q}$. Then $r^{-1} \in \mathbb{Q}$, and by closure under multiplication $r^{-1}(ra) = a \in \mathbb{Q}$ — contradiction.

Taking $r = -1$ gives $-a$; and $a \neq 0$ since $0 \in \mathbb{Q}$, so applying the argument to $1/a$ (whose inverse is $a$) gives the last claim.
</Proof>

Each proof is the same three steps: assume rational, apply closure to recover $a$, contradict. Irrationality is *non-membership*, so the only available handle is what membership would entail — which is why every theorem in this module is a contradiction proof.

## The structural statement

$(\mathbb{R},+)$ is a group and $(\mathbb{Q},+)$ a subgroup, so $\mathbb{R}$ partitions into **cosets** $a + \mathbb{Q}$. The proposition says exactly:

- $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ is the union of all cosets other than $\mathbb{Q}$ itself;
- translation by a rational preserves each coset, hence preserves irrationality;
- multiplication by a nonzero rational permutes the cosets, hence also preserves irrationality;
- adding two irrationals means adding two non-identity cosets, which may well land in $\mathbb{Q}$ — nothing prevents it.

Viewed this way, none of the results is surprising. The failures and the stabilities are both consequences of $\mathbb{Q}$ being a subgroup and a subfield, not facts about $\sqrt2$.

## Size, and a non-constructive classic

The irrationals are the *larger* part of $\mathbb{R}$ in every sense: $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable and $\mathbb{R}$ is not (§3.8), so $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ is uncountable; and $\mathbb{Q}$ has Lebesgue measure zero, so the irrationals have full measure. A "random" real is irrational with probability $1$, and indeed transcendental with probability $1$.

> **Proposition.** There exist irrational $a, b$ with $a^b \in \mathbb{Q}$.

<Proof>
Consider $t = \sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$. Either $t \in \mathbb{Q}$ or not.

If $t \in \mathbb{Q}$, take $a = b = \sqrt2$; both are irrational (§4.7) and $a^b = t \in \mathbb{Q}$.

If $t \notin \mathbb{Q}$, take $a = t$ and $b = \sqrt2$; both are irrational, and
$$a^b = \left(\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}\right)^{\sqrt2} = \sqrt2^{\,2} = 2 \in \mathbb{Q}.$$

In either case such a pair exists.
</Proof>

The proof establishes existence without identifying a witness, because it never decides which branch holds. It is the standard illustration of non-constructive reasoning (§1.12), and a constructivist rejects it — the excluded middle applied to an undecided statement is doing all the work.

<Aside>
As it happens $\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$ *is* irrational, indeed transcendental, by the Gelfond–Schneider theorem (1934): $\alpha^\beta$ is transcendental whenever $\alpha$ is algebraic, $\alpha \neq 0,1$, and $\beta$ is algebraic irrational. So the second branch is the true one. But the point of the proof above is precisely that it needs none of that — which is why an elementary argument can reach a conclusion whose direct verification requires a deep theorem.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
The coset picture is the first appearance of quotient structures, which organise most of algebra: $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$ is a quotient group, and its elements are exactly the cosets above. That particular quotient is pathological — choosing one representative from each coset requires the Axiom of Choice, and the resulting **Vitali set** is not Lebesgue measurable, which is the standard proof that non-measurable sets exist. In a different direction, deciding whether specific numbers are irrational or transcendental is an active field: Gelfond–Schneider settled Hilbert's seventh problem, Baker's theorem on linear forms in logarithms extended it and won a Fields Medal, and yet whether $e+\pi$ is irrational remains open. The gap between "almost every real is transcendental" and "here is a proof that this one is" has not closed in 180 years.
</WhereThisGoes>
