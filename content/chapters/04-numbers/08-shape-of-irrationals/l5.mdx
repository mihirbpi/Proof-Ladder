Write $\mathbb{I}$ for the irrationals — the reals that are not rational. Module 4.7 showed $\mathbb{I}$ is not empty. This module asks what kind of set it is, and the answer is: a badly behaved one.

## Closure fails, immediately

$$
\sqrt2 + \big(-\sqrt2\big) = 0, \qquad \sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt2 = 2.
$$

Both inputs irrational, both outputs rational. **$\mathbb{I}$ is closed under neither addition nor multiplication.**

Nor does it contain $0$ or $1$, since both are rational. So $\mathbb{I}$ is not a field, not a ring, and not much of anything — it is simply a set, the complement of $\mathbb{Q}$ in $\mathbb{R}$ (module 2.5).

This is a real contrast with $\mathbb{Q}$, and it is worth registering. **Being defined as "the ones that are not" rarely produces a well-behaved object**, and that is a general lesson rather than an accident of this case.

## What is true

Three things do survive, and each is proved the same way.

> **Proposition.** If $a$ is irrational, so is $-a$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">*Irrational* is a negation, so a direct proof has nothing to grip. Module 2.5 again: <strong>suppose not</strong>.</div>
  <div slot="want">A contradiction from assuming $-a$ rational.</div>
  <div slot="how">If $-a$ were rational, then closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ under negation (module 4.5) would make $a$ rational too. That contradicts the hypothesis directly.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $a$ be irrational, and suppose for contradiction that $-a \in \mathbb{Q}$.

Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under additive inverses, $-(-a) \in \mathbb{Q}$. But $-(-a) = a$, so $a \in \mathbb{Q}$ — contradicting that $a$ is irrational.

So $-a$ is irrational.
</Proof>

> **Proposition.** If $a$ is irrational and $a \neq 0$, then $1/a$ is irrational.

<Proof>
Suppose for contradiction that $1/a \in \mathbb{Q}$. Note $1/a \neq 0$.

Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under reciprocals of nonzero elements, $1/(1/a) \in \mathbb{Q}$. But that is $a$, contradicting irrationality of $a$.
</Proof>

**Both proofs have the same three lines.** Assume the conclusion fails; apply a closure property of $\mathbb{Q}$; land on a contradiction with the hypothesis. Once you see the pattern the third result writes itself.

## The main one

> **Theorem.** If $a \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $a \neq 0$, and $b$ is irrational, then $ab$ is irrational.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$a$ is a nonzero rational, $b$ is irrational. Goal is again a negation.</div>
  <div slot="want">A contradiction from $ab \in \mathbb{Q}$.</div>
  <div slot="how">If $ab$ were rational, divide by $a$ — legal because $a \neq 0$ — and closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ under division makes $b$ rational.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose for contradiction that $ab \in \mathbb{Q}$.

Since $a \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $a \neq 0$, its reciprocal $1/a$ is rational (module 4.5). Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under multiplication,
$$
(ab) \cdot \frac1a \in \mathbb{Q}.
$$
But that product is $b$, so $b \in \mathbb{Q}$ — contradicting that $b$ is irrational.

Hence $ab$ is irrational.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Every hypothesis is used">
Drop $a \neq 0$ and the theorem is false: $0 \cdot \sqrt2 = 0$ is rational.

Drop *$a$ rational* and it is false: $\sqrt2 \cdot \sqrt2 = 2$ is rational.

**Both conditions are load-bearing**, and you can see exactly where each entered the proof — $a \neq 0$ licensed the reciprocal, and $a \in \mathbb{Q}$ made that reciprocal rational. A hypothesis you cannot locate in the proof is one to be suspicious of.
</Warning>

This theorem **generalises the first proposition**: taking $a = -1$ gives *if $b$ is irrational so is $-b$*. Having proved the general statement, the special case is free — the same economy as module 4.3.

## What you can and cannot conclude

$$
\text{rational} + \text{irrational} = \text{irrational} \quad\checkmark
$$
$$
\text{irrational} + \text{irrational} = \ ? \quad \text{could be either}
$$

The first is a theorem, proved just as above. The second is genuinely undetermined: $\sqrt2 + \sqrt2 = 2\sqrt2$ is irrational, while $\sqrt2 + (2 - \sqrt2) = 2$ is rational.

**When both inputs are irrational, no conclusion follows.** That is not a gap in our knowledge; it is a fact about the sets, and the two examples prove it.

<Aside>
Here is the strangest thing in the module, and it is worth knowing even though the proof is out of reach for now.

The rationals can be listed in an infinite sequence — first, second, third, and so on, with every rational appearing somewhere. The irrationals cannot: Cantor's argument (module 3.8's Aside) applies to them.

So in the size sense of Chapter 3, **there are far more irrationals than rationals**, even though the rationals are the ones that are dense and familiar and easy to write down.

The number line is almost entirely made of numbers you cannot express as a fraction. The rationals, which feel like all of it, are the exception.
</Aside>
