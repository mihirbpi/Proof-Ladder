A real number is **irrational** if it is not in $\mathbb{Q}$. The irrationals are $\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}$ — a complement, and hence a set defined negatively, which is why every proof about it uses contradiction (module 2.5).

> **Theorem.** $\sqrt2 \notin \mathbb{Q}$.

## Why direct proof is unavailable

The claim is a universal negative: $\forall p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $q \neq 0$, $(p/q)^2 \neq 2$. There is nothing to construct and infinitely many cases to exclude. Module 2.5's diagnostic fires: **suppose not**.

## The lemma

From module 1.9:

> For $n \in \mathbb{Z}$: $n^2$ is even $\iff$ $n$ is even.

We use the ($\Rightarrow$) direction, which was proved there by contrapositive — a direct attempt fails, because "$n^2$ even" gives $n^2 = 2k$ and no handle on $n$.

## The proof

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Every nonzero rational has a representation in lowest terms: $r = p/q$ with $p,q \in \mathbb{Z}$, $q \neq 0$, and $\gcd(p,q) = 1$. This follows from cancelling common factors, which terminates because the numerators strictly decrease.</div>
  <div slot="want">A contradiction from the assumption $\sqrt2 \in \mathbb{Q}$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Assume $\sqrt2 = p/q$ in lowest terms. Squaring gives $p^2 = 2q^2$, so $p$ is even by the lemma. Substituting $p = 2k$ and simplifying gives $q^2 = 2k^2$, so $q$ is even too. Then $2 \mid \gcd(p,q)$, contradicting $\gcd(p,q)=1$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose, for contradiction, that $\sqrt2 \in \mathbb{Q}$. Then $\sqrt2 = \tfrac pq$ for some $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $q \neq 0$ and $\gcd(p,q) = 1$.

Squaring and clearing denominators:
$$p^2 = 2q^2. \tag{3}$$

Then $p^2$ is even, so by the lemma $p$ is even; write $p = 2k$ with $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

Substituting into (3): $4k^2 = 2q^2$, hence
$$q^2 = 2k^2.$$

So $q^2$ is even, and by the lemma again $q$ is even.

Thus $2 \mid p$ and $2 \mid q$, so $2 \mid \gcd(p,q) = 1$, which is false.

The assumption is therefore untenable, and $\sqrt2 \notin \mathbb{Q}$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="The lowest-terms hypothesis is the whole proof">
Without $\gcd(p,q)=1$, the argument still shows $p$ and $q$ are both even — and that is not a contradiction, merely an observation you could repeat forever.

Two ways to make the finish rigorous:

1. **Lowest terms** (used above). The contradiction is immediate.
2. **Infinite descent.** Without assuming lowest terms, you obtain from $(p,q)$ a strictly smaller pair $(p/2, q/2)$ with the same property, and repeat. This yields an infinite strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers, contradicting well-ordering.

Both are legitimate; the second is Fermat's method and generalises further. What is *not* legitimate is stopping at "both even" and calling it a contradiction.
</Warning>

## Consequences

$\mathbb{Q}$ is **incomplete**: the set $\{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x > 0, x^2 < 2\}$ is bounded above but has no least upper bound in $\mathbb{Q}$, because the only candidate is $\sqrt2$. This is precisely the defect that $\mathbb{R}$ is constructed to repair, and it is why calculus needs $\mathbb{R}$.

Geometrically: the diagonal of a unit square has length $\sqrt2$, so a perfectly constructible length is not a ratio of whole numbers. The Pythagoreans, who held that all lengths were such ratios, are said to have found this genuinely disturbing.

<Aside>
The argument generalises. Replacing "even" by "divisible by $p$" shows $\sqrt p$ is irrational for every prime $p$. More generally, $\sqrt m$ is rational iff $m$ is a perfect square, and the rational root theorem extends this to all polynomial roots: any rational root of $x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0$ with integer coefficients must be an integer dividing $a_0$. So $\sqrt[3]{2}$, $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$, and the golden ratio are all irrational by a single mechanism.
</Aside>
