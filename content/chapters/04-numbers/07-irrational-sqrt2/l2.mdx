Take a square with sides of length $1$. Draw the line from corner to corner. That line is called the **diagonal**.

How long is the diagonal?

## Getting close

Let's guess and check.

Is it $1$? Too short — the diagonal is longer than a side.

Is it $2$? Too long.

Is it $1$ and a half? Still a bit too long.

Is it $1.4$? Very close! But a tiny bit short.

Is it $1.41$? Closer. $1.414$? Closer still.

You can keep going all day, getting closer and closer. And you will never arrive.

## The surprise

Here is what mathematicians discovered, and it upset them badly at the time.

> There is **no fraction at all** that gives the exact length of the diagonal.

Not a hard one. Not one with enormous numbers on top and bottom. **None.**

And this is not "nobody has found it yet." Grown-ups have a proof — a real, complete, airtight proof — that no fraction can ever work.

<Warning title="The line is still there">
Do not think the diagonal has no length. It absolutely does. You drew it! You could hold a piece of string against it.

The length is a perfectly good number. It just is not a fraction. Fractions turn out not to be all the numbers there are.
</Warning>

## Why this was a big deal

The people who found this out lived over two thousand years ago, and they had believed that every length could be written as one whole number compared to another. Every single one.

Then someone drew a square and ruined it.

That is one of the best things about mathematics. A drawing anyone can make, and an answer nobody expected.

<Aside>
The proof works by imagining you *had* found the fraction, written as simply as possible — and then showing that the top and bottom would both have to be even, which means it was not written as simply as possible after all. You will see the whole argument in a few years, and it takes about six lines.
</Aside>

<TryIt>
Draw a square with sides of 10 cm. Measure the diagonal with a ruler. You will get about 14.1 cm. Now try to write that as a fraction that works exactly. You cannot!
</TryIt>
