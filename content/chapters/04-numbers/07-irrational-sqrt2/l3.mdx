Module 4.6 showed the fractions are dense — between any two there are infinitely many more. It is tempting to conclude they fill the number line completely.

They do not. This module exhibits a hole.

A number on the number line that is **not** a fraction is called **irrational**.

## The claim

> **Claim.** There is no fraction that, multiplied by itself, gives $2$.

The length that does this is the diagonal of a square with sides $1$ — you can draw it in five seconds. It is a perfectly good length. It is just not a fraction.

## The plan

Direct proof is hopeless here. To show *no* fraction works, you would have to check infinitely many, and you cannot.

So use **suppose not** (module 2.5). Assume a fraction *does* work, and follow it until something impossible happens.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Every fraction can be written in **lowest terms** — with top and bottom sharing no common factor. If they shared one, you could cancel it out.</div>
  <div slot="want">To show no fraction multiplied by itself gives $2$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Suppose one does, written in lowest terms. Then show that the top *and* the bottom must both be even — meaning they shared a factor of $2$ after all. That contradicts "lowest terms."</div>
</Discussion>

We need one tool from module 1.9:

> If a number multiplied by itself is even, then the number itself is even.

(You proved it there by flipping it around: if a number is odd, then multiplied by itself it is odd.)

## The proof

<Proof>
Suppose not. Suppose there is a fraction which, multiplied by itself, gives $2$. Write it in **lowest terms**, so its top and bottom share no common factor.

Multiplying the fraction by itself means multiplying top by top and bottom by bottom. For the answer to be $2$, the new top must be twice the new bottom.

So: **the top multiplied by itself is twice the bottom multiplied by itself.**

That makes the top-times-top an even number. By our tool, **the top is even.** So the top is twice something.

Now put that back in. The top-times-top is twice-something multiplied by twice-something, which is four times (something multiplied by itself).

And we said top-times-top is twice bottom-times-bottom. So four times (something times itself) equals twice (bottom times bottom).

Halve both sides: twice (something times itself) equals bottom-times-bottom.

That makes bottom-times-bottom even. By our tool again, **the bottom is even.**

But now the top is even and the bottom is even — so they share the common factor $2$.

And we said the fraction was in lowest terms, with no shared factors. Contradiction!

Our supposition must have been wrong. No fraction multiplied by itself gives $2$.
</Proof>

## What the proof used

Count the tools. This short argument leans on nearly the whole course so far.

- **Suppose not** (module 2.5) — the shape of the whole thing.
- **Flip it around** (module 1.8) — hidden inside the even-number tool.
- **Even means a pile of $2$s** (module 4.2) — used twice.
- **Lowest terms** — the load-bearing assumption. Without it, no contradiction.

<Warning title="Lowest terms is doing all the work">
Drop the lowest-terms assumption and nothing goes wrong! You would simply learn that top and bottom are both even, cancel a $2$, and repeat forever. That is not a contradiction — it is just an endless process.

The contradiction only appears because we *said in advance* that no cancelling was possible. That single sentence is what makes the proof work.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The same argument works for the length whose square is $3$, and for $5$, and for the square root of any prime — you replace "even" with "divisible by that prime."

It also settles exactly which square roots *are* fractions: only the square roots of perfect squares. So the square root of $16$ is $4$, and the square root of $25$ is $5$, and the square root of every other counting number is irrational.
</Aside>
