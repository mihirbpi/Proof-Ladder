Between $4$ and $5$ there is no integer at all. The integers have gaps.

Do the rationals have gaps? Pick two of them, as close together as you like, and ask whether anything fits between.

The answer is no — and it is worth proving rather than assuming.

## The claim

> **Claim.** If $a$ and $b$ are rational numbers with $a < b$, then $\dfrac{a+b}{2}$ is a rational number and
> $$a < \frac{a+b}{2} < b.$$

The number $\tfrac{a+b}{2}$ is called the **midpoint** of $a$ and $b$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$a$ and $b$ are rational, and $a < b$. From module 4.5, $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under adding and dividing by a non-zero number.</div>
  <div slot="want">Three things: the midpoint is rational, it beats $a$, and it loses to $b$. Three separate checks.</div>
  <div slot="how">Rationality is pure closure. For the two inequalities, start from $a < b$ and add the right thing to both sides.</div>
</Discussion>

## The proof

<Proof>
**The midpoint is rational.** $a + b$ is rational, because $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under addition. Dividing by $2$ is multiplying by $\tfrac12$, which is rational, and $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under multiplication. So $\tfrac{a+b}{2} \in \mathbb{Q}$.

**It is bigger than $a$.** Start from $a < b$. Add $a$ to both sides:
$$a + a < a + b, \quad\text{so}\quad 2a < a + b.$$
Now divide both sides by $2$, which is positive so the inequality is preserved:
$$a < \frac{a+b}{2}.$$

**It is smaller than $b$.** Start from $a < b$ again, but this time add $b$ to both sides:
$$a + b < b + b = 2b.$$
Divide by $2$:
$$\frac{a+b}{2} < b.$$

All three checks pass, so the claim holds.
</Proof>

Notice the symmetry of the two inequality arguments: one adds $a$, the other adds $b$. Neither is harder; you just have to think of doing both.

<Warning title="Rationality is not free">
The first paragraph of the proof is doing real work. If we were working inside the *integers*, the midpoint of $3$ and $4$ would be $3\tfrac12$, which is not an integer — and the whole claim would collapse.

Closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ (module 4.5) is exactly what makes the midpoint legal. Cite it.
</Warning>

## Infinitely many, in every gap

Now repeat. Between $a$ and $b$ sits $m_1 = \tfrac{a+b}{2}$. Between $a$ and $m_1$ sits another rational $m_2$. Between $a$ and $m_2$ sits $m_3$.

There is nothing to stop this. Every step produces a new rational strictly between $a$ and $b$, and the process never terminates.

So: **between any two distinct rationals there are infinitely many rationals.** A set with this property is called **dense**.

## The trap

Dense sounds like *complete*. It is very tempting to conclude that the rationals fill the number line with nothing missing — after all, they leave no gap you can point to.

Hold that thought. Module 4.7 shows it is false, and the demonstration is one of the most famous proofs in mathematics.

<Aside>
Here is how strange the situation is. The rationals are dense — every interval, however tiny, contains infinitely many. And yet there are *vastly* more numbers on the line that are not rational than are. Both facts are true at once, and reconciling them is genuinely difficult. Density and abundance are different things.
</Aside>
