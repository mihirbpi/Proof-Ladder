The integers on a number line are spaced out: nothing sits between $3$ and $4$. The rationals are not like that at all, and the difference has consequences for what a number line can be.

## The theorem

> **Theorem.** For any rationals $a < b$, there exists a rational $c$ with $a < c< b$.

A set with this property is **dense**.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$a, b\in\mathbb{Q}$ with $a<b$. Available: closure (module 4.5) and the order rules.</div>
  <div slot="want">A rational strictly between them — an existential claim, so produce a witness.</div>
  <div slot="how">The midpoint $\frac{a+b}{2}$ is the obvious candidate. Three things then need checking: that it is rational, that it exceeds $a$, and that it falls short of $b$. All three are obligations, and the two inequalities are separate proofs.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $c = \dfrac{a+b}{2}$.

**$c$ is rational.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under addition, so $a+b\in\mathbb{Q}$; and closed under division by non-zero rationals, so $\frac{a+b}{2}\in\mathbb{Q}$.

**$a < c$.** From $a<b$, add $a$ to both sides: $2a < a+b$. Divide by $2$, which is positive and so preserves the inequality: $a < \frac{a+b}{2} = c$.

**$c < b$.** From $a<b$, add $b$ to both sides: $a+b<2b$. Divide by $2$: $c = \frac{a+b}{2} < b$.

So $a<c<b$, and $c\in\mathbb{Q}$.
</Proof>

**Three obligations, three paragraphs.** The two inequalities came from adding *different* things to $a<b$ — $a$ for one, $b$ for the other — and neither follows from the other. A proof that proves only one has proved half the theorem.

This is the same discipline as module 2.6's double inclusion: when the goal is an *and*, count its halves and prove each.

<Warning title="Dividing by 2 was a step, not a formality">
The move from $2a<a+b$ to $a<\frac{a+b}{2}$ used the order rule *"multiplying by a positive preserves the inequality."*

Had the divisor been negative, the inequality would flip. This is the rule that catches people out in algebra, and stating it once here is what makes the proof honest.
</Warning>

## The witness, computed

The proof produces a recipe, so try it on a hard case. Take two rationals that are already close:
$$a = \frac{7}{5} = 1.4, \qquad b = \frac{71}{50} = 1.42.$$
The midpoint is
$$c = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{7}{5}+\frac{71}{50}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{141}{50} = \frac{141}{100},$$
which is $1.41$, comfortably between them and plainly a ratio of integers.

Nothing about the closeness of $a$ and $b$ caused trouble, and nothing could. The proof never referred to how far apart they were, so it works for a gap of $1$ and for a gap of one trillionth alike. **A proof that ignores a quantity is a proof that holds for every value of it** — which is the whole reason to argue in general rather than by example.

## Repeat forever

The theorem applies to its own output. Between $a$ and $c$ there is another rational; between those, another.

**Between any two distinct rationals there are infinitely many rationals.** No two rationals are adjacent, and the phrase "the next rational after $\frac12$" refers to nothing.

Contrast the integers, where $4$ is unambiguously next after $3$. Density is a genuine structural difference, and it is why you can locate a rational to any accuracy you like by repeated bisection — the same procedure as bisecting a segment with compass and straightedge, run over and over.

The rate is worth knowing. Each bisection halves the interval you are working in, so after $n$ steps the interval has length $(b-a)/2^n$. Ten steps shrink it by a factor of more than a thousand, twenty by more than a million. That is why bisection is a practical method for locating a number and not merely a theoretical one.

## The geometric version

Your course has this theorem too, in different words.

> *Every segment has a midpoint.* (Module 1.10 proved it, with uniqueness.)

Bisect $\overline{AB}$ to get $M_1$. Bisect $\overline{AM_1}$ to get $M_2$. Continue. The construction never terminates and never repeats, which is the geometric face of density.

And the Ruler Postulate is what connects the two statements: it puts the points of a line in correspondence with $\mathbb{R}$, so betweenness of points becomes betweenness of numbers, and the midpoint of a segment becomes the average of two coordinates.

## Being crowded is not the same as having no gaps

Here is the module's real point, and it is the one students find hardest.

$\mathbb{Q}$ is dense: between any two rationals, another rational. **And $\mathbb{Q}$ still has gaps.**

Those sound contradictory and are not. Density says no two rationals are adjacent. It does not say the rationals *fill* the line — and they do not, because $\sqrt2$ is a location on the line with no rational sitting at it (module 4.7).

A useful image: mark every rational on a line and you have marked a set with no two points adjacent, packed everywhere you look, with infinitely many holes remaining. The holes are individually invisible — you cannot see one, because rationals crowd arbitrarily close on both sides — and there are, in the sense of module 3.8, more holes than there are rationals.

<Warning title="Between and next">
Density kills the word "next."

For integers: $n+1$ is the next one. For rationals: nothing is next, and the question is malformed.

This matters in Chapter 6. Induction works by stepping from $n$ to $n+1$, and it therefore works over $\mathbb{Z}$ and fails over $\mathbb{Q}$ — there is no step to take. The technique is tied to a structural property that the rationals lack.
</Warning>

## Any point between, not just the midpoint

The midpoint is the obvious witness and it is not the only one. For any rational $t$ with $0<t<1$, the number
$$c = a + t(b-a)$$
is rational by closure and lies strictly between $a$ and $b$.

$t = \frac12$ recovers the midpoint. $t = \frac13$ gives the point one-third of the way along. This is the **section formula** from your coordinate work, and it says the same thing there: the point dividing $\overline{AB}$ in a given ratio has coordinates you can compute, and it lies between the endpoints.

So the theorem is stronger than stated: between any two rationals lie *infinitely many*, one for each choice of $t$, and no single argument was needed for each — the general witness covers them all.

**Producing a family of witnesses rather than one is often no harder**, and it is worth doing when the extra generality is free. Module 4.3's linear-combination result had the same character.

## Irrationals are dense too

Between any two rationals there is also an **irrational**. Given $a<b$, the number
$$c = a + \frac{b-a}{\sqrt2}$$
lies strictly between them and is irrational — the proof uses module 4.8's tools.

So both sets are dense, and both are riddled with the other's members. Any interval of the number line, however short, contains infinitely many rationals and infinitely many irrationals, thoroughly interleaved.

<Aside>
Density has a limit that is easy to miss: it says nothing about *how many*.

Both $\mathbb{Q}$ and the irrationals are dense in $\mathbb{R}$, and module 3.8's counting shows they are not the same size. $\mathbb{Q}$ can be listed in a sequence; the irrationals cannot.

There is a sharper version, which a later course makes precise: you can cover every fraction on the line with tiny intervals whose total length is as small as you please. Crowded everywhere, and taking up no room at all.

Dense, infinite, and of zero length. Density is a statement about position, not about quantity, and keeping those apart is worth doing now.
</Aside>
