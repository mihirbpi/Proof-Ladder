> **Proposition.** For $a,b \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $a<b$: $\tfrac{a+b}{2} \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $a < \tfrac{a+b}{2} < b$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ under the field operations (§4.5); the arithmetic and ordering rules: $a<b \Rightarrow a+c<b+c$, and $a<b$, $c>0 \Rightarrow ac<bc$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Membership and two strict inequalities.</div>
  <div slot="how">Membership is closure and must be asserted — the proposition is false in $\mathbb{Z}$ for exactly that reason. Each inequality follows from $a<b$ by adding one endpoint and scaling by $\tfrac12 > 0$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
$a+b \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $\tfrac12 \in \mathbb{Q}$, so $\tfrac{a+b}{2} \in \mathbb{Q}$ by closure.

Adding $a$ to $a<b$ gives $2a < a+b$; scaling by $\tfrac12>0$ gives $a < \tfrac{a+b}{2}$.

Adding $b$ to $a<b$ gives $a+b < 2b$; scaling by $\tfrac12>0$ gives $\tfrac{a+b}{2}<b$.
</Proof>

Note that the argument uses nothing specific to $\mathbb{Q}$ beyond its being a system whose ordering fits its arithmetic in which $2$ is invertible. So **every ordered field is dense in itself** — and $\mathbb{Z}$, not being a field, is the relevant counterexample.

<Warning title="Where the hypothesis is spent">
In $\mathbb{Z}$: the midpoint of $3$ and $4$ is $\tfrac72 \notin \mathbb{Z}$, and nothing lies strictly between consecutive integers. The invertibility of $2$ is precisely the hypothesis $\mathbb{Z}$ fails.

Note also that the argument fails in a system whose ordering fits its arithmetic of characteristic $2$ — except that there are none, since $1 > 0$ forces $1 + 1 > 0$. Ordered fields always have characteristic $0$, which is why the caveat never bites.
</Warning>

## Density

> **Corollary.** $(a,b)\cap\mathbb{Q}$ is infinite for any rationals $a<b$.

Iterating the midpoint construction toward $a$ yields a strictly decreasing sequence in $(a,b)$, hence infinitely many distinct elements.

A stronger and more useful statement:

> **Theorem.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$: for all $x < y$ in $\mathbb{R}$ there is $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $x < q < y$.

<Proof>
By the Archimedean property choose $n \in \mathbb{N}$ with $n(y-x) > 1$, i.e. $\tfrac1n < y - x$.

Let $m = \lfloor nx \rfloor + 1$, so $m - 1 \leq nx < m$, giving $nx < m \leq nx + 1$.

Then $q = \tfrac mn \in \mathbb{Q}$ satisfies $q > x$ from the left inequality, and
$$q \leq x + \tfrac1n < x + (y-x) = y.$$
Hence $x < q < y$.
</Proof>

Two ingredients deserve names. The **Archimedean property** — for every real $r$ there is $n \in \mathbb{N}$ with $n > r$ — is what guarantees $\tfrac1n$ can be made smaller than any positive gap; it fails in non-Archimedea system whose ordering fits its arithmetics such as the field of rational functions ordered by growth, where infinitesimals exist and the theorem is false. And the floor function (§3.2) is what converts the Archimedean estimate into an actual rational.

## Any interior point, not only the midpoint

The midpoint is the obvious witness (§1.12) and far from the only one. For rational $t$ with $0<t<1$,
$$c = a + t(b-a) = (1-t)a + tb$$
is rational by closure and lies strictly between $a$ and $b$. Taking $t = \tfrac12$ recovers the midpoint.

Producing the general witness costs no more than the specific one, and it yields a stronger theorem: **between any two distinct rationals lie infinitely many rationals**, one for each admissible $t$.

The expression $(1-t)a + tb$ is worth recognising. It is the **convex combination** of $a$ and $b$, and it parametrises the segment joining them as $t$ runs over $[0,1]$. The same formula gives the section formula in coordinate geometry, the parametric equation of a line segment in $\mathbb{R}^n$, and the linear interpolation used in every numerical method — one expression, appearing under three names.

That the interior of a segment is exactly the set of such combinations with $0<t<1$ is a statement you will prove again when you meet convex sets, where convexity becomes a definition rather than an observation.

## The irrationals are dense too

> **Proposition.** Between any two rationals $a < b$ there is an irrational.

Take $c = a + \dfrac{b-a}{\sqrt2}$. Since $0 < \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2} < 1$, this lies strictly between $a$ and $b$. And it is irrational: if $c$ were rational then $\tfrac{b-a}{\sqrt2} = c - a$ would be rational, so $\sqrt2 = \tfrac{b-a}{c-a}$ would be a quotient of rationals — contradicting §4.7.

So both sets are dense, and each is riddled with members of the other. **Every interval of the number line, however short, contains infinitely many rationals and infinitely many irrationals**, thoroughly interleaved, with no interval belonging entirely to either.

That interleaving has consequences worth anticipating. The function equal to $1$ on the rationals and $0$ on the irrationals is discontinuous at every point, because every neighbourhood of every point contains both kinds. It is the standard first example of a function that is nowhere continuous, and density is the entire reason it works.

## Density is not completeness

$\mathbb{Q}$ leaves no gap that can be exhibited by naming two rationals with nothing between them. Nevertheless $\mathbb{Q}$ has gaps in the order-theoretic sense: the set
$$
S = \{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x > 0,\ x^2 < 2\}
$$
is nonempty and bounded above in $\mathbb{Q}$, yet has **no least upper bound in $\mathbb{Q}$**. Given any rational upper bound one can produce a smaller one, and given any rational in $S$ one can produce a larger one — the candidate supremum is $\sqrt2$, which §4.7 proves is not rational.

The **completeness axiom** — every nonempty bounded-above set has a least upper bound — is exactly what $\mathbb{R}$ adds, and $\mathbb{R}$ is the unique complete ordered field up to a renaming that preserves all the structure. Density is a local property; completeness is not, which is why local inspection cannot detect the failure.

<Aside>
Density and measure are independent. $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$ yet countable, so it has Lebesgue measure zero: enumerate $\mathbb{Q}$ as $q_1, q_2, \ldots$ and cover $q_k$ by an interval of length $\varepsilon/2^k$; the total length is $\varepsilon$, arbitrarily small. So $\mathbb{Q}$ is everywhere and occupies no length. Conversely the Cantor set is nowhere dense and uncountable, and the fat Cantor sets are nowhere dense with positive measure. Size measured by density and size measured by length are genuinely different notions, and this is the standard first example.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Density is the property that makes approximation possible, and calculus is a subject about approximation.

**Every limit is an approximation argument.** The definition asks the output to come within whatever tolerance you name, provided the input is close enough — and the input window can be taken as small as you like precisely because $\mathbb{R}$ has no smallest positive number. Density is what guarantees the shrinking never terminates, and a number system where it did would have no limits worth the name.

**The same property underwrites Riemann sums.** Partitioning $[a,b]$ into $n$ subintervals and letting $n$ grow relies on being able to subdivide indefinitely — which is this module's *repeat forever*, applied to intervals rather than pairs of rationals. Newton's method and bisection — two ways of hunting a solution by repeatedly narrowing the search — depend on the same structure.

**And density is exactly what completeness is not.** This module's closing warning is the one that matters most for calculus: $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense and full of holes. Every theorem you will meet whose proof invokes the "least upper bound property" is invoking completeness, not density, and the distinction is why those theorems are false over $\mathbb{Q}$ even though $\mathbb{Q}$ looks like it fills the line.

</WhereThisGoes>
