> **Proposition.** For $a,b \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $a<b$: $\tfrac{a+b}{2} \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $a < \tfrac{a+b}{2} < b$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ under the field operations (§4.5); the ordered-field axioms: $a<b \Rightarrow a+c<b+c$, and $a<b$, $c>0 \Rightarrow ac<bc$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Membership and two strict inequalities.</div>
  <div slot="how">Membership is closure and must be asserted — the proposition is false in $\mathbb{Z}$ for exactly that reason. Each inequality follows from $a<b$ by adding one endpoint and scaling by $\tfrac12 > 0$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
$a+b \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $\tfrac12 \in \mathbb{Q}$, so $\tfrac{a+b}{2} \in \mathbb{Q}$ by closure.

Adding $a$ to $a<b$ gives $2a < a+b$; scaling by $\tfrac12>0$ gives $a < \tfrac{a+b}{2}$.

Adding $b$ to $a<b$ gives $a+b < 2b$; scaling by $\tfrac12>0$ gives $\tfrac{a+b}{2}<b$.
</Proof>

Note that the argument uses nothing specific to $\mathbb{Q}$ beyond its being an ordered field in which $2$ is invertible. So **every ordered field is dense in itself** — and $\mathbb{Z}$, not being a field, is the relevant counterexample.

<Warning title="Where the hypothesis is spent">
In $\mathbb{Z}$: the midpoint of $3$ and $4$ is $\tfrac72 \notin \mathbb{Z}$, and nothing lies strictly between consecutive integers. The invertibility of $2$ is precisely the hypothesis $\mathbb{Z}$ fails.

Note also that the argument fails in an ordered field of characteristic $2$ — except that there are none, since $1 > 0$ forces $1 + 1 > 0$. Ordered fields always have characteristic $0$, which is why the caveat never bites.
</Warning>

## Density

> **Corollary.** $(a,b)\cap\mathbb{Q}$ is infinite for any rationals $a<b$.

Iterating the midpoint construction toward $a$ yields a strictly decreasing sequence in $(a,b)$, hence infinitely many distinct elements.

A stronger and more useful statement:

> **Theorem.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$: for all $x < y$ in $\mathbb{R}$ there is $q \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $x < q < y$.

<Proof>
By the Archimedean property choose $n \in \mathbb{N}$ with $n(y-x) > 1$, i.e. $\tfrac1n < y - x$.

Let $m = \lfloor nx \rfloor + 1$, so $m - 1 \leq nx < m$, giving $nx < m \leq nx + 1$.

Then $q = \tfrac mn \in \mathbb{Q}$ satisfies $q > x$ from the left inequality, and
$$q \leq x + \tfrac1n < x + (y-x) = y.$$
Hence $x < q < y$.
</Proof>

Two ingredients deserve names. The **Archimedean property** — for every real $r$ there is $n \in \mathbb{N}$ with $n > r$ — is what guarantees $\tfrac1n$ can be made smaller than any positive gap; it fails in non-Archimedean ordered fields such as the field of rational functions ordered by growth, where infinitesimals exist and the theorem is false. And the floor function (§3.2) is what converts the Archimedean estimate into an actual rational.

## Density is not completeness

$\mathbb{Q}$ leaves no gap that can be exhibited by naming two rationals with nothing between them. Nevertheless $\mathbb{Q}$ has gaps in the order-theoretic sense: the set
$$
S = \{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x > 0,\ x^2 < 2\}
$$
is nonempty and bounded above in $\mathbb{Q}$, yet has **no least upper bound in $\mathbb{Q}$**. Given any rational upper bound one can produce a smaller one, and given any rational in $S$ one can produce a larger one — the candidate supremum is $\sqrt2$, which §4.7 proves is not rational.

The **completeness axiom** — every nonempty bounded-above set has a least upper bound — is exactly what $\mathbb{R}$ adds, and $\mathbb{R}$ is the unique complete ordered field up to isomorphism. Density is a local property; completeness is not, which is why local inspection cannot detect the failure.

<Aside>
Density and measure are independent. $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$ yet countable, so it has Lebesgue measure zero: enumerate $\mathbb{Q}$ as $q_1, q_2, \ldots$ and cover $q_k$ by an interval of length $\varepsilon/2^k$; the total length is $\varepsilon$, arbitrarily small. So $\mathbb{Q}$ is everywhere and occupies no length. Conversely the Cantor set is nowhere dense and uncountable, and the fat Cantor sets are nowhere dense with positive measure. Topological size and measure-theoretic size are genuinely different notions, and this is the standard first example.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
Density is the property that makes approximation arguments work, and it is ubiquitous. Because $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$, a continuous function is determined by its values on $\mathbb{Q}$ — which is why continuity plus a countable amount of data pins down an uncountable object. The Weierstrass approximation theorem says polynomials are dense in $C([a,b])$, so every continuous function is a uniform limit of polynomials; the Stone–Weierstrass theorem generalises this to arbitrary compact spaces. In functional analysis, separability — the existence of a countable dense subset — is the hypothesis that makes Hilbert spaces tractable and Fourier series converge. The construction of $\mathbb{R}$ itself is a density argument run backwards: Cauchy sequences of rationals, or Dedekind cuts, fill exactly the gaps that density could not detect.
</WhereThisGoes>
