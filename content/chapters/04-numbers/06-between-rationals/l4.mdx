> **Proposition.** Take any two fractions $a < b$. Then their midpoint is also a fraction, and
> $$a < \frac{a+b}{2} < b.$$

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$a$ and $b$ are fractions with $a < b$. Available: fractions stay fractions under adding, multiplying and dividing by anything non-zero (module 4.5); and the arithmetic and ordering rules that adding a constant preserves $<$ and multiplying by a positive preserves $<$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Three claims: membership in $\mathbb{Q}$, and two strict inequalities.</div>
  <div slot="how">Membership is pure closure and must be stated, not assumed. The inequalities come from $a<b$ by adding $a$ to both sides in one case and $b$ in the other, then halving.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**Rationality.** $a + b \in \mathbb{Q}$ by closure under addition. Since $2 \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $2 \neq 0$, its inverse $\tfrac12 \in \mathbb{Q}$, and $\tfrac{a+b}{2} = (a+b)\cdot\tfrac12 \in \mathbb{Q}$ by closure under multiplication.

**Lower bound.** From $a < b$, adding $a$ to both sides gives $2a < a+b$. Multiplying by $\tfrac12 > 0$ preserves the inequality, so $a < \tfrac{a+b}{2}$.

**Upper bound.** From $a < b$, adding $b$ gives $a + b < 2b$. Multiplying by $\tfrac12 > 0$ gives $\tfrac{a+b}{2} < b$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Closure is the load-bearing step">
The proposition is false for the integers: the midpoint of $3$ and $4$ is $\tfrac72$, which is not an integer, and indeed nothing lies strictly between consecutive integers.

So the argument cannot merely produce the midpoint; it has to certify that the midpoint is still in the system under discussion. Closure (module 4.5) is what supplies that, and it is what $\mathbb{Z}$ lacks.
</Warning>

## Density

> **Corollary.** Between any two distinct rationals lie infinitely many rationals.

<Proof>
Let $a < b$ in $\mathbb{Q}$. Define $m_1 = \tfrac{a+b}{2}$, and recursively $m_{k+1} = \tfrac{a + m_k}{2}$.

By the proposition, each $m_k \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $a < m_{k+1} < m_k$. So $m_1 > m_2 > m_3 > \cdots$ is a strictly decreasing sequence of rationals, all lying strictly between $a$ and $b$.

A strictly decreasing sequence has pairwise distinct terms, so this exhibits infinitely many rationals in $(a,b)$.
</Proof>

A collection with this property is called **dense**. The fractions are dense among all the numbers on the line too: every stretch of the line, however short, contains one — a fact used constantly later, and provable from the whole numbers climbing past any real.

Contrast the integers, which are **discrete**: neighboring integers have nothing between them, and every integer has a nearest one on each side. That difference is why the integers supports the division algorithm and induction (§6.1: prove the first case, then that each case forces the next) while $\mathbb{Q}$ does not, and why $\mathbb{Q}$ supports limits and $\mathbb{Z}$ does not.

## Running the midpoint trick on purpose

The proof gives you a machine, and it is worth turning the crank a few times to see what it produces.

Start with $0$ and $1$. Their midpoint is $\tfrac12$. Now take $0$ and $\tfrac12$: midpoint $\tfrac14$. Again with $0$ and $\tfrac14$: midpoint $\tfrac18$.

$$0, \quad \tfrac12, \quad \tfrac14, \quad \tfrac18, \quad \tfrac1{16}, \quad \ldots$$

Every one of these is a fraction, every one is strictly bigger than $0$, and they get as close to $0$ as you care to ask. **There is no first fraction after zero**, and the machine above is a recipe for defeating anyone who claims to have found one: whatever they name, take the midpoint of it and $0$.

That is worth pausing on, because the counting numbers behave in exactly the opposite way. After $7$ comes $8$, with nothing in between, and no amount of cleverness produces a whole number between them.

## Two questions that sound the same

**"Is there a fraction between these two?"** Always yes, by the proposition — provided the two are different.

**"Is there a fraction next to this one?"** Never. The idea of *next* does not survive the trip from the integers to the fractions, and asking for it is asking for something the system does not contain.

Losing the notion of *next* is the price paid for density, and it costs more than it first appears: induction (Chapter 6) runs on stepping from each number to the next one, which is exactly why induction works over the integers and cannot be run over the fractions.

## The trap this sets

Density makes the fractions *feel* as though they fill the number line. No gap can be exhibited by pointing: name any two rationals and infinitely many more sit between them.

The feeling is wrong. There are gaps — points on the line no rational occupies — and they cannot be found by this kind of local inspection, because locally the rationals look complete. Module 4.7 exhibits one, using a proof that does not look at the line at all.

<Aside>
The reconciliation is that being densely packed and being numerous are unrelated. The fractions are dense, and yet they can be listed one after another (module 3.8) while the full number line cannot — so in a precise sense almost every real is irrational. It is even possible to cover all of $\mathbb{Q}$ by intervals of total length as small as you like, which is a way of saying $\mathbb{Q}$ occupies no length at all despite being everywhere. Both facts are true; only intuition objects.
</Aside>
