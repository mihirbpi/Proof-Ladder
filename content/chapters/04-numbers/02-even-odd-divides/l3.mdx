You have known about even and odd since you were small. This module gives you the versions you can *compute with* — and that difference is bigger than it sounds.

## The algebraic definitions

> A whole number $n$ is **even** when $n = 2k$ for some whole number $k$.
>
> A whole number $n$ is **odd** when $n = 2k + 1$ for some whole number $k$.

Compare with "even means it pairs up nicely." Both are correct. But if a proof hands you "$n$ pairs up nicely," you can do nothing with it. If it hands you "$n = 2k$," you can substitute, multiply, and rearrange. That is why this version wins.

Examples: $12$ is even because $12 = 2 \times 6$. $13$ is odd because $13 = 2 \times 6 + 1$.

<Warning title="k must be a whole number">
Is $9$ even? Certainly $9 = 2 \times 4\tfrac12$. But $4\tfrac12$ is not a whole number, so this does not qualify. The requirement that $k$ be a whole number is carrying the entire definition — drop it and every number becomes even.
</Warning>

## Divides

Pairing was about $2$. Nothing is special about $2$, so here is the general version.

> $m$ **divides** $n$, written $m \mid n$, when $n = m \times k$ for some whole number $k$.

So "even" is just "$2 \mid n$." And $3 \mid 12$, because $12 = 3 \times 4$. But $3 \nmid 13$, because no whole number times $3$ gives $13$.

<Warning title="Which way round?">
$3 \mid 12$ says **3 goes into 12**. The small one comes first.

It does *not* mean $3 \div 12$, and it is not a fraction. The vertical bar here is a whole word — "divides" — not a division sign.

A memory aid: the symbol looks like the divisor standing up straight, ready to march into the bigger number.
</Warning>

## Divisibility is a hunt for a number

Here is the most useful thing in this module.

Look again at the definition: $m \mid n$ means *there exists* a whole number $k$ with $n = mk$. That is a **there exists** claim — module 1.10.

And module 1.12 told you exactly how to prove one of those: **exhibit a witness.** Produce the $k$.

So every divisibility proof has the same ending: *here is the number, and here is why it is a whole number.*

## A worked example

> **Claim.** If $6 \mid n$, then $2 \mid n$.

<Proof>
Suppose $6 \mid n$. By the definition, there is a whole number $k$ with $n = 6k$.

Now $6 = 2 \times 3$, so
$$n = 2 \times 3 \times k = 2 \times (3k).$$

To show $2 \mid n$ we must produce a whole number that $2$ multiplies to give $n$. Our witness is $3k$.

And $3k$ is a whole number, because multiplying two whole numbers gives a whole number (module 4.1).

So $n = 2 \times (3k)$ with $3k$ a whole number, which means $2 \mid n$.
</Proof>

Read the last two paragraphs again. The proof did not stop at $n = 2 \times (3k)$; it explicitly said *and $3k$ is a whole number, by closure.* That step is easy to skip and it is not optional — the whole definition depends on it.

<Aside>
Almost everything in the rest of this chapter is this one move repeated: unpack what you are given into an equation, do algebra, and pull out the divisor you want at the end. Module 4.3 turns it into a template you can follow every time.
</Aside>
