You have known about even and odd since you were small, and you know how to find factors and multiples. This module gives you the versions you can *compute with* — and that difference is bigger than it sounds.

## Even and odd, said usefully

> A counting number is **even** when it is some number of $2$s with nothing left over.
>
> A counting number is **odd** when it is some number of $2$s with exactly one left over.

Compare with "even means it pairs up nicely." Both are correct. But if a proof hands you "this number pairs up nicely", you can do nothing with it. If it hands you "this number is a pile of $2$s", you can count the $2$s, split them, group them, and rearrange them.

That is why this version wins: it gives you something to hold.

$12$ is even, because $12$ is six $2$s. $13$ is odd, because $13$ is six $2$s with one left over.

<Warning title="The pile has to be a whole number of 2s">
Is $9$ even? You might say $9$ is "four and a half $2$s". But half a $2$ is not a $2$, and the definition asks for a **whole** number of them.

That word *whole* is carrying the entire definition. Drop it and every number becomes even.
</Warning>

## Goes into

Pairing was about $2$. Nothing is special about $2$, so here is the general version.

> One number **goes into** another when the second is some whole number of copies of the first, with nothing left over.

So "even" is just "$2$ goes into it."

$3$ goes into $12$, because $12$ is four copies of $3$. ✓
$3$ does not go into $13$, because four copies of $3$ leaves one over and five copies overshoots. ✗

You already have two words for this. If $3$ goes into $12$, then $3$ is a **factor** of $12$, and $12$ is a **multiple** of $3$. Three phrases, one idea, and it is worth being able to switch between them freely.

<Warning title="Which way round?">
"$3$ goes into $12$" is true. "$12$ goes into $3$" is false.

The small one goes into the big one, and saying it backwards changes the claim completely. When you read a divisibility statement, check the direction before anything else.
</Warning>

## Going into is a hunt for a number

Here is the most useful thing in this module.

Look again at the definition: *the second number is some whole number of copies of the first.* That is a **there exists** claim — module 1.10. It says: there is a number of copies, and here it is.

And module 1.12 told you exactly how to prove one of those: **produce the number.**

So every "goes into" proof has the same ending: *here is how many copies, and here is why that count is a whole number.*

## A worked example

> **Claim.** If $6$ goes into a number, then $2$ goes into it too.

<Proof>
Take any number that $6$ goes into, and follow it. Being a multiple of $6$, it is some whole number of copies of $6$.

Now look at a single copy of $6$. It is three $2$s.

So replace every copy of $6$ by three $2$s. Nothing is added and nothing is lost — you have just described the same pile differently.

Now the whole thing is made of $2$s, and there are three times as many of them as there were $6$s.

Three times a whole number is a whole number, so the count is fine. And nothing is left over, because nothing was left over before and we only re-grouped.

So the number is a whole number of $2$s, which means $2$ goes into it.

And we never said *which* multiple of $6$ we started with — so this works for all of them.
</Proof>

Read the last two paragraphs again. The proof did not stop at "it is made of $2$s"; it explicitly said *and the count is a whole number*, and *we never used which number it was*. Those two sentences are what make it a proof rather than an example.

<Aside>
Almost everything in the rest of this chapter is this one move repeated: take what you are given, re-group it, and pull out the factor you want at the end. Module 4.3 turns it into a template you can follow every time.
</Aside>
