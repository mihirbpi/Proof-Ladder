A number is **even** when you can pair everything up with nothing left over. It is **odd** when exactly one is left over.

$8$ counters: four pairs, nothing left. **Even.**

$9$ counters: four pairs and a lonely one. **Odd.**

## The real reason for the last-digit trick

You probably know the trick: look at the last digit. If it is $0, 2, 4, 6$ or $8$, the number is even.

That trick works, but it is not the *reason*. The reason is pairing.

Here is why the trick works, using pairing.

<Proof level="l2">
Take any number, say $46$. Split it into the tens part and the last digit: $46$ is $40$ and $6$.

The tens part is always made of $10$s. And $10$ pairs up perfectly — five pairs, nothing left. So *any* number of tens pairs up perfectly.

That means the tens part never leaves anything over. Only the last digit can.

So the whole number is even exactly when its last digit is.
</Proof>

Now you know why, not just what.

## Doubles

Here is another way to say even. A number is even exactly when it is somebody's **double**.

$8$ is double $4$. $12$ is double $6$. $0$ is double $0$.

And $9$? Nothing doubles to $9$. So $9$ is not even.

This version is the one that grown-up mathematicians use, because "$n$ is double something" gives you an actual thing to work with, and "$n$ pairs up nicely" does not.

<Aside>
Careful with that word *something*. It has to be a **whole number**. Is $9$ double something? Well, $9$ is double $4\tfrac12$ — but $4\tfrac12$ is not a whole number, so it does not count. The whole-number part is doing all the work.
</Aside>

## Going into

Pairing is about sharing between $2$. But you can share between any number.

$12$ shared between $3$: four each, nothing left. We say $3$ **goes into** $12$.

$13$ shared between $3$: four each, one left over. So $3$ does **not** go into $13$.

<TryIt>
Take 12 counters. Share them between 2, then 3, then 4, then 5. Which ones come out with nothing left over?
</TryIt>
