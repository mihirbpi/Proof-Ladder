## Definitions

For a whole number $n$:
$$
n \text{ is \textbf{even}} \iff \exists k \in \mathbb{Z},\ n = 2k,
$$

*So the line reads: $n$ is even exactly when there is some whole number $k$ with $n$ equal to $2$ times $k$.*

$$
n \text{ is \textbf{odd}} \iff \exists k \in \mathbb{Z},\ n = 2k+1.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when". So the line reads: a number is even exactly when it is $2$ times some whole number.*So the line reads: a number is odd exactly when it is $2$ times some whole number, plus one.*

And in general, for whole numbers $m$ and $n$:
$$
m \mid n \iff \exists k \in \mathbb{Z},\ n = mk.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: $m$ divides $n$ exactly when $n$ is $m$ times some whole number.*

Read $m \mid n$ as "$m$ **divides** $n$", or "$m$ is a factor of $n$", or "$n$ is a multiple of $m$". Its negation is written $m \nmid n$.

So "even" is exactly "$2 \mid n$", and "odd" is exactly "$2 \nmid n$" — that second one needing the division algorithm of module 4.4 to see, since it requires that a non-multiple of $2$ must have remainder exactly $1$.

## Divisibility is existential

This is the structural fact that determines every proof in the chapter.
$$
m \mid n \quad\text{is an } \exists \text{ statement.}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\exists$ is "there is some", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: *$m$ divides $n$* is a there-is claim: it says that some whole number exists.*

By module 1.12:

- **To prove that $m$ divides $n$:** produce a **witness** — an actual whole number $k$ with $n = mk$. Both halves matter: produce it, *and* check that it really is a whole number.
- **To disprove it:** show that **no** whole number works. That is a claim about every candidate at once, so it is an argument and not a search — usually a remainder argument (module 4.4).

<Warning title="Verify the witness is an integer">
$9 = 2 \cdot \tfrac92$ does not make $9$ even, because $\tfrac92 \notin \mathbb{Z}$. The integrality requirement is the entire content of the definition — without it every integer divides every other.

In practice this means every divisibility proof ends by citing closure (module 4.1): *"and that factor is a whole number, since whole numbers stay whole when you multiplication."
</Warning>

<Warning title="Direction and type">
$m \mid n$ means $m$ **divides** $n$: the divisor is on the left. So $3 \mid 12$ is true and $12 \mid 3$ is false.

Also: $m \mid n$ is a **statement** (true or false), while $m/n$ is a **number**. Writing "$3 \mid 12 = 4$" is a type error.
</Warning>

## Worked proofs

> **Proposition.** For all $a, b, n \in \mathbb{Z}$: if $a \mid b$ and $b \mid n$, then $a \mid n$.

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, the bar $\mid$ is "divides". So the line reads: if $a$ divides $b$ and $b$ divides $n$, then $a$ divides $n$ — dividing passes along a chain.*

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$b = ak_1$ and $n = bk_2$ for some integers $k_1, k_2$. Note we must use *different* names — reusing $k$ would say the two leftover factors are equal, which was never given.</div>
  <div slot="want">An integer $k$ with $n = ak$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Substitute the first equation into the second; the witness is $k_1k_2$, an integer by closure.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>

*The symbols in this proof: $\in$ is "is in", $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, the bar $\mid$ is "divides". In words, what follows shows: dividing passes along a chain, and the proof just multiplies the two factors together.*

Suppose $a \mid b$ and $b \mid n$. Then there exist $k_1, k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$ with $b = ak_1$ and $n = bk_2$.

Substituting, $n = (ak_1)k_2 = a(k_1k_2)$.

Since $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under multiplication, $k_1k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. So $k_1k_2$ is a witness, and $a \mid n$.
</Proof>

> **Proposition.** For any whole number $n$: $n$ is even exactly when $n^2$ is even.

This is module 1.9's theorem, restated in divisibility language — a good illustration that new notation often just renames results you already have. Its proof was: forward directly, backward by contrapositive.

## Restating old results

Divisibility notation compresses several earlier statements:

| Old phrasing | Divisibility form |
|---|---|
| $n$ is even | $2$ divides $n$ |
| $n$ is a multiple of $6$ | $6$ divides $n$ |
| $n^2$ even forces $n$ even | $2$ divides $n^2$ forces $2$ divides $n$ |
| $mn$ odd $\Rightarrow$ $m$ and $n$ odd | $2 \nmid mn \Rightarrow 2\nmid m \wedge 2 \nmid n$ |

<Aside>
Some quick facts, each provable in two lines from the definition: $m \mid 0$ for every $m$ (take $k=0$); $1 \mid n$ for every $n$; $n \mid n$; and if $m \mid n$ with $n \neq 0$ then $|m| \leq |n|$. That last one is the reason divisor sets are finite, and it uses the order on $\mathbb{Z}$ rather than the arithmetic rules — note that $m \mid 0$ for all $m$ is exactly the exception it needs.
</Aside>
