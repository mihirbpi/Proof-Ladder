For $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$:
$$
m \mid n \;:\iff\; \exists k \in \mathbb{Z},\ n = mk,
$$
with $n$ **even** meaning $2 \mid n$ and **odd** meaning $n = 2k+1$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

## The quantifier structure is the method

$m \mid n$ is an existential statement over $\mathbb{Z}$, and §1.12 fixes what follows:

- **Proving $m \mid n$** means exhibiting a witness $k$ and verifying $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. The verification is not decoration: it is where closure (§4.1) is cited, and it is the step that fails in the wrong ring.
- **Disproving $m \mid n$** means proving $\forall k \in \mathbb{Z},\ n \neq mk$ — a universal claim requiring an argument, typically supplied by the division algorithm (§4.4) or by a size bound.

<Warning title="Three standard slips">
**Non-integral witness.** $9 = 2\cdot\tfrac92$ proves nothing; $\tfrac92 \notin \mathbb{Z}$.

**Name collision.** From $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, write $b = ak_1$ and $c = bk_2$ with *distinct* names. Reusing $k$ silently asserts the cofactors are equal — a hypothesis never given, and a genuinely common error.

**Type confusion.** $m \mid n$ is a proposition; $m/n$ is a number. "$3 \mid 12 = 4$" is ill-formed.
</Warning>

## Elementary properties

For all $a,b,c,m,n \in \mathbb{Z}$:

$$
m \mid 0, \qquad 1 \mid n, \qquad n \mid n,
$$
$$
a \mid b \wedge b \mid c \Rightarrow a \mid c \quad (\text{transitivity}),
$$
$$
a \mid b \wedge a \mid c \Rightarrow a \mid (bx + cy) \ \ \forall x,y \in \mathbb{Z},
$$
$$
a \mid b \wedge b \mid a \Rightarrow a = \pm b,
$$
$$
a \mid b \wedge b \neq 0 \Rightarrow |a| \leq |b|.
$$

<Proof title="Proof of the linear-combination property">
Suppose $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$, say $b = ak_1$ and $c = ak_2$ with $k_1,k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. For any $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$,
$$bx + cy = ak_1x + ak_2y = a(k_1x + k_2y),$$
and $k_1x + k_2y \in \mathbb{Z}$ by closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ under multiplication and addition. Hence $a \mid bx+cy$.
</Proof>

That property is the workhorse of elementary number theory — it is what makes the Euclidean algorithm terminate correctly and what proves Bézout's identity — and its proof is four lines of closure.

Note that $m \mid 0$ for every $m$, including $m = 0$: $0 = 0\cdot 0$. So $0 \mid 0$ is *true*, while $0 \mid n$ is false for $n \neq 0$. This is consistent and occasionally surprising; it is also why the last property above needs $b \neq 0$.

## Parity as a structure-preserving map

The even/odd arithmetic of this module is more structured than the individual computations suggest. Define the **parity** of $n$ to be its remainder mod $2$. Then

$$\operatorname{par}(m+n) = \operatorname{par}(m) + \operatorname{par}(n), \qquad \operatorname{par}(mn) = \operatorname{par}(m)\operatorname{par}(n),$$

with the right-hand arithmetic done mod $2$. A map preserving both operations is called **structure-preserving**, and the five parity facts of this module are its multiplication and addition tables — five separate proofs replaced by one structural statement.

This is the pattern that recurs throughout the course: a family of facts first proved individually, then subsumed by identifying the structure that made them true. §4.4's congruences do the same for general moduli, and the payoff is that the general statement is easier to remember and easier to apply than the list.

Parity also supplies the fastest route to several classic results. A chessboard with two opposite corners removed cannot be tiled by dominoes, because each domino covers one square of each colour and the mutilated board has unequal counts. That argument is a parity argument, and it is complete — no case analysis, no search.

## Divisibility as an order

Transitivity and reflexivity make $\mid$ behave like an ordering on $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ — much as $\leq$ does, and much as $\subseteq$ does on sets (§2.3). It is a *partial* ordering only: given two numbers, neither need divide the other, so unlike $\leq$ it does not line everything up in a row. The structure is rich:

- $\gcd$ plays the role of $\cap$ and $\operatorname{lcm}$ the role of $\cup$ (§2.4 identified these as intersections of divisor and multiple sets);
- least element $1$, and $0$ is the greatest element if included, since everything divides $0$;
- the building blocks — the numbers with nothing strictly between them and $1$ — are exactly the primes.

So divisibility on $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ has the same shape as inclusion on subsets in §2.3, with $\gcd$ and $\operatorname{lcm}$ playing $\cap$ and $\cup$ — including both distributive laws.

## The ring-theoretic reading

$$
m \mid n \iff n \in (m) \iff (n) \subseteq (m),
$$
where $(m) = m\mathbb{Z}$ is the set of multiples of $m$. Divisibility is reverse inclusion of these sets — "to divide is to contain" — and that reformulation is the one that survives into settings where factorisation itself misbehaves.

Units divide everything, which is why $\pm 1 \mid n$ always, and why divisibility can only ever be well defined up to units.

<Aside>
The definition transfers verbatim to any commutative ring, and its behaviour changes dramatically. In a field every nonzero element divides every element, so divisibility is trivial — which is exactly why number theory studies $\mathbb{Z}$ rather than $\mathbb{Q}$. In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ divisibility is polynomial factorisation. In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, $6 = 2\cdot3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ shows factorisation into irreducibles is not unique, which is the failure that forced the invention of ideals.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

The move this module trains — **replace a descriptive word by an algebraic condition you can compute with** — is the one that makes the definitions ahead usable.

"Even" became $n = 2k$; "divides" became $b = ak$. Neither description supported algebra, and both existential forms do.

**The same move is what makes limit proofs possible.** "Close to $L$" is a description; *the gap between them is smaller than this* is an inequality you can manipulate. "Approaches" is a description; the official definition is a condition. The reason the definition of a limit looks so unlike the intuition is that the intuition cannot be computed with, and the definition can.

**Divisibility itself reappears in calculus,** in the trigonometric solution sets. The equation $\sin\theta = 0$ has solution set $\{\theta : \theta = k\pi,\ k\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ — an existential condition of exactly the form this module defines, and the parameter $k$ is the witness. Periodicity statements are divisibility statements: $f$ has period $p$ when $f(x+kp) = f(x)$ for every integer $k$.

Every one of these is an **existential statement**, so the rules of §1.12 apply unchanged: to prove one, exhibit the witness; to use one, name it; to refute one, rule out all candidates — which is the hard direction, and the reason spanning is harder to disprove than to prove.

</WhereThisGoes>
