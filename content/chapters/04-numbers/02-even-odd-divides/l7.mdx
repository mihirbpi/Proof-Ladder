For $m, n \in \mathbb{Z}$:
$$
m \mid n \;:\iff\; \exists k \in \mathbb{Z},\ n = mk,
$$
with $n$ **even** meaning $2 \mid n$ and **odd** meaning $n = 2k+1$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$.

## The quantifier structure is the method

$m \mid n$ is an existential statement over $\mathbb{Z}$, and §1.12 fixes what follows:

- **Proving $m \mid n$** means exhibiting a witness $k$ and verifying $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. The verification is not decoration: it is where closure (§4.1) is cited, and it is the step that fails in the wrong ring.
- **Disproving $m \mid n$** means proving $\forall k \in \mathbb{Z},\ n \neq mk$ — a universal claim requiring an argument, typically supplied by the division algorithm (§4.4) or by a size bound.

<Warning title="Three standard slips">
**Non-integral witness.** $9 = 2\cdot\tfrac92$ proves nothing; $\tfrac92 \notin \mathbb{Z}$.

**Name collision.** From $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$, write $b = ak_1$ and $c = bk_2$ with *distinct* names. Reusing $k$ silently asserts the cofactors are equal — a hypothesis never given, and a genuinely common error.

**Type confusion.** $m \mid n$ is a proposition; $m/n$ is a number. "$3 \mid 12 = 4$" is ill-formed.
</Warning>

## Elementary properties

For all $a,b,c,m,n \in \mathbb{Z}$:

$$
m \mid 0, \qquad 1 \mid n, \qquad n \mid n,
$$
$$
a \mid b \wedge b \mid c \implies a \mid c \quad (\text{transitivity}),
$$
$$
a \mid b \wedge a \mid c \implies a \mid (bx + cy) \ \ \forall x,y \in \mathbb{Z},
$$
$$
a \mid b \wedge b \mid a \implies a = \pm b,
$$
$$
a \mid b \wedge b \neq 0 \implies |a| \leq |b|.
$$

<Proof title="Proof of the linear-combination property">
Suppose $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$, say $b = ak_1$ and $c = ak_2$ with $k_1,k_2 \in \mathbb{Z}$. For any $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$,
$$bx + cy = ak_1x + ak_2y = a(k_1x + k_2y),$$
and $k_1x + k_2y \in \mathbb{Z}$ by closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ under multiplication and addition. Hence $a \mid bx+cy$.
</Proof>

That property is the workhorse of elementary number theory — it is what makes the Euclidean algorithm terminate correctly and what proves Bézout's identity — and its proof is four lines of closure.

Note that $m \mid 0$ for every $m$, including $m = 0$: $0 = 0\cdot 0$. So $0 \mid 0$ is *true*, while $0 \mid n$ is false for $n \neq 0$. This is consistent and occasionally surprising; it is also why the last property above needs $b \neq 0$.

## Divisibility as an order

Transitivity and reflexivity make $\mid$ a **preorder** on $\mathbb{Z}$. It is not a partial order, since $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$ give only $a = \pm b$ — antisymmetry fails by units. Restricted to $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ it *is* a partial order, and a rather rich one:

- meet = $\gcd$, join = $\operatorname{lcm}$ (§2.4 identified these as intersections of divisor and multiple sets);
- least element $1$, and $0$ is the greatest element if included, since everything divides $0$;
- the atoms are exactly the primes.

So the divisibility lattice on $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ has the same shape as the subset lattice of §2.3, with $\gcd$ and $\operatorname{lcm}$ playing $\cap$ and $\cup$ — including both distributive laws.

## The ring-theoretic reading

$$
m \mid n \iff n \in (m) \iff (n) \subseteq (m),
$$
where $(m) = m\mathbb{Z}$ is the ideal generated by $m$. Divisibility is reverse inclusion of principal ideals — "to divide is to contain" — and this is the formulation that survives in general rings, where elements may not have unique factorisations but ideals still behave.

Units divide everything, which is why $\pm 1 \mid n$ always, and why divisibility can only ever be well defined up to units.

<Aside>
The definition transfers verbatim to any commutative ring, and its behaviour changes dramatically. In a field every nonzero element divides every element, so divisibility is trivial — which is exactly why number theory studies $\mathbb{Z}$ rather than $\mathbb{Q}$. In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ divisibility is polynomial factorisation. In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, $6 = 2\cdot3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ shows factorisation into irreducibles is not unique, which is the failure that forced the invention of ideals.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
Divisibility organises all of elementary number theory: the Euclidean algorithm computes gcds, Bézout's identity writes $\gcd(a,b) = ax+by$ using the linear-combination property proved above, Euclid's lemma ($p \mid ab \Rightarrow p\mid a$ or $p \mid b$ for prime $p$) follows from Bézout, and unique factorisation follows from Euclid's lemma. Each step is short; the sequence is the backbone of the subject. Beyond it, the reformulation "to divide is to contain" is what lets Dedekind recover unique factorisation in rings of algebraic integers by factoring *ideals* instead of elements — the birth of algebraic number theory, and the reason class groups measure exactly how badly unique factorisation fails.
</WhereThisGoes>
