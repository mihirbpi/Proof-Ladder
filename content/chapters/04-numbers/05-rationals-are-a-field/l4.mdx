$$
\mathbb{Q} = \left\{\, \frac{p}{q} \;\middle|\; p, q \in \mathbb{Z},\ q \neq 0 \,\right\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: the fractions are all the ways of writing one integer over another, so long as the bottom is not zero.*

Every integer is a fraction, since $n = \tfrac n1$ — and the fractions have more besides, since $\tfrac12$ is not an integer.

Recall that a rational has many names: $\tfrac{p_1}{q_1} = \tfrac{p_2}{q_2}$ iff $p_1q_2 = p_2q_1$. Any operation defined via representatives must be checked to be well defined — module 3.1's thing to check. The formulas below all pass that check, and verifying one of them is a worthwhile exercise.

## The four closure properties

Throughout, $\tfrac ab, \tfrac cd \in \mathbb{Q}$ with $b, d \neq 0$.

> **(F1) Addition.** $\dfrac ab + \dfrac cd = \dfrac{ad+bc}{bd} \in \mathbb{Q}$.

<Proof>

*The symbols in this proof: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions. In words, what follows shows: adding two fractions gives a fraction, because top and bottom stay whole and the bottom stays non-zero.*

The numerator $ad + bc$ is an integer, being built from integers by multiplication and addition ($\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under both, module 4.1).

The denominator $bd$ is an integer for the same reason, and $bd \neq 0$: since a product of non-zero integers is never zero, a product of nonzero integers is nonzero.

Then the sum has integer numerator and nonzero integer denominator, so lies in $\mathbb{Q}$.
</Proof>

Notice the proof does no arithmetic. It verifies that the pieces are of the right *type*. Every closure proof looks like this, and the step that could fail — $bd \neq 0$ — uses one special property of $\mathbb{Z}$: a product of nonzero integers is never zero.

> **(F2) Negatives.** The negative of $\tfrac ab$ is $\tfrac{-a}{b}$, which is again a whole number over a non-zero whole number — so again a fraction.

> **(F3) Multiplication.** $\dfrac ab \cdot \dfrac cd = \dfrac{ac}{bd} \in \mathbb{Q}$, by the same two observations as (F1).

> **(F4) Multiplicative inverses.** If $\dfrac ab \neq 0$ then $\dfrac ba \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $\dfrac ab \cdot \dfrac ba = 1$.

<Proof>
$\tfrac ab \neq 0$ means $a \neq 0$ (a fraction is zero exactly when its numerator is). Together with $b \neq 0$ from the hypothesis, $\tfrac ba$ has integer numerator and nonzero integer denominator, so $\tfrac ba \in \mathbb{Q}$.

Then $\tfrac ab \cdot \tfrac ba = \tfrac{ab}{ba} = 1$.
</Proof>

Track the two nonzero conditions: $b \neq 0$ was needed for $\tfrac ab$ to be a rational at all, and $a \neq 0$ was needed for the reciprocal to exist. They do different jobs and both are essential.

## What division buys

| Number system | add, subtract, multiply | can you always divide? |
|---|---|---|
| whole numbers | ✓ | ✗ — $1 \div 3$ leaves them |
| fractions | ✓ | ✓ (except by zero) |
| the reals | ✓ | ✓ (except by zero) |
| hours on a $6$-hour clock | ✓ | ✗ — $2$, $3$ and $4$ have no reciprocal |
| hours on a $5$-hour clock | ✓ | ✓ (except by zero) |

Being able to divide is one extra property, and it is the one that licenses:

- **solving $ax = b$** when $a \neq 0$ — impossible over the whole numbers, where $3x = 1$ has no answer;
- **canceling**: from $ab = ac$ with $a \neq 0$ you may conclude $b = c$, by multiplying both sides by the reciprocal of $a$.

Notice the two clocks. The size of the clock decides whether division works — and $5$ being prime is exactly why the second one behaves. That is not a coincidence, and Chapter 4 has already given you the tools to see why.

<Warning title="Zero is excluded permanently">
There is no reciprocal of $0$, and that is forced rather than agreed. Suppose $0 \cdot x = 1$ for some $x$. But $0 \cdot x = 0$ is a theorem (module 4.1), so $0 = 1$ — and multiplying that by any $y$ gives $y = 0$, collapsing every number to zero.

So "you cannot divide by zero" is not a rule imposed from outside. It is what the other rules force.
</Warning>

## What the rationals still cannot do

$\mathbb{Q}$ fixed division. It did not fix everything.

- $x^2 = 2$ has no solution among the fractions (module 4.7); the full number line fixes that.
- $x^2 = -1$ has no solution on the number line; the complex numbers fix this (Chapter 5).
- $\mathbb{Q}$ has **gaps**. You can march along fractions getting closer and closer to a definite spot on the line and find that the spot itself is not a fraction — $\sqrt2$ is one such spot (module 4.7). Filling those gaps is what the real numbers are for, and it is why later mathematics is done over the reals rather than the fractions.

The pattern proved in module 4.1 continues: an operation escapes the system, so the system is enlarged.

<Aside>
The fractions are the *smallest* system closed under all four operations that contains the integers — any such system must contain all quotients $p/q$, so all of $\mathbb{Q}$. The construction generalizes. Start with any system where a product of nonzero elements is never zero, run the same recipe, and you get the smallest such system containing it. Applied to the expressions of Algebra I, it produces the fractions built from them — one such expression written over another — which is why those behave so much like ordinary fractions.
</Aside>
