In module 4.1 you found a hole. Adding whole numbers keeps you inside the whole numbers. Sharing them out does not.

Here are $7$ cookies and $2$ friends. Give each friend $3$. One cookie is left over.

What do you do with the last one?

## You snap it in half

Fold a paper so both sides match exactly. Each side is a **half**.

Fold it again. Now there are four matching parts. Each one is a **quarter**.

Cut a sandwich into three matching parts. Each one is a **third**.

None of these is a whole number. But you can hold one. You can eat one. You can point at one and say *that much*. So they are amounts, and amounts are numbers.

We will call them **pieces**.

## Pieces fill the hole

Go back to the sharing that got stuck.

$7$ cookies, $2$ friends. Each friend gets $3$ and a half. Now it has an answer.

Try the hard ones.

- $1$ cookie, $2$ friends. A half each.
- $1$ cookie, $4$ friends. A quarter each.
- $1$ cake, $3$ friends. A third each.

With only whole numbers, not one of those had an answer. There is no whole number between $0$ and $1$ to give anybody.

With pieces, **every one of them has an answer.** That is the hole, filled.

<Aside>
There is one sharing that still has no answer, and it is the only one: sharing among **no** friends. Nobody is there to hand a cookie to. That is why you can never share by zero, at any level of mathematics, forever.
</Aside>

## The picture worth keeping

Whole numbers answer *how many*.

Pieces answer *how much of one*.

You needed both, and the whole numbers alone could only do the first. Module 4.6 shows something surprising about how tightly the pieces pack in, and much later the course finds a length that even the pieces cannot reach.

<TryIt>
Fold a paper in half, then in half again, and open it up. Count the matching parts: $4$.

Now colour $1$ of them. You have coloured a quarter.

Try to fold a paper into $3$ matching parts. It is much harder than $4$ — and that is a real fact about $3$, not about your folding.
</TryIt>
