$\mathbb{Z}$ failed one row of module 4.1's table: no multiplicative inverses. The repair is to build a system that has them.

## Definition

The **rational numbers** are written $\mathbb{Q}$:

$$
\mathbb{Q} = \left\{\, \frac{a}{b} \;:\; a, b\in\mathbb{Z},\ b \neq 0 \,\right\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\in$ is "is in". So the line reads: the fractions are one integer over another, with a nonzero bottom.*

The **rational numbers**. Note the set-builder form from module 2.2: the collection you are drawing from is $\mathbb{Z}$, and $b\neq 0$ is part of the condition, not a footnote.

A subtlety worth naming: $\frac12$ and $\frac{2}{4}$ are the same rational number written two ways. The pair $(a,b)$ is not the number — the number is what all the equivalent pairs have in common. Compare module 3.2's point that different formulas can give the same function.

## Closure

> **Proposition.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division by non-zero elements.

<Proof>
Let $x = \frac ab$ and $y = \frac cd$ with $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $b,d\neq0$.

**Addition.** $x + y = \dfrac{ad+bc}{bd}$. The numerator is an integer and the denominator is an integer, both by closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ (module 4.1). And $bd\neq0$, since a product of non-zero integers is non-zero. So $x+y\in\mathbb{Q}$.

**Multiplication.** $xy = \dfrac{ac}{bd}$, an integer over a non-zero integer by the same reasoning.

**Subtraction.** $x - y = \dfrac{ad-bc}{bd}$, likewise.

**Division.** Suppose $y \neq 0$, so $c \neq 0$. Then $\dfrac{x}{y} = \dfrac{ad}{bc}$, and $bc\neq0$ since both factors are non-zero.
</Proof>

**Every step cited closure of $\mathbb{Z}$.** The rationals inherit their good behavior from the integers, and the whole proof is the fraction arithmetic you already know, checked against the definition rather than assumed.

The step *"a product of non-zero integers is non-zero"* is doing real work and is not free — it is the statement that a product of two non-zero integers is never zero, which fails in some systems. The number grids Algebra II calls *matrices*, for instance, can multiply to give all zeros without either one being all zeros.

## Division

Here is the one property that separates the fractions from the integers: **every non-zero fraction has a reciprocal, and it is a fraction.**

| | add, subtract, multiply | divide by anything non-zero |
|---|---|---|
| the integers | ✓ | ✗ |
| the fractions | ✓ | ✓ |
| the reals | ✓ | ✓ |
| polynomials | ✓ | ✗ |

The reciprocal of $\frac ab$ (with $a\neq0$) is $\frac ba$, and
$$\frac ab \cdot \frac ba = \frac{ab}{ba} = 1.$$

That single line is the whole difference, and it is why $3x = 7$ can be solved over the fractions and not over the integers.

<Warning title="Zero has no reciprocal, in any number system">
$0$ never has one. Suppose $b$ satisfied $0\cdot b = 1$. Module 4.1 proved $0\cdot b = 0$, so $0 = 1$ — and multiplying that by anything collapses every number to zero.

So $\frac10$ is undefined, and undefined for a **provable** reason rather than by convention. No number system repairs it: adding a reciprocal for $0$ destroys the system.
</Warning>

## Why coordinate geometry needs division

Every algebraic move in a coordinate proof needs it.

The slope $\frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$ is a division. Solving $y = mx+b$ for $x$ divides by $m$. The midpoint formula, the section formula and the intersection point of two lines all need reciprocals.

Integers would not do. Two lines with integer coefficients can meet at a non-integer point, so the coordinate system must be closed under division or the intersection would fall outside it.

**And the fractions are not enough either.** The diagonal of a unit square has length $\sqrt2$, and module 4.7 proves it is not a fraction. So a plane with only fraction coordinates has *holes*: the circle $x^2+y^2 = 2$ and the line $y = x$ would fail to meet, even though the picture plainly shows them crossing.

That is why the Ruler Postulate names the real numbers and not the fractions. Your geometry needs a system that can divide **and** has no gaps, and pinning one down took mathematicians until the 1870s.

## Order

The fractions also carry an ordering that fits the arithmetic:

$$a < b \Rightarrow a + c < b+c \quad\text{for all } c, \qquad\quad a<b \text{ and } c>0 \Rightarrow ac<bc$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces". So the line reads: adding the same thing to both sides keeps the inequality; multiplying both sides by something positive keeps it too.*

Both rules sit behind every inequality you have ever manipulated, and the second is the one that flips: multiplying by a negative reverses the inequality, because $c<0$ falls outside the stated hypothesis.

The reals order the same way. The complex numbers of Chapter 5 have **no** ordering compatible with their arithmetic at all, which is a genuine loss and worth remembering when you get there.

<Aside>
A system where you can always divide need not be infinite. Take the remainders $\{0,1,2,3,4\}$ on division by $5$, adding and multiplying and keeping the remainder each time (module 4.4).

Every non-zero one has a reciprocal: $2\cdot3 = 6$, which leaves remainder $1$; $4\cdot4=16$, remainder $1$; and $1\cdot1=1$. So division always works, in a system with five elements.

On division by $6$ it fails: $2\cdot 3 = 6$ leaves remainder $0$, so two non-zero things multiply to zero, and neither can have a reciprocal. The pattern is that it works exactly when the divisor is prime.

Systems like these are what error-correcting codes are built from — the reason a scratched disc still plays, and a QR code survives being partly obscured.
</Aside>

## What is still missing

$\mathbb{Q}$ has every algebraic property you could want, and it is still not enough for geometry.

The missing property is that the number line should have **no gaps**. $\mathbb{Q}$ has gaps at $\sqrt2$, at $\pi$, and at infinitely many other places. $\mathbb{R}$ is what you get by filling them, and the next three modules are about the difference — first that $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense, then that it is nonetheless incomplete, then what the gap-filling numbers look like.
