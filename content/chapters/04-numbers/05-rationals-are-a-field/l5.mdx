$$
\mathbb{Q} = \left\{\, \frac{a}{b} \ \middle|\ a, b \in \mathbb{Z},\ b \neq 0 \,\right\}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: the fractions are all the ways of writing one integer over another, with a nonzero bottom.*

A **rational number** is one *expressible* as a ratio of integers with nonzero denominator. Expressible — $7$ is rational, written $7/1$, and so is $0.25$, written $1/4$.

Module 4.1 found $\mathbb{Z}$ closed under $+$, $-$, $\times$ and not under division. $\mathbb{Q}$ repairs exactly that failure, and this module proves it.

## The closure proofs

Each is a fraction formula from Algebra I, read as a closure statement.

> **Proposition.** $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under addition.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Two arbitrary rationals, each unpacked into a ratio — and, as in module 4.3, they need <strong>different letters</strong>.</div>
  <div slot="want">Their sum exhibited as a ratio of integers with nonzero denominator.</div>
  <div slot="how">The common-denominator formula produces the ratio. Then two checks: numerator and denominator are integers, and the denominator is nonzero.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $x, y \in \mathbb{Q}$ be arbitrary. Then $x = a/b$ and $y = c/d$ with $a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $b, d \neq 0$.
$$
x + y = \frac{a}{b} + \frac{c}{d} = \frac{ad + bc}{bd}.
$$
By closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ (module 4.1), $ad + bc \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $bd \in \mathbb{Z}$.

And $bd \neq 0$, since a product of nonzero integers is nonzero.

Then $x + y$ is a ratio of integers with nonzero denominator, so rational.
</Proof>

**Note what the proof rests on**: closure of $\mathbb{Z}$, cited twice. Every fact about $\mathbb{Q}$ in this module is a fact about $\mathbb{Z}$ in disguise, which is why Chapter 4 built $\mathbb{Z}$ first.

The other three run identically:

$$
-\frac ab = \frac{-a}{b}, \qquad
\frac ab \cdot \frac cd = \frac{ac}{bd}, \qquad
\left(\frac ab\right)^{-1} = \frac ba.
$$

Each time: apply the formula, confirm numerator and denominator are integers, confirm the denominator is nonzero.

## The one that needs care

<Warning title="The reciprocal needs a nonzero numerator">
For $\left(\frac ab\right)^{-1} = \frac ba$ to be a rational number, we need $a \neq 0$.

And $a \neq 0$ is exactly the condition $x \neq 0$: a fraction is zero exactly when its numerator is.

So the closure statement is not "$\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under reciprocals" — it is **"$\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under reciprocals of nonzero elements."** The exception at $0$ is real and cannot be removed.

This is not a blemish. **No number system can have a reciprocal for $0$.** If $0 \cdot z = 1$ for some $z$, then since $0 \cdot z = 0$ we would get $0 = 1$, and everything collapses. Division by zero is undefined in every number system there is or could be.
</Warning>

## Systems where you can always divide

A system closed under $+$, $-$, $\times$, **and** division by nonzero elements — with the distributive law — is a system in which ordinary algebra works.

So the difference from module 4.1's list is one property:

| | closed under $+,-,\times$ | reciprocals of nonzero |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | ✓ | ✗ — only $\pm1$ |
| $\mathbb{Q}$ | ✓ | ✓ |
| $\mathbb{R}$ | ✓ | ✓ |
| $\mathbb{C}$ | ✓ | ✓ |
| polynomials | ✓ | ✗ |

$\mathbb{Z}$ obeys the arithmetic rules but has no reciprocals. The fractions, the reals and the complex numbers all do have them. Polynomials are like the integers in this respect — $1/x$ is not a polynomial — and the parallel is close enough that polynomial long division works exactly as long division does on whole numbers.

**This is where ordinary algebra works.** Every manipulation you learned in Algebra I — collecting terms, clearing denominators, dividing both sides — is licensed by those properties, and each is available exactly when the system has them.

## Why this matters for solving equations

$ax + b = 0$ with $a \neq 0$ has the solution $x = -b/a$.

That step is one of those properties being used. In $\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{R}$ you may divide by $a$, so the equation always has a solution. In $\mathbb{Z}$ you may not: $3x - 1 = 0$ has no integer solution, and module 1.10 said so without explaining why.

**Now there is a reason.** The equation is unsolvable in $\mathbb{Z}$ because you cannot divide in $\mathbb{Z}$, and it becomes solvable in $\mathbb{Q}$ for exactly the property $\mathbb{Q}$ adds.

<Aside>
$\mathbb{Q}$ is the *smallest* system closed under all four operations containing $\mathbb{Z}$, and "smallest" can be made precise.

Any such system containing $\mathbb{Z}$ must contain every $a \cdot b^{-1}$ with $b \neq 0$, by closure — and those are exactly the rationals. So there is no room to fit such a system strictly between $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$.

The construction is reusable. Start with any system obeying the arithmetic rules in which a product of nonzero things is never zero, build pairs, and declare $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ to name the same thing when $ad = bc$. What comes out is the smallest enlargement in which you can divide — and applied to the integers, what comes out is the fractions.
</Aside>
