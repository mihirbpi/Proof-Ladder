Module 4.1 found a gap in the counting numbers: they are not closed under dividing. $7 \div 2$ runs off the edge.

This module looks at the number system built to fill that gap.

## Fractions

A **fraction** is a number written as one counting number over another, with the bottom never zero:
$$
\tfrac34, \qquad \tfrac{7}{2}, \qquad \tfrac{12}{5}, \qquad \tfrac{5}{1}.
$$

That last one is worth noticing. $\tfrac51$ is just $5$. So **every counting number is already a fraction** — write it over $1$. The fractions do not replace the counting numbers; they contain them and add more.

And there really are more: $\tfrac12$ is a fraction and is not a counting number.

## Checking closure

You know how to add and multiply fractions. What you may not have noticed is that those procedures are *closure proofs* — each one shows the answer is still a fraction.

**Adding.**
$$
\tfrac34 + \tfrac25 = \tfrac{15}{20} + \tfrac{8}{20} = \tfrac{23}{20}.
$$

<Proof>
Take any two fractions and follow them. Each is a counting number over a counting number, with neither bottom zero.

To add them, rewrite both over a common bottom — multiplying the two bottoms together always gives one that works.

The new top is a sum of products of counting numbers, and the new bottom is a product of counting numbers. Both are counting numbers, because counting numbers are closed under adding and multiplying (module 4.1).

And the new bottom is not zero, since neither original bottom was zero, and multiplying two non-zero numbers never gives zero.

Counting number on top, non-zero counting number underneath. So the sum is a fraction.
</Proof>

Read that proof again. It never actually added anything — it just checked that the pieces were the right *kind* of thing. That is what a closure proof looks like.

**Multiplying.** $\tfrac34 \times \tfrac25 = \tfrac{6}{20}$. Same check: top times top is a counting number, bottom times bottom is a non-zero counting number. Closed. ✓

**Dividing.** $\tfrac34 \div \tfrac25 = \tfrac34 \times \tfrac52 = \tfrac{15}{8}$. Also closed — as long as you never divide by zero.

## The new thing fractions have

Here is what fractions have and the counting numbers do not.

The **reciprocal** of a number is what you multiply it by to get $1$.

Among counting numbers, only $1$ has one (module 4.1) — there is nothing you can multiply $5$ by to get $1$ and stay whole.

Among fractions, **everything except zero** has one. Just turn it upside down:
$$
\tfrac34 \times \tfrac43 = \tfrac{12}{12} = 1. \qquad \tfrac{7}{2}\times\tfrac{2}{7} = 1. \qquad 5 \times \tfrac15 = 1.
$$

<Proof>
Take any fraction that is not zero, and follow it. A fraction is zero exactly when its top is zero — so our top is not zero, and our bottom was never zero.

Turn it upside down. The old bottom is now on top, and the old top is now underneath. Both are counting numbers, and the new bottom is not zero.

So the upside-down version is a fraction too.

Multiply them: top times bottom over bottom times top. The same two numbers appear above and below, so the answer is $1$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="Zero has no reciprocal, and that is not a gap">
There is nothing you can multiply $0$ by to get $1$, because $0$ times anything is $0$. So $\tfrac10$ does not exist — not among fractions, not anywhere.

This is not a hole waiting for a cleverer number system. If $\tfrac10$ existed, you could show that $0 = 1$, and then every number would equal every other one and arithmetic would collapse.

Leaving zero out is the price of everything else working.
</Warning>

## What got fixed and what did not

| | closed under $+$ | closed under $\times$ | every non-zero one has a reciprocal |
|---|---|---|---|
| counting numbers | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |
| fractions | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

That single difference in the last column is why you can solve "three times what gives $1$?" with fractions and not with counting numbers.

But fractions did not fix everything. Module 4.7 finds a length you can *draw* — the slanted line across a square — that no fraction can measure exactly. The gap-and-repair story is not over.

<Aside>
A number system where you can add, multiply, and take reciprocals of everything except zero has a name. Mathematicians call it a **field**.

The counting numbers are not a field. The fractions are. That one word summarizes the whole module.
</Aside>
