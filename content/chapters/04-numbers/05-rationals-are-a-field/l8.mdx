$$
\mathbb{Q} = \left\{ \frac pq \;\middle|\; p,q \in \mathbb{Z},\ q \neq 0 \right\},
\qquad
\frac{p_1}{q_1} = \frac{p_2}{q_2} \iff p_1q_2 = p_2q_1.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: the fractions are one integer over a nonzero integer, and two name the same number exactly when their cross-products agree.*

The equality condition is the first thing to take seriously: elements of $\mathbb{Q}$ have infinitely many names, so any rule that works from a name owes you one check: that a different name for the same number gives the same answer. §3.1 called this being well defined.

> **Lemma.** Addition is well defined on $\mathbb{Q}$.

<Proof>
Suppose $\tfrac{a_1}{b_1} = \tfrac{a_2}{b_2}$, i.e. $a_1b_2 = a_2b_1$. We must show $\tfrac{a_1d + b_1c}{b_1d} = \tfrac{a_2d+b_2c}{b_2d}$, i.e.
$$(a_1d+b_1c)(b_2d) = (a_2d+b_2c)(b_1d).$$
Expanding, the left is $a_1b_2d^2 + b_1b_2cd$ and the right is $a_2b_1d^2 + b_1b_2cd$. These agree exactly because $a_1b_2 = a_2b_1$.
</Proof>

The point underneath is that a fraction has many names — $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac24$ are the same number — so any rule written in terms of a name owes you a check that it does not depend on which name you picked. That check is the argument above, and it is the model for every one like it.

## Checking every rule

For fractions $\tfrac ab$ and $\tfrac cd$:

$$
\frac ab + \frac cd = \frac{ad+bc}{bd}, \quad
-\frac ab = \frac{-a}{b}, \quad
\frac ab \cdot \frac cd = \frac{ac}{bd}, \quad
\left(\frac ab\right)^{-1} = \frac ba \ (a \neq 0).
$$

Each closure check has two steps: the numerator and denominator are integers by closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ (§4.1), and the denominator is nonzero because **a product of nonzero integers is never zero**.

For the reciprocal: $\tfrac ab \neq 0$ exactly when $a \neq 0$, so $\tfrac ba$ is a fraction and $\tfrac ab \cdot \tfrac ba = 1$.

So the fractions do everything the integers do, and one thing more: **every nonzero one can be divided by**. That single extra property is what the whole chapter has been building toward.

<Warning title="There is no reciprocal of zero">
Suppose $0x = 1$. But $0x = 0$ is a theorem (§4.1), so $0 = 1$ — and then $y = 1\cdot y = 0\cdot y = 0$ for every $y$, collapsing the entire system to a single element.

"You cannot divide by zero" is therefore a theorem, not a prohibition.
</Warning>

## The same construction, in general

Nothing above used anything about $\mathbb{Z}$ except that a product of nonzeros is nonzero.

That is worth noticing, because it means the construction is reusable. Any system obeying the arithmetic rules in which products of nonzeros stay nonzero can be enlarged the same way — build pairs, declare $(a,b)$ and $(c,d)$ to name the same thing when $ad = bc$, and you get a system in which you can divide.

The no-zero-divisors condition is doing real work. Among remainders on division by $6$ you would want $\tfrac13$ to exist, but $3 \times 2 = 6$ leaves remainder $0$ there, and the construction breaks.

## Why the closure proofs look like fraction arithmetic

Each check above was the fraction rule you learned in primary school, verified against a definition rather than asserted. That is worth pausing on, because it is the first place the course does something subtle.

$$\frac ab + \frac cd = \frac{ad+bc}{bd}$$

is a **definition**, not a discovery. What needs proving is that it is *well defined*: replacing $\tfrac ab$ by an equal fraction $\tfrac{a'}{b'}$ must give an equal answer.

The check is a computation: from $ab' = a'b$ and $cd' = c'd$, verify that $(ad+bc)(b'd') = (a'd'+b'c')(bd)$. It works, and it is the reason the rule is legitimate rather than merely traditional.

**This is the first time in the course that a definition has owed you that check**, and it will not be the last. Whenever the things you are working with have more than one name, a rule that reads the name has to give the same answer for every name.

## Order, and what is still missing

The fractions carry an ordering that fits their arithmetic: adding the same thing to both sides preserves $<$, and multiplying both sides by something positive does too. §4.6 proves they are dense — between any two lies another.

But something is still absent. The fractions $x$ with $x^2 < 2$ have no *smallest* fraction sitting above all of them, because the number that ought to serve, $\sqrt2$, is not one. **That gap is what §4.7 exhibits and what the real numbers exist to fill** — and it, rather than any arithmetic rule, is the property calculus cannot do without.

## Remainder arithmetic where every non-zero thing has a reciprocal

§4.5 built the fractions by insisting every non-zero number get a reciprocal. There is a much smaller system with the same property, and it is worth meeting because it shows the property is not about infinitude.

Fix a prime $p$ and work with remainders on division by $p$ — the numbers $0, 1, \ldots, p-1$, with addition and multiplication carried out and then reduced. Take $p = 5$ and look at what each non-zero remainder multiplies with:

$$1 \cdot 1 = 1, \qquad 2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 1, \qquad 4 \cdot 4 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod 5.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\equiv \ldots \pmod 5$ is §4.4's shorthand for leaving the same remainder on division by $5$. So the line reads: each non-zero remainder has a partner multiplying with it to $1$.*

Every non-zero remainder has a reciprocal, so division is available throughout — in a system of five elements.

**Why the prime matters.** Try $p = 6$ and it breaks: $2 \cdot 3 = 6 \equiv 0$, so two non-zero things multiply to zero, and neither can have a reciprocal — multiplying $2 \cdot 3 = 0$ by a reciprocal of $2$ would give $3 = 0$. That is precisely the no-zero-divisors condition §4.5 needed, and it holds exactly when the number you divide by is prime, since a composite factors into two smaller pieces that multiply back to it.

So the property the fractions were built for is not about being infinite or about being dense. It is about having no way to multiply two non-zero things down to zero, and a five-element system can manage it while the integers cannot.

<WhereThisGoes level="l8">

Being able to divide is what makes ordinary algebra work, and you will feel its absence the moment it is missing.

**Over the integers, none of it works.** Solving $2x = 1$ has no integer answer, so the whole "solve and substitute back" method stops at the first step. Every technique you use without thinking assumes you are allowed to divide.

**In calculus, the ordering rules are what inequality work rests on.** Every limit argument multiplies and divides inequalities, and the rules — adding preserves the direction, multiplying by a positive preserves it, multiplying by a negative reverses it — are exactly the ones stated in this module. Getting the last one wrong is the standard error in a limit proof, and it is a rule violation rather than an algebra slip.

**The gap that remains matters more than any of them.** A collection of fractions can be bounded — never exceeding some fixed size — above and still have no *smallest* upper bound among the fractions — the fractions whose square is below $2$ are the example, since the number that should serve, $\sqrt2$, is not a fraction. Filling that gap is what the real numbers are for, and it is the property calculus genuinely cannot do without. A curve running from below zero to above it must cross zero somewhere: that is false over the fractions, where $x^2 - 2$ changes sign and never lands on zero.

</WhereThisGoes>
