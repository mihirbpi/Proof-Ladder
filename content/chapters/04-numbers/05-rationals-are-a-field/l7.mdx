$$
\mathbb{Q} = \left\{ \frac pq \;\middle|\; p,q \in \mathbb{Z},\ q \neq 0 \right\},
\qquad
\frac{p_1}{q_1} = \frac{p_2}{q_2} \iff p_1q_2 = p_2q_1.
$$

The equality condition is the first thing to take seriously: elements of $\mathbb{Q}$ have infinitely many names, so every operation defined on representatives carries a well-definedness obligation (§3.1).

> **Lemma.** Addition is well defined on $\mathbb{Q}$.

<Proof>
Suppose $\tfrac{a_1}{b_1} = \tfrac{a_2}{b_2}$, i.e. $a_1b_2 = a_2b_1$. We must show $\tfrac{a_1d + b_1c}{b_1d} = \tfrac{a_2d+b_2c}{b_2d}$, i.e.
$$(a_1d+b_1c)(b_2d) = (a_2d+b_2c)(b_1d).$$
Expanding, the left is $a_1b_2d^2 + b_1b_2cd$ and the right is $a_2b_1d^2 + b_1b_2cd$. These agree precisely because $a_1b_2 = a_2b_1$.
</Proof>

Formally, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the quotient of $\mathbb{Z}\times(\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\})$ by the equivalence relation $(a,b)\sim(c,d) \iff ad=bc$, with the operations induced from representatives. Every construction on a quotient carries this obligation, and the argument above is the model for all of them.

## The field axioms, verified

For $\tfrac ab, \tfrac cd \in \mathbb{Q}$:

$$
\frac ab + \frac cd = \frac{ad+bc}{bd}, \quad
-\frac ab = \frac{-a}{b}, \quad
\frac ab \cdot \frac cd = \frac{ac}{bd}, \quad
\left(\frac ab\right)^{-1} = \frac ba \ (a \neq 0).
$$

Each closure verification has two steps: numerator and denominator are integers by closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ (§4.1), and the denominator is nonzero because **$\mathbb{Z}$ has no zero divisors**. That second point is where the absence of zero divisors in $\mathbb{Z}$ (§4.1) is spent, and it is why the construction below requires a domain rather than an arbitrary ring.

For the inverse: $\tfrac ab \neq 0$ iff $a \neq 0$, so $\tfrac ba \in \mathbb{Q}$ and $\tfrac ab \cdot \tfrac ba = \tfrac{ab}{ab} = 1$.

Hence $\mathbb{Q}$ is a **field**: a commutative ring with $1 \neq 0$ in which every nonzero element is a unit.

<Warning title="0⁻¹ cannot exist">
If $0x = 1$ in a ring, then $0 = 0x = 1$ (using $0x = 0$, itself a theorem from distributivity, §4.1), and then $y = 1\cdot y = 0 \cdot y = 0$ for every $y$ — the ring is the zero ring. The axiom $1 \neq 0$ excludes this, so no field has $0^{-1}$.

"You cannot divide by zero" is therefore a theorem, not a prohibition.
</Warning>

## The same construction, in general

Nothing above used anything about $\mathbb{Z}$ except that it has no zero divisors (§4.1).

> **Theorem.** Any system with the ring properties and no zero divisors sits inside a field of quotients built exactly this way — pairs, with $(a,b)\sim(c,d)$ when $ad=bc$ — and that field is the smallest one containing it.

Taking $D = \mathbb{Z}$ gives $\mathbb{Q}$. Taking $D = k[x]$ gives the rational function field $k(x)$. Taking $D = \mathcal{O}(U)$, holomorphic functions on a domain, gives the meromorphic functions.

The no-zero-divisors hypothesis is necessary, and the proof above shows why: working with remainders mod $6$, one would want $\tfrac13$ to exist, but $3 \times 2 = 0$ there, and the construction degenerates.

The universal property in the second sentence is the useful part. It says $\mathbb{Q}$ is characterised by what it does, not by how it was built — the same shift that §2.9 flagged for ordered pairs.

## Why the closure proofs look like fraction arithmetic

Each verification above was the fraction rule you learned in primary school, checked against a definition rather than asserted. That is worth pausing on, because it is the first time in the course that a familiar procedure has been *justified* rather than used.

$$\frac ab + \frac cd = \frac{ad+bc}{bd}$$

is a definition, not a discovery — and what needs proving is that it is **well defined**: that replacing $\frac ab$ by an equivalent fraction $\frac{a'}{b'}$ gives an equivalent answer. Without that, the operation would depend on which representative you happened to write, and "the sum of two rationals" would not name a single number.

The check is a computation: from $ab' = a'b$ and $cd'=c'd$, verify $(ad+bc)(b'd') = (a'd'+b'c')(bd)$. It works, and it is the reason the rule is legitimate.

**This is the first well-definedness obligation in the course**, and it will not be the last. §7.2 shows that $\mathbb{Q}$ is built as a set of equivalence classes, and every operation defined on classes carries exactly this obligation — one that §3.1 flagged in the abstract and that here has real content.

## Ordered fields, and what is still missing

$\mathbb{Q}$ carries a compatible order ($a < b \Rightarrow a+c < b+c$; $a<b$, $c>0 \Rightarrow ac<bc$), making it an **ordered field**. §4.6 will prove it is dense in itself.

But $\mathbb{Q}$ is not **complete**: the set $\{x \in \mathbb{Q} : x^2 < 2\}$ is bounded above and has no least upper bound in $\mathbb{Q}$, because $\sqrt2 \notin \mathbb{Q}$ (§4.7). $\mathbb{R}$ is defined to be the completion, and it is the unique complete ordered field up to isomorphism — a characterisation strong enough that calculus is really a subject about that one axiom.

<Aside>
The **characteristic** of a field is the least $n > 0$ with $\underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{n} = 0$, or $0$ if no such $n$ exists. $\mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}, \mathbb{C}$ have characteristic $0$; $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ has characteristic $p$. Every field of characteristic $0$ contains a copy of $\mathbb{Q}$, and every field of characteristic $p$ contains a copy of $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ — these are the *prime fields*, and $\mathbb{Q}$'s role as one of exactly two kinds of minimal field is why it is unavoidable.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
**Over $\mathbb{Z}$ none of that works.** The system $2x = 1$ has no integer solution, so "solve and back-substitute" fails immediately, and none of the usual structure survives over $\mathbb{Z}$ resembling the one over a field.

**In calculus, the ordered-field axioms are what inequality manipulation rests on.** Every $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ proof multiplies and divides inequalities, and the rules — adding preserves, multiplying by a positive preserves, multiplying by a negative reverses — are the order axioms of this module. Getting the last one wrong is the standard error in a limit proof, and it is an axiom violation rather than an algebra slip.

**Completeness is the property $\mathbb{Q}$ still lacks**, and it is the one calculus cannot do without. The Extreme Value Theorem, the Intermediate Value Theorem, and the Mean Value Theorem are all false over $\mathbb{Q}$: $f(x)=x^2-2$ changes sign on $[0,2]$ and has no rational root, so IVT fails. §4.7 exhibits the gap, and filling it is what $\mathbb{R}$ is for — which makes completeness, not the field axioms, the defining property of the number system calculus runs on.

</WhereThisGoes>
