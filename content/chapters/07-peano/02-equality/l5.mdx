The first four of the nine axioms are about the equals sign. That seems like a strange place to spend four axioms, until you notice you have been using unstated rules about $=$ in every proof so far.

## The four axioms

For all $a, b, c$:

> **A1 (reflexive).** $a = a$.
>
> **A2 (symmetric).** If $a = b$ then $b = a$.
>
> **A3 (transitive).** If $a = b$ and $b = c$ then $a = c$.
>
> **A4 (substitution).** If $a = b$, then $a$ may replace $b$ anywhere.

A3 is what licenses a chain of equalities. Every time you wrote
$$
x = \text{something} = \text{something else} = y
$$
and concluded $x = y$, you used transitivity — repeatedly, and without mentioning it.

A4 is what licenses substitution, which is most of algebra. It is also the axiom that makes $=$ more than a bare relation: it says equal things are interchangeable *for every purpose*.

## Relations with the first three properties

A relation with all of A1–A3 is an **equivalence relation**. It behaves like equality without necessarily *being* equality — it says "these are the same **for present purposes**."

Two examples you know.

**Congruence of figures.** Your geometry course states these as properties of $\cong$:

| Property | Geometry version |
|---|---|
| reflexive | $F \cong F$ |
| symmetric | $F \cong G \Rightarrow G \cong F$ |
| transitive | $F \cong G$ and $G \cong H \Rightarrow F \cong H$ |

Congruent triangles are not the *same* triangle — they can sit in different places. They are interchangeable for every question about side lengths and angles, and that is what the three properties record.

**Same remainder on division by $n$.** Say $a \equiv b \pmod n$ — read *"$a$ and $b$ leave the same remainder on division by $n$"* — when $n \mid (a - b)$. The notation is standard in later courses; the idea is long division.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$a \equiv b \pmod n$ unfolds to $n \mid (a-b)$, which unfolds again to $a - b = nk$ for some integer $k$ (module 4.2).</div>
  <div slot="want">All three properties.</div>
  <div slot="how">Each is a divisibility statement, so each is proved by producing the integer witness. Three short proofs.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**Reflexive.** $a - a = 0 = n\cdot0$, so $n \mid (a-a)$ ✓

**Symmetric.** Suppose $a \equiv b$, so $a - b = nk$ for some $k \in \mathbb{Z}$. Then $b - a = n(-k)$, and $-k \in \mathbb{Z}$ by closure ✓

**Transitive.** Suppose $a - b = nk$ and $b - c = nm$ with $k, m \in \mathbb{Z}$. Adding,
$$
a - c = (a-b) + (b-c) = nk + nm = n(k+m),
$$
and $k+m \in \mathbb{Z}$ by closure ✓
</Proof>

**The divisibility template again** — unpack, compute, factor, cite closure. Three times, from module 4.3, with nothing new required.

## What an equivalence relation does

> An equivalence relation **partitions** its set: it splits everything into families, where each element belongs to exactly one family, and two elements are related exactly when they share a family.

For congruence mod $3$, the families are the remainder classes of module 4.4:
$$
\{\ldots,-3,0,3,6,\ldots\}, \quad \{\ldots,-2,1,4,7,\ldots\}, \quad \{\ldots,-1,2,5,8,\ldots\}.
$$

Every integer is in exactly one — which is precisely what the division algorithm guaranteed, and it is why proof by cases on remainders is exhaustive. **Module 4.4's technique was an equivalence relation all along.**

<Warning title="Not every relation is an equivalence relation">
Check all three before using the word, because most relations fail at least one.

$\leq$ on $\mathbb{R}$: reflexive ✓, transitive ✓, symmetric ✗ — $2 \leq 3$ but $3 \not\leq 2$.

$\subseteq$ on sets: same pattern, and for the same reason (module 2.3).

$\neq$: symmetric ✓, but not reflexive, since $a \neq a$ is false. Nor transitive: $1 \neq 2$ and $2 \neq 1$, yet $1 = 1$.

*Is a sibling of*: symmetric ✓, transitive ✓ (roughly), reflexive ✗ — you are not your own sibling.

**Three properties, checked separately.** Two out of three is common and is not enough.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Equality itself is the strictest equivalence relation: it puts every object in a group by itself, so nothing is interchangeable with anything but itself.

Every other equivalence relation is a deliberate loosening. Choosing one is choosing **what to ignore.**

Congruence mod $12$ ignores everything about a number except its clock position, which is why $15$ o'clock is $3$ o'clock. Congruence of figures ignores position and keeps shape and size. Fractions are an equivalence relation on pairs of integers that ignores the choice of representative, which is why $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac24$ are the same number — and why module 3.2's rule $p/q \mapsto p+q$ failed to be a function.

**Deciding what to ignore is one of the most productive moves in mathematics**, and each such decision is an equivalence relation.
</Aside>
