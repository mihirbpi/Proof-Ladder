The words **same as** obey three rules. You have used them your whole life without noticing.

**Rule 1.** Everything is the same as itself.

**Rule 2.** If this is the same as that, then that is the same as this.

**Rule 3.** If this matches that, and that matches the other, then this matches the other.

## Try them on some real matchings

**Same age as.** Does rule 1 hold? You are the same age as yourself. ✓ Rule 2? If you are the same age as Sam, Sam is the same age as you. ✓ Rule 3? ✓

All three. Good.

**Same color as.** All three again. ✓

<Kids kids={["Sam", "Pip", "Ada"]} caption="matching works all three ways" />

**Lives in the same house as.** All three. ✓

## One that fails

Now try **is a friend of**.

Rule 1: are you a friend of yourself? Sort of, but let's say yes.

Rule 2: if you are Sam's friend, is Sam your friend? Usually! ✓

Rule 3: you are friends with Sam. Sam is friends with Pip. Are **you** friends with Pip?

Maybe not! You might never have met Pip.

**Rule 3 fails.** So "is a friend of" is not a *same as* kind of relation, even though it feels a bit like one.

<Warning title="All three, or it does not count">
A matching has to pass all three rules to be a proper *same as*.

Friendship passes two and fails one. That is enough to disqualify it — and it is why you cannot reason about friendship the way you reason about equality.
</Warning>

## Why grown-ups write these down

When you build mathematics from the very bottom (module 7.1), you cannot leave *anything* unsaid — including what the equals sign means.

So these three rules go on the list of things we agree to start with. They look almost too obvious to write. That is exactly why they are easy to forget, and why the list would be incomplete without them.

<Aside>
Here is one you might not expect. Take numbers and say two are the same if they leave the same amount over when shared among $3$.

So $4$ and $7$ are "the same" (both leave $1$ over). And $5$ and $8$ are the same (both leave $2$).

Check the three rules. They all hold! So this is a proper *same as*, even though the numbers are genuinely different. Sometimes it is useful to treat different things as the same, and the three rules tell you when you are allowed to.
</Aside>
