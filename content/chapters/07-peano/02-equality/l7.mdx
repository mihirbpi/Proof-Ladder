## Axioms 1–4

For all $x,y,z$:
$$
x = x; \qquad x=y\Rightarrow y=x; \qquad (x=y\wedge y=z)\Rightarrow x=z;
$$
$$
(x \in \mathbb{N}\wedge x=y)\Rightarrow y\in\mathbb{N}.
$$

The fourth is a special case of **substitution**: if $x = y$ then any statement true of $x$ is true of $y$. Peano isolates the instance he needs; a modern treatment states substitution in full, or — more commonly — declines to axiomatise equality at all, treating it as a logical symbol with fixed interpretation. On that view A1–A4 belong to the underlying logic rather than to arithmetic, and many presentations of Peano arithmetic omit them for exactly that reason.

## Equivalence relations

A relation $\sim\ \subseteq S\times S$ (§2.9) satisfying A1–A3 is an **equivalence relation**.

Dropping symmetry and adding antisymmetry ($a\sim b \wedge b \sim a \Rightarrow a = b$) gives a **partial order** instead — $\leq$ on $\mathbb{R}$, $\subseteq$ on $\mathcal{P}(X)$ (§2.3), $\mid$ on $\mathbb{Z}_{>0}$ (§4.2). The two families of structures are the two natural ways to weaken equality, and almost every relation of interest is one or the other.

> **Proposition.** $a \equiv b \pmod n :\iff n \mid (a-b)$ is an equivalence relation on $\mathbb{Z}$.

<Proof>
Reflexive: $n \mid 0 = a-a$. Symmetric: $a-b = nk \Rightarrow b-a = n(-k)$. Transitive: $a-b = nj$, $b-c=nk$ give $a - c = n(j+k)$, with $j+k \in \mathbb{Z}$ by closure (§4.1).
</Proof>

## The fundamental theorem

> **Theorem.** The equivalence classes $[a] = \{x \in S : x\sim a\}$ partition $S$. Conversely, every partition of $S$ arises from a unique equivalence relation.

<Proof>
*Classes cover $S$.* $a \in [a]$ by reflexivity.

*Classes are equal or disjoint.* If $c \in [a]\cap[b]$ then $c\sim a$ and $c\sim b$. For $x \in [a]$: $x \sim a$, and $a \sim c$ by symmetry, so $x \sim c$ by transitivity, so $x \sim b$ by transitivity again. Hence $[a]\subseteq[b]$, and symmetrically $[b]\subseteq[a]$; so $[a]=[b]$ by double inclusion (§2.6).

*Converse.* Given a partition $\mathcal{Q}$, define $x \sim y$ iff $x$ and $y$ lie in the same block. Reflexivity holds since blocks cover $S$; symmetry is immediate; transitivity holds since blocks are disjoint. Its classes are exactly the blocks.
</Proof>

Each of the three axioms was used exactly once, which is a compact demonstration of their independence in practice.

The set of classes is the **quotient** $S/{\sim}$, and $\pi : S \to S/{\sim}$, $\pi(a)=[a]$, is the canonical surjection. Note $\pi$ is surjective by construction and injective iff $\sim$ is equality — so quotienting is precisely the operation of deliberately forgetting distinctions.

<Warning title="Well-definedness on quotients">
An operation on classes must be checked independent of representatives (§3.1).

**Works:** $[a]+[b] := [a+b]$ on $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. If $a\equiv a'$, $b\equiv b'$ then $n \mid (a-a')+(b-b')= (a+b)-(a'+b')$.

**Fails:** $[a]\mapsto a$ on $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$; $[1]=[4]$ mod $3$ but $1 \neq 4$. No canonical representative — no single standard choice — exists.

**Fails:** $[a]\mapsto 2^a$ on $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ valued in $\mathbb{Z}$ — same objection.

Every construction on a quotient carries this obligation, and it is the most frequently skipped verification in algebra.
</Warning>

## Relations that fail, and what the failures mean

The three conditions are independent, and examining what each rules out is more informative than the definition.

| Relation | Reflexive | Symmetric | Transitive |
|---|---|---|---|
| $=$ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| congruence of figures | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| same remainder mod $n$ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| row equivalence | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| $\leq$ | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| $<$ | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ |
| $\subseteq$ | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| $\perp$ | ✗ | ✓ | ✗ |

**Failure of symmetry is what makes a relation an order.** $\leq$ and $\subseteq$ are reflexive and transitive and not symmetric, and that is precisely the definition of a partial order. Orders and equivalences are the two natural weakenings of equality, distinguished by exactly this condition.

**Failure of transitivity is usually fatal.** Perpendicularity fails it: $\ell_1\perp\ell_2$ and $\ell_2\perp\ell_3$ give $\ell_1 \parallel \ell_3$ in the plane, not $\ell_1\perp\ell_3$. A relation without transitivity supports no chaining, and chaining is what proofs do.

## Quotients build the number systems

$$
\mathbb{Z} = (\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})/\!\sim,\quad (a,b)\sim(c,d)\iff a+d=b+c;
$$
$$
\mathbb{Q} = \big(\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{\neq0}\big)/\!\sim,\quad (p,q)\sim(r,s)\iff ps=qr \quad(\S4.5);
$$
$$
\mathbb{R} = \{\text{Cauchy sequences in }\mathbb{Q}\}/\!\sim,\quad (a_n)\sim(b_n)\iff a_n-b_n\to0;
$$
$$
\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} = \mathbb{Z}/{\equiv_n}.
$$

The pattern is uniform: take known objects, impose the right notion of sameness, and the classes are the new objects. Chapter 7 builds $\mathbb{N}$ from axioms; every system above it is built by quotient.

<Aside>
In intensional type theory, equality is not a primitive relation but a *type* — $\mathrm{Id}_A(x,y)$ — and A1–A3 become constructions rather than axioms. Reflexivity is a term, symmetry and transitivity are functions built from it.

The consequences are strange and productive: identifications can carry information, and homotopy type theory reinterprets $\mathrm{Id}_A(x,y)$ as a path space, so that equality proofs acquire a geometry of their own. Univalence then asserts that identity of types is equivalence of types — making "equal" mean "isomorphic", which is what mathematicians have always meant informally.
</Aside>

Equivalence relations are how mathematics decides what to ignore, and both courses ahead use them constantly.

**In calculus the important one is "differ by a constant."** Two antiderivatives of the same function on an interval differ by a constant — reflexive, symmetric, transitive — and the classes are what $+\,C$ denotes. The indefinite integral names an equivalence class, not a function, and that is why the constant is not optional.
