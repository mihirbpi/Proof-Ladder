Building mathematics from the bottom means writing down *everything* you assume — including what "$=$" means.

## The first four axioms

**Axiom 1 (reflexive).** Everything is equal to itself.

**Axiom 2 (symmetric).** If one thing is equal to a second, then the second is equal to the first.

**Axiom 3 (transitive).** If a first thing is equal to a second, and the second is equal to a third, then the first is equal to the third.

**Axiom 4 (closure).** If something is a counting number, and something else is equal to it, then that something else is a counting number too.

The first three are the famous ones. The fourth says something you would never think to doubt: equal things belong to the same collections. Without it, "equal" numbers could sit in different places, and nothing would work.

## Any relation with the first three

The three properties are not special to $=$. Lots of relations have them, and such a relation is called an **equivalence relation**.

Here are three that qualify.

**Same age as.** Reflexive: you are your own age. Symmetric: if you match Sam's age, Sam matches yours. Transitive: matches chain along. ✓

**Same color as.** All three. ✓

**Leaves the same remainder when divided by 3.** Reflexive: any number leaves the same remainder as itself. Symmetric: obvious. Transitive: if $a$ and $b$ agree, and $b$ and $c$ agree, then $a$ and $c$ agree. ✓

That last one comes straight from module 4.4, and it is far more interesting than it looks.

## One that fails

**Is a friend of.** Reflexive and symmetric are arguable, but transitivity plainly fails: your friend's friend need not be your friend.

So friendship is not an equivalence relation, and you cannot reason about it the way you reason about equality.

<Warning title="All three, or none of it works">
Missing any one property breaks things.

Without **reflexive**, some object is not even equal to itself.

Without **symmetric**, "$a = b$" and "$b = a$" would say different things, and every equation would have a direction.

Without **transitive**, chains like $a = b = c = d$ would tell you nothing about $a$ and $d$ — and almost every calculation you have ever done is such a chain.
</Warning>

## What equivalence relations do

Here is the payoff, and it is the reason mathematicians care.

An equivalence relation **sorts everything into groups**, with each thing in exactly one group.

Take "leaves the same remainder when divided by $3$." Every whole number falls into exactly one of three groups:

- remainder $0$: $\ldots, 0, 3, 6, 9, \ldots$
- remainder $1$: $\ldots, 1, 4, 7, 10, \ldots$
- remainder $2$: $\ldots, 2, 5, 8, 11, \ldots$

No number is in two groups. No number is left out. The relation carved the whole numbers into three neat piles.

That happens for *every* equivalence relation, and it is what makes them useful: they let you deliberately treat different things as interchangeable when only some feature matters.

<Aside>
This is how new number systems get built. Fractions are built by declaring $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac24$ "the same" — an equivalence relation on pairs of whole numbers. Clock arithmetic is built by declaring $1$ o'clock and $13$ o'clock the same.

In each case you start with familiar objects, choose a sensible notion of *same*, and the groups become the new numbers.
</Aside>
