## Primitives and axioms

The language of arithmetic has two undefined starting words: a constant $0$ and a symbol $S$ for an operation taking one number to another.

$$
\textbf{A5}\quad 0 \in \mathbb{N}, \qquad\qquad \textbf{A6}\quad x\in\mathbb{N}\Rightarrow S(x)\in\mathbb{N}.
$$

Neither $0$ nor $S$ is defined; per §7.1 their content is exactly what the axioms permit to be derived. A modern presentation folds A5–A6 into the setup rather than stating them: the objects under discussion are a set with a distinguished point and an endofunction, $(\mathbb{N}, 0, S)$.

## Numerals

$$
1 := S(0),\quad 2:=S(1),\quad 3:=S(2),\quad\ldots
$$

Definitions, not theorems. The **numeral** $\overline{n} = S^n(0)$ is a syntactic object; the number is what it denotes. Decimal notation is a further layer of abbreviation with no mathematical content — a fact that becomes important in logic, where the length of $\overline n$ written as $n$ tally marks, versus the length of the same number written in decimal is the difference between tractable and intractable formalisations.

<Warning title="No addition yet">
$S(x)$ may not be written $x+1$: addition is defined in §7.6 *by recursion on $S$*, so the notation would be circular.

Afterwards, $x + 1 = x + S(0) = S(x + 0) = S(x)$ is a theorem in three steps. The distinction between what is defined and what is derived is the entire subject of this chapter, and collapsing it here forfeits the exercise.
</Warning>

## The structural view

$S : \mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ is a function (§3.1), and the remaining axioms are statements about it in Chapter 3's vocabulary:

| Axiom | Chapter 3 form |
|---|---|
| A7 | $0 \notin \operatorname{Im}(S)$, i.e. $S^{-1}(\{0\}) = \emptyset$ |
| A8 | $S$ is injective |
| A9 | $\mathbb{N}$ is the smallest set containing $0$ and closed under $S$ |

Together: $S$ is an injection $\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$, and $\mathbb{N}$ is generated by $0$ under it. Note that A7 and A8 together say $S$ is a bijection onto $\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$ — so $\mathbb{N}$ is in bijection with a proper subset of itself, which is Dedekind's definition of infinite (§3.8).

## Numerals are names, not numbers

$1 := S(0)$, $2 := S(1)$, $3 := S(2)$. The symbol $:=$ means *is defined to be*, and these are abbreviations — nothing has been assumed about $1$ beyond its being the successor of $0$.

Roman numerals, tally marks, and strings of $0$s and $1$s name the same objects. **None of them is the number**, and the relationship is the one between a point and the letter labelling it in a diagram.

That is why base-ten arithmetic is absent from this chapter. The fact that $7+5=12$ has a $1$ in the tens place is a fact about **notation**, dependent on a choice of base that the axioms know nothing about. Computers use base two and get identical arithmetic, which is the clearest evidence that positional notation is not part of the mathematics.

**The distinction has a practical use.** When an argument seems to depend on how a number is written — its digits, its last digit, its digit sum — it is an argument about the base representation, and it will need the division algorithm (§4.4) to be made rigorous. Divisibility-by-three tests are the standard example, and they are theorems about base ten specifically.

## Insufficiency of A5–A6

$M_1 = \{0\}$ with $S(0)=0$, and $M_2 = \{0,1\}$ with $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=0$, both satisfy A5 and A6 — and both are finite. So **"$\mathbb{N}$ is infinite" cannot be proved from A5 and A6 alone.**

That is the method of §7.1: a structure in which the axioms hold and the conclusion fails settles unprovability outright. It is not merely evidence that nobody has found a proof; it shows no proof exists.

$M_1$ violates A7 ($0$ is a successor). $M_2$ violates A7 as well, and the model $\{0,1\}$ with $S(0)=S(1)=1$ violates A8. §7.4 introduces exactly the axioms these models refute, which is the standard way to demonstrate that no axiom is redundant.

<Aside>
$(\mathbb{N},0,S)$ has a universal property: for any set $A$ with a point $a\in A$ and a map $f: A \to A$, there is a *unique* $h : \mathbb{N}\to A$ with $h(0)=a$ and $h(S(n)) = f(h(n))$.

That single property packages A5–A9 and the recursive-definition principle at once — existence is definition by recursion (§7.6), uniqueness is proof by induction (§7.5). It is a striking repackaging: everything the nine axioms say, in one sentence about a single arrow.
</Aside>

The discipline of this module — **use only what has been stated** — is the one that makes axiomatic subjects tractable.

$S(x)$ cannot be written $x+1$ until addition exists, and addition is defined *from* $S$ (§7.6). Writing it early would be circular, and the circularity would be invisible.

The routine error is importing $\mathbb{R}^n$ intuition into a proof about an abstract space. It is the same error as writing $S(x) = x+1$ here: using a property the axioms have not granted, in a setting where it happens to be true for the example you are picturing.

**Recognising $S$ as a function matters.** Axiom 6 says $S : \mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ is total, single-valued, and codomain-respecting — the three conditions of §3.1 — and the axioms to come describe it in Chapter 3's vocabulary: Axiom 8 is injectivity, Axiom 7 is emptiness of a pre-image.

That is not decoration. **The chapter is comprehensible because the earlier chapters supplied the words**, and an axiom system stated without them would look arbitrary.

**In calculus the same restraint applies to the definition of a limit.** The official definition says what it says; a proof may use only that, not the picture of a graph sidling up to a height. When a limit proof goes wrong, it is usually because a step appealed to the picture rather than the definition.
