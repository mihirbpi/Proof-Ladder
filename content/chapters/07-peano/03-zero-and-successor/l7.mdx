## Primitives and axioms

The language of arithmetic has two non-logical primitives: a constant $0$ and a unary function symbol $S$.

$$
\textbf{A5}\quad 0 \in \mathbb{N}, \qquad\qquad \textbf{A6}\quad x\in\mathbb{N}\Rightarrow S(x)\in\mathbb{N}.
$$

Neither $0$ nor $S$ is defined; per §7.1 their content is exactly what the axioms permit to be derived. In a modern presentation, $\mathbb{N}$ is the universe of discourse and A5–A6 are absorbed into the signature — the structure is a set with a distinguished point and an endofunction, $(\mathbb{N}, 0, S)$.

## Numerals

$$
1 := S(0),\quad 2:=S(1),\quad 3:=S(2),\quad\ldots
$$

Definitions, not theorems. The **numeral** $\overline{n} = S^n(0)$ is a syntactic object; the number is what it denotes. Decimal notation is a further layer of abbreviation with no mathematical content — a fact that becomes important in logic, where the length of $\overline n$ (unary, hence linear in $n$) versus its decimal representation (logarithmic) is the difference between tractable and intractable formalisations.

<Warning title="No addition yet">
$S(x)$ may not be written $x+1$: addition is defined in §7.6 *by recursion on $S$*, so the notation would be circular.

Afterwards, $x + 1 = x + S(0) = S(x + 0) = S(x)$ is a theorem in three steps. The distinction between what is stipulated and what is derived is the entire subject of this chapter, and collapsing it here forfeits the exercise.
</Warning>

## The structural view

$S : \mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ is a function (§3.1), and the remaining axioms are statements about it in Chapter 3's vocabulary:

| Axiom | Chapter 3 form |
|---|---|
| A7 | $0 \notin \operatorname{Im}(S)$, i.e. $S^{-1}(\{0\}) = \emptyset$ |
| A8 | $S$ is injective |
| A9 | $\mathbb{N}$ is the smallest set containing $0$ and closed under $S$ |

Together: $S$ is an injection $\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$, and $\mathbb{N}$ is generated by $0$ under it. Note that A7 and A8 together say $S$ is a bijection onto $\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$ — so $\mathbb{N}$ is in bijection with a proper subset of itself, which is Dedekind's definition of infinite (§3.8).

## Insufficiency of A5–A6

$M_1 = \{0\}$ with $S(0)=0$, and $M_2 = \{0,1\}$ with $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=0$, both satisfy A5 and A6. So
$$
\{A5, A6\} \not\vdash \text{“}\mathbb{N}\text{ is infinite”},
$$
by the model-theoretic method of §7.1: a structure satisfying the axioms and refuting the conclusion witnesses unprovability, and by Gödel completeness this is not merely evidence but proof.

$M_1$ violates A7 ($0$ is a successor). $M_2$ violates A7 as well, and the model $\{0,1\}$ with $S(0)=S(1)=1$ violates A8. §7.4 introduces exactly the axioms these models refute, which is the standard way to demonstrate that no axiom is redundant.

<Aside>
Categorically, $(\mathbb{N},0,S)$ is the **initial algebra** for the functor $F(X) = 1 + X$ on **Set**: initiality says that for any set $A$ with a point $a\in A$ and a map $f: A \to A$, there is a *unique* $h : \mathbb{N}\to A$ with $h(0)=a$ and $h(S(n)) = f(h(n))$.

That single property packages A5–A9 and the recursion theorem at once — existence of $h$ is definition by recursion (§7.6), uniqueness is proof by induction (§7.5). Lawvere's 1964 observation that this characterises $\mathbb{N}$ is why "natural numbers object" is a standard notion in category theory and in the semantics of programming languages.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
The pattern "one constructor for a base case, one for a step" is the definition of an **inductive type**, and it is how $\mathbb{N}$ is declared in every proof assistant: `Inductive nat := O | S (n : nat)`. From that declaration the system mechanically generates the recursor and the induction principle, so §7.5 and §7.6 are not separate work but consequences of the type's shape.

Which functions can be defined by such recursion is a substantive question. Primitive recursion — recursion on the immediate predecessor — gives the primitive recursive functions, a large class that nonetheless omits Ackermann's function, whose definition requires nested recursion. Extending the schema stratifies the computable functions into a hierarchy that classifies exactly how much recursion a definition needs, and the same hierarchy measures the strength of the arithmetics that can prove those functions total.
</WhereThisGoes>
