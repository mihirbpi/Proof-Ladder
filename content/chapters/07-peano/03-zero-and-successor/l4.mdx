## The words we start from

Two undefined terms (module 7.1):

- a constant $0$;
- an operation $S$ taking one number to another, the **successor**.

Their meaning is fixed entirely by the axioms below. Nothing else about them may be assumed.

## Axioms 5 and 6

$$
\textbf{A5}\quad 0 \in \mathbb{N}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers, $\in$ is "is in". So the line reads: zero is a counting number, and the next number after a counting number is again one.*

$$
\textbf{A6}\quad x \in \mathbb{N} \Rightarrow S(x)\in\mathbb{N}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers, $\in$ is "is in", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces". So the line reads: if $x$ is a counting number, then so is the next one after it.*

A5 supplies a starting point; A6 supplies closure under $S$. Together they generate
$$
0,\ S(0),\ S(S(0)),\ S(S(S(0))),\ \ldots
$$

## Numerals are definitions

$$
1 := S(0),\quad 2:= S(1) = S(S(0)),\quad 3 := S(2),\quad \ldots
$$

These are *a matter of definition definitions*, i.e. abbreviations. The object denoted by $3$ is $S(S(S(0)))$, and the decimal numeral is a naming convention with no mathematical content — base ten, Arabic digits, and the words *one, two, three* are all human choices layered on top.

What is mathematically real is the structure: a distinguished element and an operation.

<Warning title="S(x) may not be written x + 1">
Addition does not exist yet. It is *defined* in module 7.6 by recursion on $S$, so using it here would be circular.

After that definition, $x + 1 = x + S(0) = S(x+0) = S(x)$ becomes a two-line **theorem**. It is neither an axiom nor a definition, and deriving it is one of the small satisfactions of this chapter.

The discipline matters because the entire point is to see how little is needed at the bottom. Importing addition early conceals exactly what we came to examine.
</Warning>

## $S$ as a function

$S : \mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ is a function in the sense of module 3.1:

| Condition | Source |
|---|---|
| totality — every $x \in \mathbb{N}$ has a successor | A6 |
| well-definedness — exactly one successor each | implicit in $S$ being an operation |
| codomain — $S(x) \in \mathbb{N}$ | A6 |

Later axioms constrain it further in Chapter 3's vocabulary: A7 will say $0 \notin \operatorname{Im}(S)$, and A8 will say $S$ is injective (module 3.5). Chapter 3 is not merely a prerequisite here; it is the language the axioms are best stated in.

## Why build the numbers this way at all

It is fair to ask what the point is. You have counted since you were four, and nobody was confused. Why replace something that works with two undefined words and a list of rules?

Three reasons, and none of them is that counting was broken.

**To make the assumptions visible.** Chapters 1 to 6 used arithmetic constantly — that addition can be done in either order, that every number is even or odd. Those were taken on trust. Building the numbers from a stated list turns each of them into something you can point at and trace, and shows exactly how much has to be assumed before the rest follows.

**To find out what depends on what.** Once the rules are separated, you can ask which results need which rule. Module 7.4 shows that dropping a single axiom leaves you with clock arithmetic — a perfectly consistent system that simply is not the counting numbers. You cannot ask that question at all until the rules are apart.

## The method travels

**The same move works elsewhere.** Every system in mathematics gets built this way in the end: name the undefined words, state the rules, derive the rest. Doing it once on something you already know is far easier than meeting the method for the first time on something you do not.

## What the two axioms leave open

Notice how little A5 and A6 actually say. They say $0$ is there, and that every number has a next one. That is all.

They do not say the next one is *new*. Nothing yet stops $S(0)$ from being $0$ itself, or the counting from looping back on itself after a while. They do not say two different numbers have different next ones. And they do not say that everything in $\mathbb{N}$ is reachable by counting from $0$ — there could be extra numbers sitting off to one side, never reached.

Each of those holes gets plugged by a later axiom, and the next module builds the broken systems that show why each is needed.

## Why A5–A6 are insufficient

Take $\mathbb{N} = \{0\}$ with $S(0) = 0$. Then A5 holds and A6 holds — and the "counting numbers" have one element.

Or take $\mathbb{N} = \{0,1\}$ with $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=0$, as drawn. Both axioms hold again, and counting cycles $0,1,0,1,\ldots$

Both are legitimate **models** of A5–A6 (module 7.1), so those axioms cannot prove $\mathbb{N}$ is infinite. Two more axioms are needed, and module 7.4 supplies exactly the two that kill exactly these two pathologies:

- $S$ never returns to $0$ — no looping;
- $S$ is injective (§3.5) — no merging.

Constructing bad models to show which axioms are missing is the standard way to see that an axiom system is doing real work. It is also how independence is proved.

<Aside>
Whether $\mathbb{N}$ starts at $0$ or $1$ is a convention (module 2.1). Starting at $0$ makes the successor structure cleanest: one constant, one operation, and $0$ is the additive identity that module 7.6's recursion needs as its base case.

Peano's own 1889 formulation started at $1$; he switched to $0$ later. Nothing mathematical turns on it, and everything about the *statements* of theorems does — which is why the convention must be stated.
</Aside>
