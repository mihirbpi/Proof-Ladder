$$
\textbf{(A1)}\ a+0 = a, \qquad \textbf{(A2)}\ a + S(b) = S(a+b).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $S(b)$ is the next number after $b$, and the bold labels are names for the two rules so later proofs can point at them. So the line reads: (A1) adding zero changes nothing, and (A2) adding the next number after $b$ gives the next number after $a+b$.*

Recursion on the **second** argument, with the first held fixed. Every asymmetry below traces to that choice.

## Is a recursion allowed to define anything?

Writing down two rules does not by itself create a function. Something has to guarantee that exactly one function obeys them — no fewer, no more.

Something does. There is a theorem, proved by induction from Axiom 9, saying that a base value plus a rule for getting from each case to the next always pins down exactly one function on $\mathbb{N}$. Uniqueness is the easy half: if two functions obeyed the rules, the set where they agree contains $0$ and is closed under *next*, so by Axiom 9 it is everything.

So the ninth axiom does double duty. It licenses proof by induction, and it licenses definition by recursion — the two halves of §6.4's observation that recursion and induction are one structure.

## Derivations

<Proof>
$1+1 = 1+S(0) = S(1+0) = S(1) = 2$.

$2+3 = 2+S(2) = S(2+2) = S(S(2+1)) = S(S(S(2+0))) = 5$.
</Proof>

> **Proposition.** $a+1 = S(a)$.

<Proof>
$a+1 = a+S(0) = S(a+0) = S(a)$.
</Proof>

This is the theorem §7.3 promised. Writing $a+1$ for $S(a)$ — forbidden until addition existed — is now licensed.

## The surprise: $0 + a = a$ has to be proved

Rule (A1) gives $a + 0 = a$: zero on the **right**. Zero on the **left** is a different statement, and no rule produces it. The recursion peels the second slot, so it has nothing to say about the first.

> **Lemma 1.** $0 + a = a$ for every $a$.

<Proof>

*The symbols in this proof: $S(k)$ is the next number after $k$. In words, what follows shows: adding zero on the left does nothing — checked at $0$, then passed up the chain.*

*Base.* $0+0 = 0$, by (A1) with $a = 0$.

*Step.* Assume $0+k = k$. Then
$$
0 + S(k) \;=\; S(0+k) \;=\; S(k),
$$
the first equality by (A2), the second by the assumption. That is the claim at $S(k)$, so Axiom 9 gives it for every $a$.
</Proof>

**Four lines to prove that adding zero on the left does nothing.** That it needs proving at all is the point: **the definition is asymmetric**, so every symmetric-looking fact costs its own argument.

## Commutativity, worked

This is the one worth doing in full, because it is the fact everyone assumes and nobody proves.

It needs one more lemma, of the same character as the first: the definition gives $a + S(b) = S(a+b)$, and the mirror image is a separate induction on $b$.

> **Lemma 2.** $S(a)+b = S(a+b)$.

> **Theorem.** $a+b = b+a$ for all $a$ and $b$.

<Proof>

*The symbols in this proof: $S(k)$ is the next number after $k$. In words, what follows shows: the two orders agree at $b = 0$, and agreeing at $k$ forces agreeing at the next one.*

Induct on $b$, holding $a$ fixed.

*Base.* $a + 0 = a$ by (A1), and $0 + a = a$ by Lemma 1. So both orders give $a$.

*Step.* Assume $a + k = k + a$. Then
$$
a + S(k) \;=\; S(a+k) \;=\; S(k+a) \;=\; S(k) + a,
$$
the first equality by (A2), the second by the assumption, the third by Lemma 2 read right to left. That is the claim at $S(k)$.

By Axiom 9, it holds for every $b$. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

**Three inductions for one familiar fact** — Lemma 1, Lemma 2, and the theorem itself.

## The rest of the arithmetic

The same method delivers the remaining laws, each by an induction on one slot with the others held fixed:

- **Associativity**, $(a+b)+c = a+(b+c)$ — induct on $c$; the step is (A2) applied twice with the hypothesis in between.
- **Cancellation**, $a + c = b + c \Rightarrow a = b$ — induct on $c$; the step strips an $S$ from both sides, which is legal exactly because **A8** says $S$ never sends two numbers to the same place.

Cancellation is where A8 is finally spent, and with it every axiom has now done a job.

<Warning title="Familiarity is not derivation">
Nothing in (A1)–(A2) gives $a+b=b+a$; the definition is plainly asymmetric. Three inductions were required.

The general discipline: when a subject is built from a stated list of rules, the question is never whether a statement is obvious but whether it has been derived. Chapters 1–6 used commutativity freely, and were — strictly — incomplete until this module.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Had addition been defined by recursion on the first argument, Lemma 1 would be immediate and (A1) would need proof. The asymmetry lives entirely in the definition, and commutativity is exactly the theorem that the operation does not inherit it.

Chapter 4's assumptions are now discharged for $\mathbb{N}$: closure under $+$ is (A1)–(A2), associativity and commutativity are above, and the distributive law follows in §7.7. The arithmetic rules of §4.1 are theorems here.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l8">

Defining a thing by saying what it does at the first step and how each step follows from the last is a pattern you will use constantly, and the guarantee that it terminates is always the same one.

**Sequences given by a rule from the previous term are everywhere.** Start at $1$ and repeatedly replace $a$ by $\tfrac12(a + \tfrac2a)$ and the numbers close in on $\sqrt2$ fast — that is how a calculator does it. Every such definition is legitimate for the reason this module gives: each step reaches back to a smaller index, and the counting numbers cannot descend forever.

**Proofs about them are inductions**, and the proof mirrors the definition exactly. To show the terms stay below some bound, you show the first one does and that each step preserves it.

**The commutativity result is the module's real lesson.** The definition builds on the second slot only, so $x + y = y + x$ is not free — it is a theorem, and it takes a proof by induction. A fact this obvious needing an argument is the point: *obvious* and *proved from the definition* are different things.

**Order was defined, not assumed.** Saying $x\leq y$ means *you can get from $x$ to $y$ by adding something*, and every property of $\leq$ follows from that. Getting the ordering free from the arithmetic is one of the tidier surprises here.

</WhereThisGoes>
