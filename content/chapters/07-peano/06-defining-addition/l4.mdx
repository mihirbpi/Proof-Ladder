## The recursion

$$
\textbf{(A1)}\ \ a + 0 = a, \qquad\qquad \textbf{(A2)}\ \ a + S(b) = S(a+b).
$$

A **recursive definition**: the base case (A1) is outright, and (A2) reduces $a + S(b)$ to $a+b$, a strictly simpler instance. Termination is guaranteed because the second argument descends to $0$ and A7 forbids going below.

Recursion is on the **second** argument; the first is a spectator. That asymmetry is the source of everything interesting below.

## Derivations

<Proof>
$1+1$: using $1 = S(0)$ and $2 = S(1)$,
$$
1 + 1 = 1 + S(0) \overset{\text{(A2)}}{=} S(1+0) \overset{\text{(A1)}}{=} S(1) = 2.
$$

$2+3$: unwind, then rebuild.
$$
2+3 = 2+S(2) = S(2+2) = S(S(2+1)) = S(S(S(2+0))) = S(S(S(2))) = S(S(3)) = S(4) = 5.
$$
</Proof>

Every closed sum reduces to a stack of successors over $0$ in finitely many steps.

## $a + 1 = S(a)$

> **Proposition.** $a + 1 = S(a)$ for every $a \in \mathbb{N}$.

<Proof>
$a + 1 = a + S(0) \overset{\text{(A2)}}{=} S(a+0) \overset{\text{(A1)}}{=} S(a)$.
</Proof>

Module 7.3 forbade writing $S(a)$ as $a+1$; this is the theorem that finally licenses it. Two lines, no induction — but it had to wait until addition existed.

## Commutativity is not free

$$
a + b = b + a
$$
is **not** an axiom and does not follow from (A1)–(A2) just by looking: the definition treats the two arguments asymmetrically. It requires induction, and two lemmas first.

> **Lemma 1.** $0 + a = a$ for all $a$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">(A1) gives $a + 0 = a$ directly. It does **not** give $0 + a = a$, because the recursion peels the second argument, and here $a$ is second.</div>
  <div slot="want">$0 + a = a$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Induct on $a$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
*Base.* $0 + 0 = 0$ by (A1).

*Step.* Assume $0 + k = k$. Then
$$0 + S(k) \overset{\text{(A2)}}{=} S(0+k) = S(k).$$
</Proof>

> **Lemma 2.** $S(a) + b = S(a+b)$ for all $a,b$.

<Proof>
Induct on $b$.

*Base.* $S(a)+0 = S(a) = S(a+0)$, both by (A1).

*Step.* Assume $S(a)+k = S(a+k)$. Then
$$
S(a)+S(k) \overset{\text{(A2)}}{=} S\big(S(a)+k\big) = S\big(S(a+k)\big) \overset{\text{(A2)}}{=} S\big(a+S(k)\big).
$$
</Proof>

> **Theorem.** $a + b = b + a$.

<Proof>
Induct on $b$, with $a$ named at the start and never changed (§1.12) — and with nothing assumed about it.

*Base.* $a + 0 = a$ by (A1), and $0 + a = a$ by Lemma 1. Equal.

*Step.* Assume $a + k = k + a$. Then
$$
a + S(k) \overset{\text{(A2)}}{=} S(a+k) = S(k+a) \overset{\text{Lem 2}}{=} S(k)+a.
$$
</Proof>

Three inductions for a fact you have used since childhood. Associativity $(a+b)+c = a+(b+c)$ is a fourth, by induction on $c$ — and it is the easiest of them, since the recursion already peels the last argument.

<Warning title="Familiarity is not justification">
This is the central lesson of Chapter 7. $a+b = b+a$ *feels* self-evident because it has been used ten thousand times, but nothing in (A1)–(A2) delivers it, and the derivation takes real work.

The discipline generalizes: when everything has to come from a stated list of rules, the test is not whether a statement feels true but whether it has been derived. Every proof in Chapters 1–6 that silently used commutativity was, strictly, incomplete until now.
</Warning>

<Aside>
That a definition-by-steps like this really does pin down one and only one operation is itself something that has to be proved, and the proof uses A9. So even the right to *write down* (A1)–(A2) rests on induction, and almost everything in this chapter traces back to that one axiom.

Note also the choice of recursion variable is what breaks the symmetry. Had addition been defined by recursion on the first argument, Lemma 1 would be immediate and (A1) would need proof. The asymmetry is in the *definition*, never in the operation.
</Aside>
