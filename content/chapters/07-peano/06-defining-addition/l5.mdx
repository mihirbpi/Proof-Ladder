Nine axioms, and none of them mentions $+$. Addition is not given — it has to be **built**, from $0$ and the successor and nothing else.

## The definition

> $$ a + 0 = a $$
> $$ a + S(b) = S(a + b) $$

Two lines. The first says adding nothing changes nothing. The second says **adding the next number gives the next answer.**

This is a **recursive definition**: it defines $a + b$ in terms of $a + (\text{something smaller})$, with a base case to stop at. Module 6.4 listed recursive definitions as one of the natural homes for induction, and this is why the chapter ends here.

## Watch it compute

Every sum unwinds to the base case. Take $2 + 2$, remembering that $2$ means $S(S(0))$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
2 + S(S(0)) &= S\big(2 + S(0)\big) && \text{second rule}\\
&= S\big(S(2 + 0)\big) && \text{second rule again}\\
&= S\big(S(2)\big) && \text{first rule}\\
&= S(3) = 4.
\end{aligned}
$$

**$2+2=4$, proved.** Not assumed, not remembered — derived from the definition in four steps.

The pattern is worth noticing: the second rule peels one successor off the right argument and moves it outside, repeatedly, until the right argument is $0$ and the first rule finishes the job. Since $b$ is a numeral, that always terminates.

Now the fact module 7.3 forbade you to assume:

$$
a + 1 = a + S(0) = S(a+0) = S(a).
$$

**$S(a) = a+1$ is a theorem.** The successor really is "add one", and it took a definition and two steps to earn.

## What is not built in

Look at the two rules again. They treat the two arguments completely differently — the recursion runs on the **right** one, and the left one is carried along untouched.

So nothing in the definition suggests that
$$
a + b = b + a.
$$

**Commutativity is not built in.** Neither is associativity. Both are theorems, and both need induction.

<Warning title="This is the surprising part of the chapter">
$a + b = b + a$ has been obvious to you since you were five. Here it is a statement requiring proof, and the proof is not short.

The reason is that the definition is **asymmetric** — it recurses on one argument only — while the claim is symmetric. Nothing about the two rules makes the two sides interchangeable, so the symmetry has to be established rather than observed.

Proving it takes a chain of small results, each by induction: first $0 + a = a$ (the mirror of the base case, and *not* the same statement), then $S(a) + b = S(a+b)$, and only then commutativity itself.

**Familiarity is not evidence.** A statement you have never doubted can still need a proof, and a foundational chapter is where that becomes visible.
</Warning>

## One of them, done

> **Proposition.** $0 + a = a$ for every $a \in \mathbb{N}$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The definition gives $a + 0 = a$ immediately. It says nothing directly about $0 + a$, because the recursion runs on the right argument and here the interesting one is on the left.</div>
  <div slot="want">$0 + a = a$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Induct on $a$. The base case is the definition, and the step uses the second rule followed by the hypothesis.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $A(a)$ be the statement $0 + a = a$.

**Base case.** $A(0)$: $0 + 0 = 0$ by the first rule ✓

**Inductive step.** Let $k \in \mathbb{N}$ and assume $0 + k = k$. Then
$$
0 + S(k) = S(0 + k) = S(k),
$$
using the second rule and then the hypothesis. That is $A(S(k))$.

By induction (A9), $0 + a = a$ for every $a \in \mathbb{N}$.
</Proof>

Two lines of computation, and it proves something the definition appeared to state already. **It did not.** The definition gave $a + 0 = a$; this gives $0 + a = a$; and until commutativity is proved, those are different claims.

<Aside>
There is something worth appreciating in what this chapter has done.

Start with $0$, one operation, and nine axioms. Define addition in two lines. Module 7.7 defines multiplication in two more, on top of addition. From there, every law you used in Chapter 4 — commutativity, associativity, distributivity — is provable.

And from those, everything else. The integers are built from $\mathbb{N}$, the rationals from the integers, the reals from the rationals, and the complex numbers from the reals (module 5.2). **The entire tower rests on this.**

The order in which you learned arithmetic is almost exactly the reverse of the order in which it can be justified — which is the right way round for learning, and worth seeing once from the other end.
</Aside>
