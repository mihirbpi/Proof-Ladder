Think about a board game. Snakes and ladders, say.

Before you play, somebody reads out **the rules**. Roll the dice. Move that many. Up ladders, down snakes. First to the end wins.

Now — *why* do you go up ladders?

There is no why. That is just a rule. Somebody decided it, everybody agreed, and now the game works.

## Every game has rules you do not question

You cannot play if you argue about the rules forever. At some point you say "these are the rules" and start.

But notice: you *could* have picked different rules. Down ladders and up snakes! It would be a different game. A perfectly fair game, just not the one on the box.

## Math has rules too

Here is the surprise. Mathematics works exactly the same way.

Every time you prove something, you use something you proved earlier. And *that* used something earlier still. Keep going back and you get a long chain.

Does the chain go back forever? It cannot. So there must be things at the very bottom that nobody proved — things we just agreed to start with.

Those are the rules of the game. And like a board game, if you picked different ones you would get a different mathematics.

## What the bottom looks like

You have used lots of things this year without ever asking why:

- Adding two whole numbers gives a whole number.
- Three rows of ($2$ red and $4$ blue) has the same total as three rows of red plus three rows of blue.
- Every number has a next one.

Have you ever proved any of those? No! You just used them.

Chapter 7 goes all the way to the bottom and writes down the shortest possible list of rules that everything else comes from. There are **nine** of them, and they are surprisingly small.

<Warning title="Rules are chosen, not discovered">
People sometimes think the rules of mathematics were found lying around, like rocks. They were not. Somebody wrote them down, and other people agreed they were a good choice.

Good rules are: few, simple, and enough to build everything you want. Getting that balance right took mathematicians a very long time.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Here is a real example. For two thousand years, geometry had a rule about parallel lines that everyone assumed was the only possible one. In the 1800s people tried a *different* rule — and got a whole new geometry, just as sensible as the old one.

It even turned out to be the geometry the universe actually uses. Changing a rule is allowed, and sometimes it is how you find something true.
</Aside>
