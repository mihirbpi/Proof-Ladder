*Chapter question: if every proof rests on earlier facts, what do the earliest facts rest on?*

## Where the chain of *why* stops

Trace any theorem backwards. $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb{Q}$ (module 4.7) rests on "$n^2$ even $\Rightarrow$ $n$ even" (1.9), which rests on the definition of even (4.2) and closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ (4.1), which rests on…

The chain cannot be infinite: an infinite chain of *why* justifies nothing, since no link is ever anchored. So it terminates, and what it terminates in are statements accepted without proof.

> An **axiom** is a statement assumed true without proof. A **theorem** is a statement derived from the axioms by valid inference. A theory is the deductive closure of its axioms.

## Undefined terms too

The chain of *why* applies to *definitions* as well as to statements. "A set is a collection of things" defines *set* in terms of *collection*, which is just another word for it. Every definition uses earlier terms, so some terms must be **undefined starting word** — undefined, with their behavior fixed entirely by the axioms.

| Theory | Primitives | Axioms |
|---|---|---|
| Set theory | set, $\in$ | a set is fixed by its members; you may only carve a subset out of a set you already have; … |
| Euclidean geometry | point, line, incidence | Euclid's five |
| The counting numbers | zero, and *next* | the nine this chapter builds |
| Combining symmetries | the objects, and a way to combine two | regrouping is free; one does nothing; each can be undone |

One mathematician made the point sharply: it should be possible to replace "point, line, plane" by "table, chair, beer mug" throughout Euclid without affecting a single proof. The axioms carry all the content; the names carry none.

## Criteria for an axiom system

**Consistency.** No contradiction is derivable. This is not one requirement among several — it is the only indispensable one, because from a contradiction everything follows: given $p \wedge \neg p$, module 1.5's conditional makes every statement derivable, so an inconsistent theory proves all statements and distinguishes nothing.

**Independence.** No axiom follows from the others. Demonstrating independence requires exhibiting a model satisfying the others and failing the one in question — which is exactly how non-Euclidean geometry was born.

**Settling everything.** Ideally every statement in the language would be either provable or refutable from the axioms. That turns out to be impossible for any system strong enough to describe arithmetic.

**Adequacy.** The axioms generate the mathematics you wanted. A system that proves nothing at all is consistent and useless.

<Warning title="Axioms need not be self-evident">
The traditional gloss "an axiom is an obvious truth" is wrong and historically damaging. Euclid's parallel postulate was assumed to be obvious for two thousands of years, and mathematicians wasted enormous effort trying to derive it.

The truth is stranger and better: assume the opposite of it and nothing breaks. What you get is a different geometry — one where a triangle's angles add to less than $180°$ — and it is every bit as consistent as the familiar one. The postulate is not a truth about space at all; it is a choice about which geometry you are studying. And general relativity says the universe made a different choice.

The requirement is clarity, consistency, and productivity. Obviousness is neither necessary nor sufficient.
</Warning>

## Independence, demonstrated

To show axiom $A$ is independent of $B_1,\ldots,B_n$, build a **model** satisfying all the $B_i$ and violating $A$. Since the $B_i$ cannot prove something false in a structure where they hold, they cannot prove $A$.

Module 7.4 does exactly this: it exhibits small structures satisfying Peano's axioms 1–6 and failing 7 or 8, thereby proving those axioms are not redundant. Independence proofs are model constructions, and it is the technique that settled the parallel postulate — and, much later, several of the deepest open questions about the foundations of mathematics.

A fair question to ask at this point: once you have written down a set of rules, will they settle *every* question you could ask about the counting numbers?

It is a natural thing to hope for, and it was hoped for seriously in the early 1900s. The answer, found in the 1930s, is no — and it is not a matter of nobody having been clever enough.
