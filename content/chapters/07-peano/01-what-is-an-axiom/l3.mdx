Every proof in this course rests on something earlier.

Module 4.7 proved that the number which multiplies by itself to give $2$ is not a fraction, using module 1.9's fact about even squares. That used the definition of even from module 4.2. Which used closure of the integers from module 4.1.

Which used... what?

## The chain has to stop

Draw the chain of justification backwards. Each result points at the results it depends on, and those point further back.

Two possibilities:

**The chain goes back forever.** Then nothing is ever actually proved — you can always ask "and why is *that* true?" and never get to the bottom.

**The chain stops.** Then there are statements at the bottom that were never proved.

It stops. It has to. And the statements at the bottom have a name.

> An **axiom** is a statement accepted as true without proof.
>
> A **theorem** is a statement proved from the axioms.

A mathematical theory is its axioms, plus everything you can derive from them. Nothing else.

## What has been assumed so far

You have used plenty of unproved things this year:

- Adding two integers gives an integer (module 4.1).
- $3 \times (4+5) = 3\times4 + 3\times5$, and the same for any three numbers (module 4.1).
- Every nonempty set of whole numbers has a smallest element (used in module 4.4).
- Two sets are equal exactly when they have the same elements (module 2.6).

None of these was proved. They were stated and used — honestly, but without foundation. This chapter goes to the bottom and picks a small list to start from.

## Axioms are chosen

This is the part people find surprising.

Axioms are not discovered facts about a pre-existing world. They are **choices**. Somebody writes a list down, and the mathematics that follows is whatever those axioms generate.

Change the list, get different mathematics.

<Aside>
The famous example is geometry. Euclid had five axioms, and the fifth — roughly, "through a point not on a line, there is exactly one parallel line" — always looked less obvious than the others. For two thousand years people tried to prove it from the first four.

In the 1800s, several mathematicians tried assuming it was **false** and looked for a contradiction. They never found one. Instead they found a complete, consistent, perfectly sensible geometry in which parallel lines behave differently.

And then Einstein found that the universe uses one of the new geometries, not Euclid's.
</Aside>

## What makes a list of axioms good

Not any list will do. Mathematicians ask four things.

**Few.** The shorter the list, the more you have genuinely explained.

**Independent.** No axiom should be provable from the others — otherwise it is not an axiom, it is a theorem you forgot to prove.

**Consistent.** They must not lead to a contradiction. This is the crucial one: from a contradiction you can prove *anything*, so an inconsistent theory is worthless.

**Enough.** They must actually generate the mathematics you wanted. A list of two axioms that proves nothing is very short and no use at all.

<Warning title="Axioms are not obvious truths">
It is tempting to think axioms should be things nobody could doubt. That is a nice bonus and it is not the requirement.

What matters is that they are plainly stated, consistent, and productive. Some perfectly good axioms are wildly unobvious — and some obvious-looking ones turned out to be optional all along.
</Warning>
