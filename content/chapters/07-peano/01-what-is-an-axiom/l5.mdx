Every proof in this course justified its steps by appealing to something earlier. Ask *why* often enough and the chain has to stop.

> **An axiom is a statement accepted without proof.** A **theorem** is a statement proved from the axioms.

The chain stops because it must. Every justification cites something prior, and a chain of justifications cannot run backwards forever — so either it ends at statements assumed without proof, or it loops, and a loop justifies nothing.

## You have already met this

Your geometry course does exactly this, and it says so.

It does not define *point*, *line* or *plane*. They are **undefined terms**, listed and left alone, because any definition would have to use other words that would themselves need defining.

It then states postulates — through any two points there is exactly one line; a segment has exactly one midpoint — and everything after that is proved.

**That structure is not a teaching device. It is what mathematics is.** Chapter 7 does the same job for the counting numbers, and there are only nine axioms.

## Axioms are chosen

The word "axiom" sometimes suggests a self-evident truth that could not have been otherwise. That is not what mathematicians mean.

**Axioms are choices**, and different choices give different mathematics, all of it equally valid. What makes a set of axioms good is not obviousness but four properties:

| Property | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Consistent** | no contradiction can be derived |
| **Independent** | none is provable from the others |
| **Adequate** | strong enough to prove what you want |
| **Small** | as few as will do the job |

Consistency is non-negotiable — module 1.3 noted that from a contradiction everything follows, so an inconsistent system proves every statement and is worthless.

The other three are matters of taste and economy. Independence especially is a genuine question with a real technique behind it, and module 7.4 uses it.

## How to show an axiom is independent

You cannot show a statement is unprovable by failing to prove it. That is a fact about your effort.

**The method is to build a model:** a working example that obeys all the *other* axioms and breaks the one in question. If such an example exists, no derivation of that axiom from the others can exist either, because a derivation would have to work in every example where the premises hold — and here is one where it does not.

Module 7.4 constructs two such structures explicitly, and the technique is worth watching, because it turns a question about what is provable into a question about what exists.

## The most famous example

For two thousand years geometry had one postulate that looked less obvious than the rest — the **parallel postulate**, which in one form says that through a point not on a line there is exactly one parallel to it.

It was longer and clumsier than the others, and generations of mathematicians tried to prove it from them. All failed.

In the 1800s, several people tried something else: **assume it is false** and see what breaks.

Nothing broke. What emerged were geometries just as consistent as Euclid's, in which triangle angles sum to more or less than $180°$ and no parallels exist, or infinitely many do.

<Warning title="Changing an axiom does not produce nonsense">
The natural reaction is that these alternative geometries must be wrong, since the parallel postulate is plainly true.

It is not plainly true. **It is not even true of the universe** — Einstein's general relativity describes spacetime with a geometry in which it fails, and that description is the one that matches observation.

So the postulate was never a fact discovered about space. It was an assumption, and the mathematics built on assuming its opposite is exactly as legitimate.

That is why *"axioms are chosen, not discovered"* is a claim with evidence behind it rather than a philosophical preference.
</Warning>

## Why bother setting down rules for arithmetic

You have used the counting numbers your whole life. Why write down rules for them?

Because *"the numbers are what everybody means by the numbers"* is not something a proof can cite. Chapter 4 assumed closure — that adding or multiplying two whole numbers never takes you outside the whole numbers — along with commutativity and distributivity, freely; those assumptions were never justified, only used.

Chapter 7 justifies them. From nine axioms about $0$ and *the next number*, it constructs addition and multiplication and **proves** the laws Chapter 4 took for granted. The distributive law stops being an assumption and becomes a theorem.

<Aside>
There is a limit to this program, and it was found in 1931.

Mathematicians had hoped to write down axioms for arithmetic that were consistent, and complete — able to settle every statement about numbers as true or false.

That hope turned out to be impossible to satisfy, and the reason was settled in the 1930s. Any honest set of axioms strong enough to describe arithmetic leaves questions it cannot answer either way — and adding more axioms does not rescue it, because the larger system has its own unanswerable questions. A later course gives the argument, which is genuinely hard.

So module 1.1's claim that every statement has a definite truth value survives, and the hope that proof could always find it does not.
</Aside>
