*Chapter question: if every proof rests on earlier facts, what do the earliest facts rest on?*

## The regress, and its two termini

Justification regresses in two directions, and both must terminate.

**Statements.** Every theorem is derived from earlier ones. An infinite regress anchors nothing, so some statements are assumed: the **axioms**.

**Terms.** Every definition is phrased in earlier terms. So some terms are undefined: the **primitives**, whose meaning is exhausted by the axioms governing them.

A theory is then a language (primitives plus logical apparatus), a set of axioms, and the deductive closure under a fixed inference system. Nothing else is in it.

| Theory | Primitives |
|---|---|
| ZF set theory | set, $\in$ |
| Euclidean geometry | point, line, incidence, betweenness |
| Peano arithmetic | $0$, $S$ |
| Group theory | underlying set, binary operation |

Hilbert's dictum makes the point: one should be able to substitute "table, chair, beer mug" for "point, line, plane" throughout Euclid with no proof affected. All content lives in the axioms; none in the names. This is why the same group axioms describe symmetries, permutations, and elliptic curves at once.

## Semantic and syntactic

Two notions of consequence must be distinguished.

**Syntactic:** $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ — there is a formal derivation of $\varphi$ from $\Gamma$.

**Semantic:** $\Gamma \models \varphi$ — every structure making all of $\Gamma$ true makes $\varphi$ true.

> **Gödel's completeness theorem (1929).** For first-order logic, $\Gamma\vdash\varphi \iff \Gamma\models\varphi$.

Provability and validity coincide. This is what licenses the model-theoretic method: to show $\varphi$ is *not* provable from $\Gamma$, exhibit a **model** of $\Gamma$ in which $\varphi$ fails.

## Independence

To prove axiom $A$ independent of $B_1,\ldots,B_n$: construct a structure satisfying every $B_i$ and refuting $A$.

Historically decisive instances:

- **Parallel postulate.** The Beltrami–Klein and Poincaré disc models satisfy Euclid's first four axioms and refute the fifth, ending two millennia of failed derivations. Hyperbolic geometry is as consistent as Euclidean.
- **Axiom of Choice.** Gödel's constructible universe $L$ shows ZF $\not\vdash \neg$AC; Cohen's forcing shows ZF $\not\vdash$ AC.
- **Continuum hypothesis.** Same two techniques, same conclusion: independent of ZFC.

§7.4 uses precisely this method on Peano's axioms, exhibiting one- and two-element structures satisfying axioms 1–6 while violating 7 or 8. Independence is not a philosophical remark; it is a construction.

## Criteria

**Consistency** is not one virtue among several but the precondition for the theory meaning anything. From $p \wedge \neg p$ every statement follows, so an inconsistent theory proves everything and distinguishes nothing.

**Independence** is aesthetic bookkeeping: a dependent axiom is a theorem misfiled.

**Completeness** — every sentence provable or refutable — is unattainable for arithmetic:

> **Gödel's first incompleteness theorem (1931).** Any consistent, effectively axiomatised theory interpreting arithmetic has a sentence neither provable nor refutable.
>
> **Second theorem.** Such a theory cannot prove its own consistency.

Note the contrast with the completeness theorem above: first-order *logic* is complete; sufficiently strong first-order *theories* are not. The two results are compatible and are about different things.

**Adequacy.** The axioms must generate the intended mathematics. Trivially consistent systems proving nothing are useless, and the tension between adequacy and provable consistency is exactly what Gödel's second theorem formalises.

<Warning title="Self-evidence is not a criterion">
The scholastic gloss — an axiom is an obvious truth — is false and was historically costly. The parallel postulate looked obvious and is optional; the Axiom of Choice looks obvious and implies Banach–Tarski; the Axiom of Foundation looks obvious and can be consistently denied (Aczel's anti-foundation axiom, used to model circular data structures).

The criteria are clarity, consistency, independence, and adequacy. Intuitive plausibility is a convenience for the reader, not a mathematical property.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Consistency is provable only relatively. "ZFC is consistent" cannot be established within ZFC (second theorem), so results take the form: *if* ZF is consistent, so is ZFC + CH. Similarly the consistency of Peano arithmetic was proved by Gentzen using transfinite induction to $\varepsilon_0$ — a principle PA cannot itself justify, exactly as the second theorem requires.

Foundations does not rest on bedrock. It rests on a well-mapped network of relative consistency results, and knowing the map is what replaces the certainty that was hoped for in 1900.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
The axiomatic method reshaped mathematics between 1880 and 1930 and is now invisible because it is universal: a group, a ring, a topological space, a manifold, and a category are each *defined* by axioms, and every theorem about them applies to every model at once. That generality is the return on the abstraction.

Its metatheory became a field. Model theory studies the structures satisfying a set of axioms, and its results are concrete: compactness, Löwenheim–Skolem, quantifier elimination for real closed fields (hence a decision procedure for Euclidean geometry), and Ax–Grothendieck, whose proof that an injective polynomial map $\mathbb{C}^n\to\mathbb{C}^n$ is surjective goes via finite fields and a transfer principle. Proof theory studies derivations as combinatorial objects and calibrates theories by ordinals. And computability theory identifies which axiom systems can be mechanically checked — the "effectively axiomatised" hypothesis in Gödel's theorem, without which the theorem fails and with which every modern proof assistant operates.
</WhereThisGoes>
