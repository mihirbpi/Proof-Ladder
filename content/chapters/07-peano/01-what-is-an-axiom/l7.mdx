*Chapter question: if every proof rests on earlier facts, what do the earliest facts rest on?*

## The regress, and its two termini

Justification regresses in two directions, and both must terminate.

**Statements.** Every theorem is derived from earlier ones. An infinite regress anchors nothing, so some statements are assumed: the **axioms**.

**Terms.** Every definition is phrased in earlier terms. So some terms are undefined: the **undefined starting words**, whose meaning is exhausted by the axioms governing them.

A theory is then a language (undefined starting words plus logical apparatus), a set of axioms, and the deductive closure under a fixed inference system. Nothing else is in it.

| Theory | Primitives |
|---|---|
| ZF set theory | set, $\in$ |
| Euclidean geometry | point, line, incidence, betweenness |
| Peano arithmetic | $0$, $S$ |
| The study of symmetry | underlying set, binary operation |

A famous remark makes the point: one should be able to substitute "table, chair, beer mug" for "point, line, plane" throughout Euclid with no proof affected. All content lives in the axioms; none in the names. This is why the same group axioms describe symmetries, permutations, and elliptic curves at once.

## Semantic and syntactic

Two notions of consequence must be distinguished.

**Provable.** There is a chain of justified steps leading from the axioms to the statement.

**True in every case.** Every structure in which the axioms all hold is a structure in which the statement holds too.

These sound like different things and, for the usual logical system, they turn out to be the same thing — a result of Gödel's from 1929.

That equivalence is what licenses the method used throughout this chapter: **to show a statement is not provable from a set of axioms, build one structure where the axioms hold and the statement fails.** No search through possible proofs is required, and none would ever finish.

## Independence

To prove axiom $A$ independent of $B_1,\ldots,B_n$: construct a structure satisfying every $B_i$ and refuting $A$.

Historically decisive instances:

- **Parallel postulate.** The Beltrami–Klein and Poincaré disc models satisfy Euclid's first four axioms and refute the fifth, ending two millennia of failed derivations. Hyperbolic geometry is as consistent as Euclidean.
- **Two famous set-theory questions.** Both were settled the same way in the twentieth century: structures were built in which the standard axioms hold and the statement is true, and others in which they hold and it is false. So the axioms decide neither, and that is a theorem rather than an admission of defeat.

§7.4 uses precisely this method on Peano's axioms, exhibiting one- and two-element structures satisfying axioms 1–6 while violating 7 or 8. Independence is not a philosophical remark; it is a construction.

## Why proofs must terminate

Every proof justifies its claims by earlier claims, and following the chain backwards gives three possibilities.

**Infinite regress.** No claim is ever established, because the justification never completes.

**Circularity.** $P$ because $Q$, $Q$ because $P$. Nothing is established either.

**Termination.** Some statements are accepted without proof, and everything else is derived from them.

Only the third produces knowledge, so **axioms exist because proof requires a starting point** — not because anyone believes they are self-evident. That distinction matters, because the self-evidence reading is what makes the discovery of non-Euclidean geometry look paradoxical, and the termination reading makes it routine.

Undefined terms are the same phenomenon for definitions. *Point*, *line*, and *plane* in geometry; *set* and *membership* in set theory; $0$ and *successor* here. Definitions must bottom out or circle, and what replaces the definition is a list of properties assumed to hold.

**The practical consequence:** when reading a new axiomatic subject, the first question is which terms are undefined and which statements are assumed. Everything else is derived, and the derivation is checkable.

## Criteria

**Consistency** is not one virtue among several but the precondition for the theory meaning anything. From $p \wedge \neg p$ every statement follows, so an inconsistent theory proves everything and distinguishes nothing.

**Independence** is aesthetic bookkeeping: a dependent axiom is a theorem misfiled.

**Settling everything** — every statement in the language either provable or refutable — is unattainable for arithmetic:

> **Gödel's first incompleteness theorem (1931).** Any consistent, effectively axiomatised theory interpreting arithmetic has a sentence neither provable nor refutable.
>
> **Second theorem.** Such a theory cannot prove its own consistency.

Note the contrast with the result above: the *logic* is complete; sufficiently strong *theories* are not. The two results are compatible and are about different things.

### Adequacy

The axioms must generate the intended mathematics. Trivially consistent systems proving nothing are useless, and the tension between adequacy and provable consistency is exactly what Gödel's second theorem makes precise.

<Warning title="Self-evidence is not a criterion">
The scholastic gloss — an axiom is an obvious truth — is false and was historically costly. The parallel postulate looked obvious and is optional; the an extra set-theoretic assumption looks obvious and implies Banach–Tarski; the Axiom of Foundation looks obvious and can be consistently denied (Aczel's anti-foundation axiom, used to model circular data structures).

The criteria are clarity, consistency, independence, and adequacy. Intuitive plausibility is a convenience for the reader, not a mathematical property.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Consistency is provable only relatively. A system strong enough to describe arithmetic cannot prove its *own* consistency (Gödel's second theorem), so results always take the conditional form: *if* this weaker system is consistent, then so is that stronger one. Similarly the consistency of Peano arithmetic was proved by Gentzen using extended induction to $\varepsilon_0$ — a principle PA cannot itself justify, exactly as the second theorem requires.

Foundations does not rest on bedrock. It rests on a well-mapped network of relative consistency results, and knowing the map is what replaces the certainty that was hoped for in 1900.
</Aside>

The axiomatic method is what both courses ahead are organised by, and recognising it changes how you read a textbook.

**In calculus the axioms are about $\mathbb{R}$.** It is the unique complete ordered field, and the three parts of that phrase do distinguishable work. The field axioms (§4.5) permit algebra; the order axioms permit inequalities; **completeness** is what makes calculus possible, and it is the axiom every major theorem quietly invokes.

The big theorems of calculus are all false over $\mathbb{Q}$, and completeness is exactly what $\mathbb{Q}$ lacks.

**The lesson from geometry transfers.** Changing an axiom changes the subject, and the resulting subject may be perfectly consistent — non-Euclidean geometry, finite-dimensional spaces over finite fields, non-Archimedean number systems. An axiom is a choice with consequences, not a self-evident truth.
