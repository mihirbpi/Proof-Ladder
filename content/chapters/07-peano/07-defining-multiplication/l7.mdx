$$
\textbf{(M1)}\ a\cdot 0 = 0, \qquad \textbf{(M2)}\ a\cdot S(b) = a + (a\cdot b).
$$

Recursion on the second argument, iterating $+$ exactly as $+$ iterates $S$. In each case the base is the identity of the iterated operation:

$$
a + b = S^b(a), \qquad a\cdot b = \underbrace{a + \cdots + a}_{b}.
$$

Legitimacy comes from the recursive-definition principle (§7.6), so from A9.

## Basic derivations

<Proof>
$3\cdot2 = 3 + (3\cdot1) = 3 + (3 + (3\cdot0)) = 3 + (3+0) = 6$.

$a\cdot1 = a\cdot S(0) = a + (a\cdot0) = a + 0 = a$.
</Proof>

## Distributivity

Assumed as an arithmetic rule in §4.1; derivable here.

> **Theorem.** $a(b+c) = ab + ac$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">(M1), (M2), (A1), (A2), plus associativity and commutativity of $+$ from §7.6.</div>
  <div slot="want">The identity for all $a,b,c \in \mathbb{N}$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Induct on $c$, matching the recursion variable of both definitions. The rearrangement in the step consumes §7.6's theorems — so the previous module is a prerequisite in substance, not merely in sequence.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Induct on $c$.

*Base.* $a(b+0) = ab$ by (A1); $ab + a\cdot0 = ab+0 = ab$ by (M1), (A1).

*Step.* Assuming $a(b+k) = ab+ak$,
$$
a\big(b+S(k)\big) \overset{\text{(A2)}}{=} a\,S(b+k) \overset{\text{(M2)}}{=} a + a(b+k) = a + (ab+ak) = ab + (a + ak) \overset{\text{(M2)}}{=} ab + a\,S(k),
$$
the second-to-last step by associativity and commutativity of $+$.
</Proof>

Right-distributivity $(a+b)c = ac+bc$ requires a separate induction — or follows from commutativity of $\cdot$, which itself needs distributivity, so the order of proof matters.

## The remaining laws

By further inductions, in this order:

$$
0\cdot a = 0, \qquad S(a)\cdot b = b + a\cdot b, \qquad a\cdot b = b\cdot a, \qquad (ab)c = a(bc).
$$

Commutativity of $\cdot$ needs the middle lemma exactly as commutativity of $+$ needed $S(a)+b = S(a+b)$ (§7.6) — the same proof shape one level up. Associativity needs distributivity.

Result: the counting numbers obey every arithmetic rule of §4.1 except the one giving negatives. $\mathbb{Z}$ is its Grothendieck completion, formed as $(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N})/\!\sim$ with $(a,b)\sim(c,d)\iff a+d=b+c$ (§7.2), and the arithmetic laws proved here are exactly what makes that construction's operations well defined.

## What the course proved

Chapter 1 supplied the language: statements, joining words, conditionals, quantifiers, and the moves that prove each.

Chapter 2 supplied sets, and showed the logic reappearing as set operations under a systematic translation.

Chapter 3 supplied functions — and the vocabulary that this chapter's axioms are stated in. Axiom 8 *is* injectivity; Axiom 7 *is* emptiness of a pre-image.

Chapter 4 built the number systems and the divisibility template.

Chapter 5 completed them, and found plane geometry inside the algebra.

Chapter 6 supplied induction, the tool for infinitely many cases at once.

Chapter 7 went to the bottom and found nine assumptions.

**The techniques were the same throughout.** Double inclusion in Chapter 2 is check-both-boxes from Chapter 1. Transitivity of $\subseteq$, of $\mid$, and of $\leq$ are one argument written three times. De Morgan appeared for joining words, for sets, and for quantifiers.

There are not many techniques. There are many places to use them, and recognizing a familiar shape in unfamiliar notation is most of what fluency consists of.

## Where the tower stops

Iterating once more gives exponentiation, $a^0 = 1$, $a^{S(b)} = a\cdot a^b$, and the tower keeps climbing: repeated exponentiation, then repeated repeated exponentiation, and so on without end. Growth is explosive. Climb the tower and read off the $n$th operation applied to $n$ and $n$, and you get a function whose values are computable in principle and astronomically large almost immediately — large enough that the nine axioms of this chapter cannot prove it never runs forever.

Note also that the hierarchy loses structure as it climbs. Addition is commutative and associative; multiplication is too; exponentiation is neither. The good behavior of $+$ and $\cdot$ is not typical of iterated operations — it is special, and the theorems above are where that specialness is proved.

<Aside>
Arithmetic with addition alone — the counting numbers, $+$, and no multiplication at all — is perfectly well behaved: consistent, and every question in its language can be settled. Adding multiplication changes everything, because $+$ and $\cdot$ together are enough to encode statements *about* arithmetic inside arithmetic itself, and that is exactly what Gödel's argument needs.

Multiplication is what makes arithmetic strong enough to be incomplete. §7.7 is therefore also the module where the limits proved in §7.1 become inevitable.
</Aside>

This is where the course closes, and the closing observation is that everything in Chapters 1–6 concerning $\mathbb{N}$ now rests on nine axioms.

**The tower, in order.** Successor is undefined and constrained by axioms. Addition is defined recursively from it. Multiplication is defined recursively from addition. $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, and $\mathbb{C}$ are each built from the previous system to repair a specific closure failure. Nothing is assumed twice, and nothing circles.

**Every arithmetic law is a theorem.** Commutativity, associativity, and distributivity — the arithmetic rules of §4.1, used freely there — are proved here by induction. What one chapter assumes, another proves, and the direction of that dependency is worth tracing once.

**In calculus, the same tower is what the derivative rules rest on.** Linearity of differentiation is distributivity applied to the limit definition; the product rule is a statement about what differentiating does to a product; and the fact that $(fg)' \neq f'g'$ says that differentiating does not simply pass through a multiplication. Each is provable, and each depends on the arithmetic being what this chapter says it is.

**And the exponentiation pattern continues.** $x^0 := 1$, $x^{S(y)} := x^y\cdot x$ — repeated multiplication, defined from multiplication exactly as multiplication was defined from addition. The sequence does not stop, and the operations grow fast enough that the fifth one on small inputs already exceeds any quantity in the physical universe.
