Addition was built from the successor. Multiplication is built the same way, from addition.

## The definition

> $$ a \cdot 0 = 0 $$
> $$ a \cdot S(b) = a + (a \cdot b) $$

Compare module 7.6's definition of addition and the parallel is exact — a base case, and a rule that peels one successor off the right argument.

The second rule says: **multiplying by the next number adds one more copy.** Which is what multiplication has always meant. Repeated addition, made into a definition rather than an intuition.

## Watch it compute

$$
\begin{aligned}
2 \cdot 3 = 2\cdot S(2) &= 2 + (2 \cdot 2) && \text{second rule}\\
&= 2 + \big(2 + (2\cdot1)\big) && \text{again}\\
&= 2 + \big(2 + (2 + (2\cdot 0))\big) && \text{again}\\
&= 2 + (2 + (2 + 0)) && \text{first rule}\\
&= 6.
\end{aligned}
$$

Three copies of $2$, added — and the recursion unwound to the base case exactly as addition's did, then the additions were carried out using module 7.6's definition.

Note the dependency. **Multiplication is defined using addition**, which was defined using the successor, which is undefined. Three levels, each resting on the one below:

$$
S \ \longrightarrow\ + \ \longrightarrow\ \times
$$

Take away any level and the ones above it have nothing to stand on.

## The distributive law is a theorem

Chapter 4 assumed $a(b+c) = ab+ac$ freely. Module 4.1 called it one of the rules defining a ring, and never justified it.

Here it is provable.

> **Theorem.** $a \cdot (b + c) = a\cdot b + a \cdot c$ for all $a, b, c \in \mathbb{N}$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Both definitions recurse on the right argument, so induct on $c$ — the rightmost one, and the only one both rules can act on.</div>
  <div slot="want">The identity for every $c$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Base case is $c = 0$, where both sides collapse via the two base rules. The step applies each definition once and then the hypothesis, and needs associativity of addition — itself a theorem from module 7.6.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Fix $a, b \in \mathbb{N}$ and induct on $c$.

**Base case.** $c = 0$:
$$
a(b+0) = a\cdot b, \qquad a\cdot b + a\cdot 0 = a\cdot b + 0 = a \cdot b.
$$
Equal ✓

**Inductive step.** Assume $a(b+k) = ab + ak$. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
a\big(b + S(k)\big) &= a \cdot S(b+k) && \text{definition of } +\\
&= a + a(b+k) && \text{definition of } \times\\
&= a + (ab + ak) && \text{hypothesis}\\
&= ab + (a + ak) && \text{associativity and commutativity of } +\\
&= ab + a\cdot S(k) && \text{definition of } \times.
\end{aligned}
$$
That is the claim for $S(k)$.

By induction, the distributive law holds throughout $\mathbb{N}$.
</Proof>

**Every step cites a definition, the hypothesis, or an earlier theorem.** Nothing is waved through — which is the standard the whole chapter has been building toward.

<Warning title="The order of proof is the reverse of the order of teaching">
You learned multiplication as repeated addition, then met the distributive law as a rule for expanding brackets, then used it thousands of times.

Here the order is inverted: the definition comes first, and the law is derived from it. And the derivation needs commutativity and associativity of **addition**, which module 7.6 flagged as theorems requiring their own inductions.

So a law that took one line in Chapter 4 sits near the top of a stack of results. **Chapter 4 was right to assume it** — you cannot build a course bottom-up from axioms — but it is worth knowing the debt was real and has now been paid.
</Warning>

## What the chapter accomplished

| Started with | Built |
|---|---|
| $0$, $S$, nine axioms | the counting numbers, exactly (7.5) |
| $+$ defined recursively | commutativity, associativity (7.6) |
| $\times$ defined recursively | distributivity (7.7) |

Everything Chapter 4 called a rule is now a theorem. The ring properties of $\mathbb{Z}$, the field properties of $\mathbb{Q}$, and every algebraic manipulation resting on them trace back to two undefined symbols and nine assumptions.

<Aside>
It is worth asking what this was for, since nobody needs a proof that $2+2=4$.

Two answers.

**It shows the tower has a bottom.** Every proof in this course cited something earlier, and module 7.1 pointed out that such chains must terminate. This chapter exhibits the termination — you can now follow any argument in the course down to axioms, with no step left unjustified.

**It shows the bottom is small.** Nine axioms, two undefined symbols, and four recursive lines generate the whole of arithmetic. That is a remarkable economy, and it was not obvious in advance that it could be done at all.

Neither answer is about being certain that $2+2=4$. They are about knowing what that fact rests on — and, as module 7.1's Gödel aside warned, about knowing that even a foundation this careful cannot settle everything.
</Aside>
