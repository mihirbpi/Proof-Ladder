Module 7.6 built addition out of the successor. This module builds multiplication out of addition, in exactly the same way.

## The definition

> **(M1)** Multiplying by zero gives zero. Zero groups of anything is nothing at all.
>
> **(M2)** To multiply by *the next one after something*, first multiply by the something, then add one more copy.

(M2) is the rule that makes multiplying into repeated adding, and it says so out loud.

Compare with addition's rules from module 7.6:

> Adding zero changes nothing. To add the next one after something, add the something and then press **next**.

The shape is identical. Addition repeats the successor; multiplication repeats addition. **Each operation is the one below it, done over and over.**

## Deriving $3 \cdot 2 = 6$

<Proof>
$$
\begin{aligned}
3 \cdot 2 &= 3 \cdot S(1) = 3 + (3\cdot 1) && \text{by (M2)}\\
3 \cdot 1 &= 3 \cdot S(0) = 3 + (3 \cdot 0) && \text{by (M2)}\\
3 \cdot 0 &= 0 && \text{by (M1)}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Now build back up:
$$
3 \cdot 1 = 3 + 0 = 3, \qquad 3\cdot 2 = 3 + 3 = 6.
$$
</Proof>

Just as with addition, every product unwinds to the base case and then rebuilds.

## A small theorem: $a \cdot 1 = a$

<Proof>
$$
a \cdot 1 = a \cdot S(0) \overset{\text{(M2)}}{=} a + (a\cdot 0) \overset{\text{(M1)}}{=} a + 0 = a,
$$
the last step by addition's rule (A1).
</Proof>

Three lines. Notice that the proof used *both* definitions — multiplication's rules to unwind, and addition's rule to finish. The tower is load-bearing.

## The tower, complete

$$
\underbrace{0,\ S}_{\text{axioms}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{+}_{\text{module 7.6}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\cdot}_{\text{here}}
$$

Two undefined starting words. Two recursive definitions. That is the whole of arithmetic on the counting numbers.

## What still needs proving

Everything familiar remains to be earned:

- Multiplying two numbers gives the same answer either way round — **not** given by (M1)–(M2), which treat the two slots completely differently.
- Multiplying three numbers gives the same answer however you bracket them.
- The **distributive law** — multiplying across a sum, like $3 \times (4+5) = 3\times4 + 3\times5$.

That last one is worth pausing on. In module 4.1 you were told the distributive law as one of the rules the integers obey — something to accept and use.

It is not a rule. **It is a theorem**, provable from (M1), (M2), and the addition rules, by induction on $c$.

<Warning title="Everything assumed is now derivable">
Look back over the course. Module 4.1 assumed closure and distributivity. Module 4.4 assumed that any collection of counting numbers has a smallest one. Chapter 6 used induction as a technique.

All of it now comes from nine axioms and two recursive definitions. Nothing was left resting on nothing.

That is what it means to build mathematics from the bottom — and it is a fair answer to Chapter 7's question, *if every proof rests on earlier facts, what do the earliest facts rest on?*
</Warning>

<Aside>
The pattern continues upward. Just as multiplication is repeated addition, raising to a power is repeated multiplication:
> Raising to the power zero gives $1$. To raise to *the next power up*, raise to the power below and then multiply by one more copy.
Same two-rule shape, one floor higher.

And you can keep going — there is an operation that repeats exponentiation, and one that repeats *that*. They grow so fast that writing down their values is impossible almost immediately, which is a strange and rather wonderful place for a course to end.
</Aside>
