One unwanted system remains: $\mathbb{N}$ with extra chains bolted on, unreachable by counting from $0$. The last axiom removes it, and it turns out to be something you already know.

## Inductive sets

A set $A\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ is **inductive** when

1. $0\in A$, and
2. for every $x$: if $x\in A$ then $S(x)\in A$.

Closed under starting and closed under stepping — **the two domino conditions of module 6.1**, stated as a property of a set.

Examples:

- $\mathbb{N}$ itself is inductive.
- $\{0,1,2,3\}$ is **not**: it contains $3$ and not $S(3)$.
- The even numbers are **not**: they contain $0$ and not $S(0)$.
- $\{0,1,2,\dots\}\cup\{a, S(a),\dots\}$ — the rogue system — **is** inductive.

That last one is the problem. Being inductive is not enough to pin down $\mathbb{N}$, and the axiom must therefore say something stronger.

## Axiom 9

> **Axiom 9 (Induction).** If $A\subseteq\mathbb{N}$ is inductive, then $A = \mathbb{N}$.

**$\mathbb{N}$ is the *smallest* inductive set.** No proper subset can be inductive, so nothing in $\mathbb{N}$ is unreachable from $0$ by stepping.

## It kills the rogue system

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The rogue system is $R = \{0,1,2,\dots\}\cup\{a,S(a),\dots\}$, with the second chain disjoint from the first.</div>
  <div slot="want">A contradiction with Axiom 9.</div>
  <div slot="how">Exhibit a proper subset of $R$ that is inductive. The first chain alone is the obvious candidate — it contains $0$ and is closed under $S$, and it omits $a$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose $R$ satisfied all nine axioms. Let $A = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$, the first chain alone.

$0\in A$ ✓, and $A$ is closed under $S$, since the successor of any element of the first chain is again in the first chain. So $A$ is inductive.

By Axiom 9, $A = R$. But $a\in R$ and $a\notin A$ — contradiction.

So $R$ does not satisfy the axioms.
</Proof>

**Axiom 9 is exactly the statement that everything is reachable by counting.** The other eight described local behavior — a start, a step, no loops, no merges; this one is global, and it says the chain from $0$ is all there is.

## It is the induction principle

Recall Chapter 6:

> If $A(0)$ holds, and $A(k)\Rightarrow A(k+1)$ for all $k$, then $A(n)$ holds for all $n$.

Take $A = \{n \in\mathbb{N} : A(n) \text{ is true}\}$. Then:

- the base case says $0\in A$;
- the inductive step says $A$ is closed under $S$;
- so $A$ is inductive, and Axiom 9 gives $A = \mathbb{N}$;
- which says $A(n)$ holds for every $n$.

**Chapter 6's technique is this axiom, applied to a set defined by a statement.** Every induction proof you have written was an appeal to Axiom 9, and module 6.2's question — *why is induction valid?* — now has its final answer: it is assumed.

Not proved, and not provable. It is an axiom, on the same footing as the Parallel Postulate.

<Warning title="Induction is an assumption, and it is not innocuous">
Module 6.2 derived induction from the smallest-number principle. That derivation is honest and it does not settle anything, because the smallest-number principle itself cannot be proved from the other axioms.

The two are equivalent: assume either and the other follows. Neither is derivable from Axioms 1–8, and module 7.4's non-standard models are the proof — they satisfy Axioms 1–8 and fail Axiom 9.

So a choice is being made, exactly as your geometry course chose the Parallel Postulate. The counting numbers are the system in which induction works, and that is a decision rather than a discovery.
</Warning>

## The set equality

> **Theorem.** $\mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, where the right side means all values you reach from $0$ by repeated application of $S$.

<Proof>
Let $C = \{0, S(0), S(S(0)), \dots\}$, the counting chain.

**($\supseteq$)** Every element of $C$ lies in $\mathbb{N}$: $0\in\mathbb{N}$ by Axiom 5, and $\mathbb{N}$ is closed under $S$ by Axiom 6. So $C\subseteq\mathbb{N}$.

**($\subseteq$)** $C$ contains $0$ and is closed under $S$ by construction, so $C$ is inductive. By Axiom 9, $C = \mathbb{N}$, so $\mathbb{N}\subseteq C$.

Therefore $\mathbb{N} = C$.
</Proof>

**Double inclusion** (module 2.6), with **Axioms 5–6 giving one direction and Axiom 9 the other.** That is a clean division of labor, and it shows what each axiom contributes: 5 and 6 make the chain exist, 9 makes it everything.

## The nine axioms

| # | Statement | Ruled out |
|---|---|---|
| 1–4 | equality is an equivalence relation, closed | incoherent equality |
| 5 | $0\in\mathbb{N}$ | emptiness |
| 6 | $S(x)\in\mathbb{N}$ | a chain that stops |
| 7 | $S(x)\neq0$ | loops |
| 8 | $S$ is injective | merges |
| 9 | induction | unreachable extras |

Nine assumptions, and from them all of arithmetic. Modules 7.6 and 7.7 build addition and multiplication and prove that the familiar laws hold.

<Aside>
Do the axioms determine $\mathbb{N}$ completely?

**In one sense yes.** Take any two systems obeying all nine. They can be matched up one-for-one so that zero goes to zero and each system's *next* rule lines up with the other's — so anything you can prove about one is true of the other, with the names swapped. It therefore does not matter whether you build the counting numbers from sets, from tally marks, or from anything else.

**In another sense no.** That uniqueness proof quantifies (puts a *for every* or *there is* in front, §1.10) over *all subsets* of the system, which is a stronger kind of statement than the ones formal logic usually permits. Restrict Axiom 9 to properties expressible in a formal language and uniqueness fails: non-standard models exist, containing infinite "numbers."

The gap between the two senses is where the result from module 7.1 lives. **The axioms pin down the numbers if you are allowed to talk about arbitrary subsets, and not otherwise** — and the discovery that this distinction matters is one of the major results of twentieth-century mathematics.
</Aside>
