Four axioms are not enough, and module 7.4 said why: they force $0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ to all be there and all be different, but they do not stop extra objects sneaking in.

## The rogue model, exactly

Let the collection be
$$
\{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\} \text{ together with two extra objects } a \text{ and } b
$$
with the usual successor on the numbers, plus $S(a) = b$ and $S(b) = a$.

| Axiom | Holds? |
|---|---|
| 5: $0$ is in it | ✓ |
| 6: successors stay inside | ✓ — including $S(a)=b$ |
| 7: nothing has $0$ as successor | ✓ — $a$ and $b$ point at each other |
| 8: different things, different successors | ✓ — $S$ is still one-to-one |

All four pass, and $a$ and $b$ are plainly not counting numbers. They sit off to one side in their own little loop, and you can never reach them by starting at $0$ and pressing next.

## Inductive sets

A set $V$ is **inductive** if:

1. $0$ is in $V$, and
2. whenever something is in $V$, the next number after it is in $V$ too.

In words: it contains the starting point, and it is closed under taking the next one.

## Axiom 9

> **Axiom 9.** If $V$ is inductive, then every counting number is in $V$.

That is the whole thing. It says the counting numbers are the *smallest* inductive set — they sit inside every single one.

## How it kills the rogues

<Proof>
Let $V = \{0,1,2,3,\ldots\}$ — the numbers reachable from $0$ by pressing next, and nothing else.

**Is $V$ inductive?** $0$ is in it ✓. And anything in it is reachable, so pressing next once more makes the following number reachable too, which puts that in $V$ as well ✓.

Yes, $V$ is inductive.

**So by Axiom 9,** every counting number is in $V$.

But $a$ is not in $V$. So $a$ is not a counting number. Same for $b$.
</Proof>

The rogues are gone, and now we can say exactly what the counting numbers are:
$$
\text{the counting numbers} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}
$$
with nothing missing and nothing extra.

Notice the shape of that conclusion. Axioms 5–6 show that everything in $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ is a counting number. Axiom 9 shows that every counting number is in $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$. Two containments, one equality — **check both boxes**, from module 2.6.

## This is induction

Look at the definition of *inductive* again with Chapter 6 in mind.

$0$ is in $V$ — that is the **base case**.

whenever something is in $V$, its next one is in $V$ too — that is the **inductive step**.

$V$ contains every counting number — that is the **conclusion**.

**Axiom 9 is the principle of mathematical induction.** Chapter 6 taught it as a proof technique; here it turns out to be one of the axioms that define what a counting number *is*.

To prove a claim about every counting number, collect into a box all the numbers the claim is true for. Showing the base case and the step is exactly showing that box is inductive — and Axiom 9 then says the box contains every counting number.

<Warning title="Induction is not a trick you were taught">
It is easy to think of induction as a clever method somebody invented. It is not.

It is the axiom that says the counting numbers contain **nothing but** $0$ and its successors. If induction failed, there would be rogue numbers you could never reach — and the fact that there are none is exactly what induction says.
</Warning>

<Aside>
This is why induction cannot be proved from the other axioms. Module 7.4's rogue model satisfies Axioms 5–8, and in it induction *fails* — the set $\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ is inductive but does not contain $a$.

So no argument from Axioms 5–8 can prove induction, because there is a structure where those axioms hold and induction does not.
</Aside>
