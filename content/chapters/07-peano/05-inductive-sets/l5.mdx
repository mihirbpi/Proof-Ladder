Module 7.4 left a gap. The rogue structure — the counting numbers plus two extra elements $a$ and $b$ pointing at each other forever — satisfies every axiom so far, and it is not what we mean by the counting numbers.

The extras are unreachable from $0$. No amount of counting gets you there.

So the missing axiom should say: **nothing is in $\mathbb{N}$ except what counting reaches.**

## Inductive sets

> A set $V$ is **inductive** when $0 \in V$, and $x \in V$ always implies $S(x) \in V$.

An inductive set is one you cannot fall out of by counting. It holds $0$, and it never lets go of a successor.

> **A9.** If $V$ is inductive, then $\mathbb{N} \subseteq V$.

So $\mathbb{N}$ sits inside **every** inductive set. It is the smallest one.

<Warning title="Inductive sets are usually far bigger than the counting numbers">
A9 does not say $\mathbb{N}$ is the only inductive set. Plenty of sets are inductive — anything containing all the numerals and closed under $S$ qualifies, however much else it holds.

What A9 says is that $\mathbb{N}$ is contained in all of them, which is a claim about $\mathbb{N}$ having **nothing extra**, not about the others being small.
</Warning>

## Excluding the rogues

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The rogue structure contains the numerals plus $a$ and $b$. A9 applies to any inductive set we can produce.</div>
  <div slot="want">To show $a$ and $b$ cannot be in $\mathbb{N}$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Produce an inductive set that leaves them out — the numerals alone will do. A9 then squeezes $\mathbb{N}$ inside it.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>

*The symbols in this proof: $\in$ is "is in", $\notin$ is "is not in", $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers. In words, what follows shows: the reachable part satisfies every earlier axiom, so Axiom 9 is what rules the extras out.*

Let $V = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$, the numerals reachable from $0$ by counting.

$V$ is inductive: $0 \in V$, and if $x \in V$ is reached in $m$ steps then $S(x)$ is reached in $m+1$, so $S(x) \in V$.

By A9, $\mathbb{N} \subseteq V$. Since $a \notin V$, we get $a \notin \mathbb{N}$, and likewise for $b$.
</Proof>

Combining with module 7.4, which gave the containment the other way:

$$
\{0,1,2,\ldots\} \subseteq \mathbb{N} \quad\text{(A5, A6)}, \qquad
\mathbb{N} \subseteq \{0,1,2,\ldots\} \quad\text{(A9)},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers, $\subseteq$ is "sits inside". So the line reads: each sits inside the other, so the two are the same collection — the double-inclusion move of §2.6.*

$$
\text{so}\qquad \mathbb{N} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers.*

**A set equality proved by double inclusion** (module 2.6), with the two directions coming from opposite ends of the axiom list. Nine axioms in, the counting numbers are finally pinned down exactly.

## A9 *is* induction

Now look at what A9 says when $V$ is the set of numbers making some statement $A$ true — that is, $V = \{n \in \mathbb{N} : A(n)\}$.

| Inductive set condition | Induction |
|---|---|
| $0 \in V$ | base case $A(0)$ |
| $x \in V \Rightarrow S(x) \in V$ | inductive step $A(k) \Rightarrow A(k+1)$ |
| $\mathbb{N} \subseteq V$ | $A(n)$ for every $n$ |

**They are the same statement.** Chapter 6's proof technique is Chapter 7's ninth axiom, written in different notation.

This answers the question module 6.1 left open. Induction cannot be proved from more basic facts about $\mathbb{N}$, **because it is one of the facts that define $\mathbb{N}$.**

<Warning title="Induction is genuinely an axiom, not a theorem">
Module 7.4's rogue structure satisfies A5–A8 and **violates A9**: there, $V = \{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ is inductive but does not contain $a$.

By the model method of module 7.1, that settles it. No derivation of A9 from A5–A8 can exist, because a derivation would hold in every structure satisfying A5–A8, and here is one where A9 fails.

**Induction is an assumption.** A well-chosen one, and an assumption.
</Warning>

<Aside>
This also explains why induction feels like it ought to need justifying, and never gets any.

Module 6.1's dominoes are not a proof of induction. They are a **picture of what the axiom says**, which is a different thing.

Asking why all the dominoes fall is asking why $\mathbb{N}$ has no rogue elements sitting off the chain. And the answer is that we declared it so — because a number system with rogue elements is not the one we meant to describe.
</Aside>

## The nine axioms

| # | Statement |
|---|---|
| 1–3 | $=$ is reflexive, symmetric, transitive |
| 4 | equals may be substituted for equals |
| 5 | $0 \in \mathbb{N}$ |
| 6 | $x \in \mathbb{N} \Rightarrow S(x) \in \mathbb{N}$ |
| 7 | $S(x) \neq 0$ |
| 8 | $S$ is injective |
| 9 | induction |

That is the entire foundation of arithmetic. Modules 7.6 and 7.7 build addition and multiplication on it, and everything Chapter 4 assumed becomes provable.
