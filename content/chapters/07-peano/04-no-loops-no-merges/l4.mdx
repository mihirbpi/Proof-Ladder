## Axioms 7 and 8

$$
\textbf{A7}\quad \forall x \in \mathbb{N},\ S(x)\neq 0
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers, $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every". So the line reads: nothing has zero as its next number, and two numbers with the same next number must have been the same number.*

$$
\textbf{A8}\quad \forall x,y \in \mathbb{N},\ S(x)=S(y)\Rightarrow x=y
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers, $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces". So the line reads: for any two counting numbers: if the next numbers after them are equal, then they were equal to begin with.*

In Chapter 3's language:

- **A7:** $0 \notin \operatorname{Im}(S)$; equivalently nothing at all lands on $0$ (module 3.3).
- **A8:** $S$ is **injective** (module 3.5) — and note it is stated in the contrapositive form, which module 3.5 identified as the usable one.

Together, $S : \mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$ is injective (no two inputs share an output, §3.5).

Read informally, A7 says counting never comes back to the start and A8 says counting never merges two numbers into one. Those are exactly the two ways a chain of arrows can fail to be a line: it can close into a loop, or two strands can join. Rule both out and what is left has no choice but to run forever in one direction.

## Why each is needed: bad models

To show an axiom is independent of the others, exhibit a structure satisfying the others and violating it (module 7.1).

**$M_1$ violates A7.** Take $M_1 = \{0,1\}$, $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=0$.

A5 ✓, A6 ✓. A8 ✓ — $S$ is injective (no two inputs share an output, §3.5), being a bijection of a two-element set. A7 ✗: $0 = S(1)$.

So A7 is not derivable from A5, A6, A8.

**$M_2$ violates A8.** Take $M_2 = \{0,1,2\}$ with $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=2$, $S(2)=2$.

<Figure alt="Three dots on the left with arrows, two of which land on the same dot." caption="$M_2$: satisfies A5, A6, A7; violates A8.">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="M₂" labelRight="M₂"
    left={["0", "1", "2"]} right={["0", "1", "2"]}
    edges={[[0,1],[1,2],[2,2]]} />

A5 ✓, A6 ✓, A7 ✓ — nothing maps to $0$. A8 ✗: $S(1)=S(2)=2$ with $1\neq2$.

So A8 is not derivable from A5, A6, A7.

Each axiom therefore does work no other axiom does. This is what an independence argument looks like, and it is why the models are worth constructing rather than merely described.

### Why models, and not effort

The method generalizes, and it is the only method available. You cannot show an axiom is unprovable by failing to prove it — that is a fact about your effort. A model that satisfies the others and breaks the one in question settles the matter permanently, because any derivation would have to hold in every structure where the premises hold, and here is one where it does not.

## Consequence: the numerals are distinct

> **Proposition.** Under A5–A8, the elements $0, S(0), S^2(0), S^3(0), \ldots$ are pairwise distinct.

<Proof>
Suppose $S^m(0) = S^n(0)$ with $m < n$. Applying A8 repeatedly $m$ times cancels $m$ successors from each side, giving
$$0 = S^{\,n-m}(0),$$
with $n - m \geq 1$. But then $0 = S\big(S^{\,n-m-1}(0)\big)$ is a successor, contradicting A7.

So no two are equal.
</Proof>

Note what the two axioms contributed: A8 supplied the cancellation and A7 supplied the contradiction. Remove either and the argument fails — as the models above show it must.

The proof is also a good example of choosing the right shape. The claim is that infinitely many things are pairwise distinct, which is a universal statement about pairs and cannot be checked. Assuming two of them equal turns it into a single equation you can manipulate, and A8 is exactly a tool for manipulating such equations. That is module 1.8's advice about negative claims, applied here.

So $\{0,1,2,3,\ldots\} \subseteq \mathbb{N}$, with all elements distinct, and $\mathbb{N}$ is infinite.

<Warning title="Not yet enough">
A5–A8 force $\mathbb{N}$ to *contain* $0,1,2,\ldots$ distinctly. They do not force it to contain *nothing else*.

Consider $\mathbb{N}' = \{0,1,2,\ldots\}\cup\{a, b\}$ with the usual successor on the numerals, plus $S(a)=b$ and $S(b)=a$.

A5 ✓. A6 ✓. A7 ✓ — neither $a$ nor $b$ maps to $0$. A8 ✓ — $S$ is still injective (no two inputs share an output, §3.5) on the whole thing.

All four axioms hold, and the structure has two rogue elements cycling forever off to one side, unreachable from $0$.

Module 7.5's Axiom 9 is exactly what excludes this.
</Warning>

<Aside>
A7 and A8 together say $S$ is a bijection from $\mathbb{N}$ onto $\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$ — injective by A8, and surjective (every possible output is actually hit, §3.6) onto that set once A9 rules out elements outside the chain.

So $\mathbb{N}$ is in bijection with a **proper subset** of itself, which module 3.8 gave as Dedekind's definition of an infinite set. The infinitude of $\mathbb{N}$ is not an extra assumption; it is a consequence of these two axioms.
</Aside>
