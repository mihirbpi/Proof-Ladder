$$
\textbf{A7}\quad \forall x,\ S(x) \neq 0; \qquad\qquad
\textbf{A8}\quad \forall x,y,\ S(x)=S(y)\Rightarrow x=y.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\forall$ is "for every", $S(x)$ is the next number after $x$, $\neq$ is "is not", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces". So the line reads: A7 says nothing at all has zero as its next number, so counting never comes back to the start; A8 says two numbers sharing a next number were the same number to begin with, so counting never merges two into one.*

In Chapter 3's language: nothing at all is an output of $S$ except numbers other than $0$ (§3.3), and $S$ is **injective** (§3.5) — never sending two different numbers to the same place. Both words are recent, so hold onto what they are doing here: the first says counting never returns to the start, the second that it never merges.

By the method of §7.1, a structure in which the remaining axioms hold and the one in question fails shows that one is not derivable from the others. So neither A7 nor A8 is redundant, and both earn their place.

More generally, A5 and A6 alone permit *any* set with a marked starting point and *any* rule for taking the next element. A7 and A8 cut that down to the structures in which counting from $0$ runs forward forever without looping or merging.

## What each axiom forbids

Axioms 5 and 6 guarantee the chain $0, S(0), S(S(0)),\dots$ exists. They do not guarantee it behaves, and three unwanted systems survive them.

**Looping.** $\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2\}$ with $S(2) = 0$. Both axioms hold: $0$ is present, and every element has a successor in the set. The numbers cycle forever and nothing new is produced.

**Merging.** A system with $S(3) = S(5)$. Both axioms hold, and the chain forks and rejoins, so "the number before $6$" names two things.

**Unreachable extras.** $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ together with a disjoint second chain $a, S(a), S(S(a)), \dots$ where $a$ is not a successor of anything in the first chain. Both axioms hold, and half the system is unreachable by counting.

Axiom 7 kills the first, Axiom 8 the second, and Axiom 9 the third (§7.5).

**That is what an axiom is for.** Each one eliminates models, and the way to find out which axioms you need is to construct the systems you want to exclude and add exactly the assumptions that exclude them.

## Both axioms are needed

Neither implies the other, and exhibiting a system satisfying one and failing the other is what proves it.

**A7 without A8.** Take $\{0,1,2,3\}$ with $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=2$, $S(2)=3$, $S(3)=3$. Nothing maps to $0$, so A7 holds. But $S(2) = S(3)$ fails injectivity (§3.5), and the chain runs into a fixed point and stops.

**A8 without A7.** Take the twelve-hour clock, $\{0,\dots,11\}$ with $S$ the next hour, wrapping. $S$ is injective (§3.5) so A8 holds. But $S(11) = 0$, so A7 fails — and the result is the remainder arithmetic of §4.4, which is consistent, useful, and not the counting numbers.

**Two independent axioms, two independent failures.** This is how you show an axiom is not redundant, and it is exactly the method that finally settled the parallel postulate: two thousand years of attempted derivations ended when someone constructed a geometry satisfying the other four postulates and violating the fifth.

Each dropped axiom gives a real subject rather than nonsense. Drop A7 and you get remainder arithmetic; drop A8 and you get the tail-and-loop structures that finite-state machines are built from.

## The numerals are distinct

Write $S^m(0)$ for *step $m$ times starting from $0$* — so $S^0(0)$ is $0$, $S^1(0)$ is $1$, $S^2(0)$ is $2$, and so on. The claim is that this never revisits a number it has already produced.

> **Proposition.** Under A5–A8, stepping $m$ times and stepping $n$ times land on different numbers whenever $m \neq n$.

<Proof>

*The symbols in this proof: $S^m(0)$ means step $m$ times from $0$. In words, what follows shows: if two different step-counts landed on the same number, you could strip steps off both sides until one of them was $0$ — and $0$ is a place no step can land.*

Suppose stepping $m$ times and stepping $n$ times gave the same number, with $m$ the smaller.

Strip one step off both sides. A8 says that is legal: two numbers with the same next number were equal to begin with. Do it $m$ times, and the left-hand side is worn down to $0$, while the right still has $n-m$ steps left — at least one.

So $0$ is the result of taking at least one step. But A7 says nothing at all steps to $0$. Contradiction. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

A8 supplied the cancellation, A7 the contradiction. The two models above show that neither contribution can be dispensed with.

So each step-count gives a number no other step-count gives, and there are as many numbers as there are step-counts: $\mathbb{N}$ is infinite.

There is a second route to the same conclusion, and it is worth seeing. $S$ never repeats an output (§3.5), and it never outputs $0$ — so it matches $\mathbb{N}$ one-for-one against $\mathbb{N}$ *with $0$ removed*, a part of itself that leaves something out. That is §3.8's test for being **infinite**. That was proposed as the *definition* of infinity in 1888, and A7–A8 are exactly the axioms making $\mathbb{N}$ satisfy it.

<Warning title="A5 to A8 do not pin down the counting numbers">
Let $\mathbb{N}' = \{0,1,2,\ldots\} \cup \{a,b\}$ with the standard successor on the numerals and $S(a)=b$, $S(b)=a$.

A5 ✓, A6 ✓, A7 ✓ (neither $a$ nor $b$ maps to $0$), A8 ✓ ($S$ is injective (§3.5) throughout).

So a model may contain a full copy of $\mathbb{N}$ *plus* extra components disconnected from $0$. A5–A8 constrain the component of $0$ and say nothing about the rest.

§7.5's Axiom 9 is exactly the smallest-possible condition excluding these.
</Warning>

<Aside>
It is worth appreciating how little A5–A8 pin down. They force $0$ and everything reachable from it by repeated $S$, and they force that part to be an unending chain with no loops and no merges — but they say nothing whatever about elements sitting off to one side, out of $0$'s reach.

The rogue pair $\{a, b\}$ above is the smallest illustration. Nothing stops you attaching a hundred such pairs, or an entire second chain running alongside the first. Every one of those is a legitimate model of A5–A8, which is exactly why a ninth axiom is needed and why it has to be a statement about *reachability* rather than one more rule about $S$.
</Aside>

Axioms 7 and 8 are statements about $S$ as a function, in the vocabulary of Chapter 3, and reading them that way is what makes them memorable.

$$\text{Axiom 7:}\ \ S^{-1}(\{0\}) = \emptyset \qquad \text{Axiom 8:}\ \ S \text{ is one-to-one}$$

*Symbol by symbol: $S^{-1}(\{0\})$ is the collection of inputs sent to $0$ (§3.3), and $\emptyset$ is the empty set. So the line reads: nothing has $0$ as its next number, and no two numbers share a next number.*

*So the line reads: nothing has $0$ as its next, and no two things share a next.*

So $S$ is injective (§3.5) and not surjective — so not a bijection, so not invertible. **There is no predecessor function on $\mathbb{N}$**, because $0$ has no predecessor, and that single fact is why $\mathbb{N}$ is not closed under subtraction and why Chapter 4 had to build $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Each axiom is shown non-redundant by a counter-model.** Drop Axiom 7 and the clock arithmetic of §4.4 satisfies everything else. Drop Axiom 8 and you get a chain running into a loop. Exhibiting such a system is how you prove an axiom cannot be derived from the others — the same method that settled the parallel postulate after two thousand years of failed derivations.

**In calculus, the analogous constructions are the badly behaved functions.** $|x|$ shows continuity does not imply differentiability; $\sin(1/x)$ shows a limit can fail without tending to infinity; and there is a famous curve that is unbroken everywhere and yet has a corner at every single point, so it has a steepness nowhere. Each exists to prove that a plausible implication is not a theorem, and each is as much a part of the subject as the theorems.

</WhereThisGoes>
