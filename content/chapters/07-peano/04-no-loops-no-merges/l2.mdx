Module 7.3 gave two rules: zero is a number, and every number has a next one.

That is not enough! Here are two machines that follow both rules and are still wrong.

## Broken machine 1: the loop

<Figure alt="Two dots labeled 0 and 1, with an arrow from 0 to 1 and an arrow from 1 back to 0." caption="A counting machine that loops back to the start.">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="numbers" labelRight="next one"
    left={["0", "1"]} right={["0", "1"]}
    edges={[[0,1],[1,0]]} />
</Figure>

Zero is a number ✓. Every number has a next one ✓.

But count with it: $0, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, \ldots$

It **loops**. And real counting never loops — you never count back to zero.

**The fix.** Add a rule:

> **Nothing has $0$ as its next one.**

Zero is the start, and nothing points back at it. That kills the loop.

## Broken machine 2: the squish

<Figure alt="Three dots labeled 0, 1 and 2 on the left, with arrows from both 1 and 2 landing on the same dot." caption="Two different numbers sharing one next one.">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="numbers" labelRight="next one"
    left={["0", "1", "2"]} right={["1", "2", "3"]}
    edges={[[0,0],[1,2],[2,2]]} />
</Figure>

Look at the arrows. Both $1$ and $2$ point at the same place!

That is a **squish**. Two different numbers, one shared next one.

Real counting never squishes. Every number has its own next one, all to itself.

**The fix.** Add another rule:

> **Two different numbers never have the same next one.**

## Both rules, together

Now the machine cannot loop and cannot squish. It has to march off in a straight line forever:

$$0 \to 1 \to 2 \to 3 \to 4 \to \ldots$$

Never coming home. Never joining up. Just going.

<Warning title="Both rules are needed">
Take away the no-loop rule and the first broken machine sneaks back in.

Take away the no-squish rule and the second one does.

Neither rule can do the other's job — that is exactly why both are on the list.
</Warning>
