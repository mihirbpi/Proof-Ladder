Module 7.3 left three unwanted systems standing. Two axioms kill two of them.

> **Axiom 7.** For every $x\in\mathbb{N}$: $S(x)\neq 0$.
>
> **Axiom 8.** For all $x,y\in\mathbb{N}$: if $S(x)=S(y)$, then $x = y$.

## Axiom 7 stops the loop

Without it, a system could wrap around. Take $\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2\}$ with

$$S(0)=1,\quad S(1)=2,\quad S(2)=0.$$

Every axiom so far holds — $0$ is present, every element has a successor in the set. And the numbers cycle forever, never producing anything new.

Axiom 7 says $0$ is nobody's successor, so no arrow can point back to it. **The chain has a genuine beginning.**

This is not an idle worry. The clock arithmetic of module 4.4 is exactly such a system: on a $12$-hour clock, the successor of $11$ is $0$. That arithmetic is perfectly consistent and it is not the counting numbers, and Axiom 7 is what distinguishes them.

## Axiom 8 stops the merge

Without it, two different numbers could share a successor:

$$S(3) = S(5) = 6.$$

Then the chain has a fork joining back together, and counting backwards becomes ambiguous — *"the number before $6$"* would name two things.

In the language of Chapter 3, Axiom 8 is the **contrapositive form of injectivity**:

$$S(x)=S(y) \Rightarrow x=y$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces". So the line reads: if two numbers have the same next number, they were the same number all along.*

which is exactly module 3.5's working definition. **Axiom 8 says the successor function is injective.**

And Axiom 7 says nothing at all is sent to $0$ — in module 3.3's notation, the pre-image of $0$ is empty:
$$S^{-1}(\{0\}) = \emptyset,$$

*Symbol by symbol: $S^{-1}(\{0\})$ is the collection of inputs $S$ sends to $0$, and $\emptyset$ is the empty set. So the line reads: there is nothing whose next number is $0$.* Both axioms are statements about $S$ as a function, using vocabulary the course proved four chapters ago.

<Warning title="No two inputs share a next, and nothing is everyone's next">
$S$ is injective by Axiom 8. It is **not surjective** — not every possible output is hit (§3.6) — by Axiom 7: nothing maps to $0$.

So $S$ is not a bijection, and by module 3.9 it has no inverse function. There is no "predecessor function" on all of $\mathbb{N}$, because $0$ has no predecessor.

That is the exact reason $\mathbb{N}$ is not closed under subtraction, and it is why Chapter 4 had to build $\mathbb{Z}$. The closure failure you met there is this axiom, seen from the other end.
</Warning>

## A consequence

What follows is a **proposition** — a result worth stating on its own, though smaller than a theorem.

> **Proposition.** $1 \neq 0$.

<Proof>
By definition $1 = S(0)$. By Axiom 7, $S(x)\neq0$ for every $x$; taking $x=0$ gives $S(0)\neq0$.

So $1\neq0$.
</Proof>

Two lines to prove $1\neq0$. That is what building from axioms costs, and it is also what it buys: the fact now rests on something, rather than on its being obvious.

> **Proposition.** $2\neq1$.

<Proof>
Suppose $2 = 1$, that is, $S(1) = S(0)$.

By Axiom 8, $1 = 0$ — contradicting the previous proposition.

So $2 \neq 1$.
</Proof>

The same argument gives $3\neq2$, $4\neq3$, and so on. **All the numbers are distinct**, and the general claim is proved by induction — Axiom 9, coming in module 7.5.

## Both axioms are needed

Neither implies the other, and the way to see it is to build a system satisfying one and failing the other.

**Axiom 7 without Axiom 8.** Take $\{0,1,2,3\}$ with $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=2$, $S(2)=3$, $S(3)=3$. Nothing maps to $0$ ✓, so Axiom 7 holds. But $S(2)=S(3)$, so $S$ is not injective (§3.5) — Axiom 8 fails, and the chain runs into a fixed point and stops.

**Axiom 8 without Axiom 7.** Take the clock $\{0,1,\dots,11\}$ with $S(x)$ the next hour, wrapping. $S$ is injective (§3.5) ✓, so Axiom 8 holds. But $S(11)=0$, so Axiom 7 fails.

**Two independent axioms, two independent failures.** That is how you show an axiom is not redundant: exhibit a system where everything else holds and it does not.

The same technique settled the parallel postulate. For two thousand years people tried to *derive* it; what finally worked was constructing a geometry where the other four postulates hold and the fifth fails — which proves no derivation can exist.

## The one still standing

Axioms 7 and 8 kill loops and merges. They do **not** kill extras.

Consider a system containing $0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$ **and** a second, separate chain
$$a,\ S(a),\ S(S(a)),\ \dots$$
that never touches the first, with $a$ not a successor of anything in either chain.

Check the axioms. $0$ is there ✓. Every element has a successor ✓. Nothing has $0$ as its successor ✓. $S$ is injective (§3.5) ✓.

**Every axiom holds, and the system is not $\mathbb{N}$.** The elements of the second chain are unreachable by counting from $0$, and no axiom so far forbids them.

That is what Axiom 9 is for, and it turns out to be the induction principle of Chapter 6.

<Aside>
Which axiom is doing what becomes visible when you ask what fails without it.

**Drop Axiom 7:** you get remainder arithmetic — the residue systems of module 4.4, and the symmetry group of a regular polygon from module 4.4's closing note. Consistent, useful, not the counting numbers.

**Drop Axiom 8:** you get systems shaped like the letter $\rho$ — a tail leading into a loop. These are exactly what a finite-state machine looks like, and computer scientists study them.

**Drop Axiom 9:** you get $\mathbb{N}$ together with extra chains, called **non-standard models of arithmetic**. Skolem constructed one in 1934, and they are genuinely strange: they contain "numbers" larger than every ordinary number, and they satisfy every first-order statement true of $\mathbb{N}$.

Each dropped axiom gives a real subject. This is the geometry situation from module 7.1 all over again: an axiom is not a description of the only possibility, and dropping it opens a door rather than breaking anything.
</Aside>
