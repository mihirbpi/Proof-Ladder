$$
\textbf{A7}\quad \forall x,\ S(x) \neq 0; \qquad\qquad
\textbf{A8}\quad \forall x,y,\ S(x)=S(y)\Rightarrow x=y.
$$

Structurally: $0 \notin \operatorname{Im}(S)$ (§3.3) and $S$ is injective (§3.5). Together $S$ is an injection $\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$.

By the method of §7.1, a structure in which the remaining axioms hold and the one in question fails shows that one is not derivable from the others. So neither A7 nor A8 is redundant, and both earn their place.

More generally, A5 and A6 alone permit *any* set with a marked starting point and *any* rule for taking the next element. A7 and A8 cut that down to the structures in which counting from $0$ runs forward forever without looping or merging.

## What each axiom forbids

Axioms 5 and 6 guarantee the chain $0, S(0), S(S(0)),\dots$ exists. They do not guarantee it behaves, and three unwanted systems survive them.

**Looping.** $\mathbb{N} = \{0,1,2\}$ with $S(2) = 0$. Both axioms hold: $0$ is present, and every element has a successor in the set. The numbers cycle forever and nothing new is produced.

**Merging.** A system with $S(3) = S(5)$. Both axioms hold, and the chain forks and rejoins, so "the number before $6$" names two things.

**Unreachable extras.** $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ together with a disjoint second chain $a, S(a), S(S(a)), \dots$ where $a$ is not a successor of anything in the first chain. Both axioms hold, and half the system is unreachable by counting.

Axiom 7 kills the first, Axiom 8 the second, and Axiom 9 the third (§7.5).

**That is what an axiom is for.** Each one eliminates models, and the way to find out which axioms you need is to construct the systems you want to exclude and add exactly the assumptions that exclude them.

## Both axioms are needed

Neither implies the other, and exhibiting a system satisfying one and failing the other is what proves it.

**A7 without A8.** Take $\{0,1,2,3\}$ with $S(0)=1$, $S(1)=2$, $S(2)=3$, $S(3)=3$. Nothing maps to $0$, so A7 holds. But $S(2) = S(3)$ fails injectivity, and the chain runs into a fixed point and stops.

**A8 without A7.** Take the twelve-hour clock, $\{0,\dots,11\}$ with $S$ the next hour, wrapping. $S$ is injective, so A8 holds. But $S(11) = 0$, so A7 fails — and the result is the remainder arithmetic of §4.4, which is consistent, useful, and not the counting numbers.

**Two independent axioms, two independent failures.** This is how you show an axiom is not redundant, and it is exactly the method that finally settled the parallel postulate: two thousand years of attempted derivations ended when someone constructed a geometry satisfying the other four postulates and violating the fifth.

Each dropped axiom yields a real subject rather than nonsense. Drop A7 and you get remainder arithmetic; drop A8 and you get the tail-and-loop structures that finite-state machines are built from.

## The numerals are distinct

> **Proposition.** Under A5–A8, $S^m(0) \neq S^n(0)$ for $m \neq n$.

<Proof>
Suppose $S^m(0)=S^n(0)$ with $m<n$. Applying A8 $m$ times cancels, giving $0 = S^{\,n-m}(0)$ with $n-m\geq 1$. Then $0 = S\big(S^{\,n-m-1}(0)\big)$ is a successor, contradicting A7.
</Proof>

A8 supplied the cancellation, A7 the contradiction. The two models above show that neither contribution can be dispensed with.

Consequently $n \mapsto S^n(0)$ is an injection $\mathbb{N}_{\text{meta}} \hookrightarrow \mathbb{N}$, and $\mathbb{N}$ is infinite.

Moreover $S : \mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}$ is injective but not surjective (its image omits $0$), so $\mathbb{N}$ is in bijection with the proper subset $\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}$ — i.e. **Dedekind infinite** (§3.8). Dedekind took this as the *definition* of infinity in 1888, and A7–A8 are exactly the axioms making $\mathbb{N}$ satisfy it.

<Warning title="A5 to A8 do not pin down the counting numbers">
Let $\mathbb{N}' = \{0,1,2,\ldots\} \cup \{a,b\}$ with the standard successor on the numerals and $S(a)=b$, $S(b)=a$.

A5 ✓, A6 ✓, A7 ✓ (neither $a$ nor $b$ maps to $0$), A8 ✓ ($S$ is injective throughout).

So a model may contain a full copy of $\mathbb{N}$ *plus* extra components disconnected from $0$. A5–A8 constrain the component of $0$ and say nothing about the rest.

§7.5's Axiom 9 is precisely the smallest-possible condition excluding these. The instructive point is that no *finitely many* first-order axioms of this kind can do the job — see below.
</Warning>

<Aside>
A7 and A8 hold in models with rogue elements, and A9 will exclude them — but only in its **second-order** form, quantifying over all subsets. The weaker version — one induction axiom for each formula you can write down — cannot: by building a model, adding a constant $c$ and the axioms $c \neq \overline n$ for every numeral $\overline n$ yields a consistent theory, hence a model containing an element exceeding every numeral.

These are arithmetic's **impostors**. They satisfy every theorem expressible in the restricted language of $\mathbb{N}$ and are not the same up to renaming to it — so the notion "natural number" is not first-order definable. Skolem constructed the first in 1934.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Axioms 7 and 8 are statements about $S$ as a function, in the vocabulary of Chapter 3, and reading them that way is what makes them memorable.

$$\text{Axiom 7:}\ \ S^{-1}(\{0\}) = \emptyset \qquad \text{Axiom 8:}\ \ S \text{ is injective}$$

So $S$ is injective and not surjective — hence not a bijection, hence not invertible. **There is no predecessor function on $\mathbb{N}$**, because $0$ has no predecessor, and that single fact is why $\mathbb{N}$ is not closed under subtraction and why Chapter 4 had to build $\mathbb{Z}$.

**Each axiom is shown non-redundant by a counter-model.** Drop Axiom 7 and the clock arithmetic of §4.4 satisfies everything else. Drop Axiom 8 and you get a chain running into a loop. Exhibiting such a system is how you prove an axiom cannot be derived from the others — the same method that settled the parallel postulate after two thousand years of failed derivations.

**In calculus, the analogous constructions are the pathological functions.** $|x|$ shows continuity does not imply differentiability; $\sin(1/x)$ shows a limit can fail without tending to infinity; the Weierstrass function shows continuity everywhere is compatible with differentiability nowhere. Each exists to prove that a plausible implication is not a theorem, and each is as much a part of the subject as the theorems.
