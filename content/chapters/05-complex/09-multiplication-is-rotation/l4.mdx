## The claim

> Multiplying every point of the plane by a fixed complex number **turns the plane and stretches it**, and does nothing else.

That is the chapter's payoff, and this module establishes as much of it as the tools allow.

## The part that is proved

Multiplying by $i$:

$$i\cdot 1 = i, \qquad i\cdot i = -1, \qquad i\cdot(-1) = -i, \qquad i\cdot(-i) = 1.$$

Each of those four points moves a quarter turn counterclockwise. Take a general point and the same thing happens:

<Proof>
Let $z = a+bi$. Then
$$i\,z = i(a+bi) = ai + bi^2 = -b + ai.$$

So the point $(a,b)$ moves to the point $(-b, a)$.

That is exactly the quarter-turn rule from your coordinate geometry: a quarter turn counterclockwise about the origin takes $(x,y)$ to $(-y,x)$. Every point obeys it, so multiplying by $i$ turns the whole plane a quarter turn. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

**So $i^2 = -1$ reads: two quarter turns make a half turn.** The equation the chapter opened with, the one that looked like an arbitrary decree, is a statement about turning that a five-year-old can act out — and this course's Kindergarten version does exactly that.

Two more cases, both provable the same way:

- **Multiplying by a positive real $r$** turns $a+bi$ into $ra+rbi$, which stretches every distance by $r$ and turns nothing.
- **Multiplying by $-1$** turns $(a,b)$ into $(-a,-b)$: a half turn.

## The part that is not yet proved

The general claim needs turns of any size, not just quarters and halves, and naming the point you land on after an arbitrary turn takes a tool this course does not assume.

What can be checked now is the **distance** half. Module 5.6 proved

$$|zw| = |z|\,|w|,$$

with algebra you can follow line by line. So multiplying by $w$ certainly multiplies every distance from the origin by $|w|$. That is the stretching, established.

The turning is what waits. Module 5.8 stated the rule — multiply the distances, add the turns — and the higher levels of this module prove it.

<Warning title="Rotation about the origin only">
Multiplying rotates about $0$, not about any other point. To turn the plane about a different centre $c$, first slide $c$ to the origin, then turn, then slide back:

$$\text{new position} = w\,(z - c) + c.$$

Three transformations composed, in that order and no other — module 3.7's point about composition, arriving in a place where getting the order wrong visibly moves your figure to the wrong spot.
</Warning>

## Why this is worth having

You have two ways of describing a turn now: as a transformation of the plane, and as multiplication by a number.

Transformations are hard to compute with. **Numbers are easy to compute with**, and multiplication does all the bookkeeping automatically: turning by one amount and then another is just multiplying twice, and the answer comes out in the same form you started in.

That trade — turn a geometric question into an arithmetic one — is why complex numbers are used far outside the problem they were invented for. They were built to solve $x^2 = -1$. What they turned out to be good at was rotation.

<Aside>
The four points $1, i, -1, -i$ are the four solutions of $z^4 = 1$: raise any of them to the fourth power and you turn a full four quarter turns, landing back at $1$.

Divide the circle into $n$ equal steps instead of four and the same thing happens — the $n$ points are exactly the solutions of $z^n = 1$, sitting at the corners of a regular $n$-gon.

So a question about equations has an answer about polygons. Nobody looking at $z^n = 1$ would predict that, and finding a bridge between two subjects that had no reason to meet is one of the things mathematicians are actually chasing.
</Aside>
