## The multiplication rule

$$
\big(r_1e^{i\theta_1}\big)\big(r_2e^{i\theta_2}\big) = r_1r_2\,e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}.
$$

**Moduli multiply; arguments add.** The proof is the exponent law $e^Ae^B = e^{A+B}$ together with commutativity, and it can also be checked directly against the Cartesian formula:

<Proof>
Expanding both sides via Euler's formula:
$$
\begin{aligned}
r_1e^{i\theta_1}\cdot r_2e^{i\theta_2}
&= r_1r_2(\cos\theta_1 + i\sin\theta_1)(\cos\theta_2 + i\sin\theta_2)\\
&= r_1r_2\big[(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2) + i(\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2 + \sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2)\big]\\
&= r_1r_2\big[\cos(\theta_1+\theta_2) + i\sin(\theta_1+\theta_2)\big] = r_1r_2 e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)},
\end{aligned}
$$
the third line by the addition formulas for $\cos$ and $\sin$.
</Proof>

Note what that computation shows: **the polar multiplication rule and the trigonometric addition formulas are the same fact.** Either can be derived from the other.

Two consequences already established: $|z_1z_2| = |z_1||z_2|$ (module 5.6), and $\overline{re^{i\theta}} = re^{-i\theta}$, since conjugation reflects in the real axis and so negates the angle.

## Geometric reading

Multiplication by $z = re^{i\theta}$ is: **rotate by $\theta$, then scale by $r$.**

- $|z| = 1$: pure rotation.
- $\theta = 0$, $r > 0$: pure scaling.
- $z = i$: rotation by $\pi/2$.
- $z = -1$: rotation by $\pi$.

$i^2 = -1$ now reads: *two quarter turns make a half turn*. The defining property of $i$ is a geometric identity, and every remarkable feature of $\mathbb{C}$ traces back to it.

## De Moivre's formula

Iterating the rule with $r=1$:

> **De Moivre's formula.** For $n \in \mathbb{Z}$,
> $$\big(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta\big)^n = \cos(n\theta) + i\sin(n\theta).$$

<Proof>
Equivalently $\big(e^{i\theta}\big)^n = e^{in\theta}$. For $n \geq 0$ this is induction: true at $n=0$, and if $(e^{i\theta})^k = e^{ik\theta}$ then
$$(e^{i\theta})^{k+1} = (e^{i\theta})^k e^{i\theta} = e^{ik\theta}e^{i\theta} = e^{i(k+1)\theta}$$
by the multiplication rule. For $n < 0$, note $(e^{i\theta})^{-1} = e^{-i\theta}$ since their product is $e^0 = 1$.
</Proof>

## The payoff: multiple-angle formulas

Comparing De Moivre's two sides and equating real and imaginary parts (module 5.2) produces trigonometric identities on demand.

**$n = 2$:**
$$
\cos2\theta + i\sin2\theta = (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^2 = (\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta) + i(2\sin\theta\cos\theta),
$$
so
$$
\cos2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta, \qquad \sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta.
$$

**$n = 3$:** expanding $(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^3$ gives
$$
\cos3\theta = \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta, \qquad
\sin3\theta = 3\cos^2\theta\sin\theta - \sin^3\theta.
$$

This is the moment complex numbers repay a course you have already taken. Identities that were memorised now have a one-line derivation, and **two identities come out of every computation**, because a single complex equation carries a real part and an imaginary part.

<Warning title="Moduli multiply, arguments add">
$2e^{i\pi/6}\cdot 3e^{i\pi/4} = 6e^{i(\pi/6+\pi/4)} = 6e^{5i\pi/12}$. The arguments are added, never multiplied.

Also, $\arg$ adds only **up to whole turns** (module 5.8): principal arguments can sum past $\pi$ and need reducing.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Setting $z^n = 1$ and solving in polar form gives the **$n$th roots of unity**: $z_k = e^{2\pi i k/n}$ for $k = 0, \ldots, n-1$. There are exactly $n$ of them, equally spaced around the unit circle at the vertices of a regular $n$-gon.

So a purely algebraic question — the solutions of $z^n = 1$ — has a purely geometric answer, and it is the reason ruler-and-compass constructibility of regular polygons turns out to be a question about number fields.
</Aside>
