$$
\big(r_1e^{i\theta_1}\big)\big(r_2e^{i\theta_2}\big) = r_1r_2\,e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}.
$$

Moduli multiply, arguments add. Modulus and argument are therefore **independent coordinates**: the modulus multiplies while the argument adds, and neither interferes with the other. Every non-zero complex number is determined by that pair, and multiplication acts on each half separately.

Expanding both sides via Euler's formula recovers the addition formulas for $\cos$ and $\sin$; conversely those formulas prove the rule. **The two are the same theorem**, and which is "the reason" for which is a matter of presentation, not mathematics.

Consequences: $|zw| = |z||w|$ (§5.6), $\overline{re^{i\theta}} = re^{-i\theta}$, $z^{-1} = r^{-1}e^{-i\theta}$, and $e^{a+bi} = e^a e^{ib} = e^a(\cos b + i\sin b)$.

## Geometric content

Multiplication by $z = re^{i\theta}$ is rotation by $\theta$ composed with scaling by $r$ — the map with matrix $r\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ from §5.5. The numbers with $|z|=1$ act by pure rotations, changing no distance — so the unit circle *is* the set of rotations of the plane about the origin.

$i^2 = -1$ is thereby the identity "two quarter turns make a half turn". Every distinctive fact about $\mathbb{C}$ descends from this single geometric statement.

## De Moivre

> **Theorem.** $(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i \sin n\theta$ for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

<Proof>
Equivalently $(e^{i\theta})^n = e^{in\theta}$. Induction for $n \geq 0$: the case $n = 0$ is $1 = 1$, and $(e^{i\theta})^{k+1} = (e^{i\theta})^ke^{i\theta} = e^{ik\theta}e^{i\theta} = e^{i(k+1)\theta}$ by the multiplication rule. For $n < 0$, $e^{i\theta}e^{-i\theta} = e^0 = 1$ gives $(e^{i\theta})^{-1} = e^{-i\theta}$, and the positive case applies to $-n$.
</Proof>

Equating real and imaginary parts yields, for each $n$, a pair of multiple-angle identities:
$$
\cos n\theta = \sum_{k \text{ even}} \binom nk (-1)^{k/2}\cos^{n-k}\theta\,\sin^k\theta,
$$
with the odd terms giving $\sin n\theta$. For $n=2$ and $n=3$ these are the familiar double- and triple-angle formulas; note that **each computation produces two identities**, since one complex equation carries two real ones.

The reverse direction is equally useful. From $\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2}$,
$$
\cos^n\theta = \frac{1}{2^n}\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk e^{i(n-2k)\theta},
$$
which linearises powers into sums of $\cos m\theta$ — the standard first step in integrating $\cos^n$, and the elementary case of Fourier expansion.

<Warning title="De Moivre for non-integer n">
$(e^{i\theta})^{1/n} \neq e^{i\theta/n}$ as a statement about *the* $n$th root: there are $n$ of them. Writing $(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^{1/2} = \cos(\theta/2)+i\sin(\theta/2)$ silently selects a branch and can produce sign errors — this is §5.8's branch problem in another guise.

Correct statement: $z^{1/n}$ denotes the set $\{r^{1/n}e^{i(\theta+2\pi k)/n} : k = 0,\ldots,n-1\}$.
</Warning>

## Deriving the identities

The technique is: **compute one quantity two ways, then equate real and imaginary parts** (§5.2).

For the double-angle formulas, compute $e^{2i\theta}$ both ways.

$$e^{i(2\theta)} = \cos2\theta + i\sin2\theta$$

$$\left(e^{i\theta}\right)^2 = (\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^2 = \cos^2\theta + 2i\cos\theta\sin\theta + i^2\sin^2\theta = \left(\cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta\right) + i\left(2\sin\theta\cos\theta\right)$$

Equating parts:
$$\cos2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta, \qquad \sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta.$$

**Two identities from expanding a square.** The minus sign in the cosine formula is the $i^2$, which is worth knowing — it is more reliable than any mnemonic.

The angle-addition formulas come from $e^{i(\alpha+\beta)} = e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta}$ by the same route, and the triple-angle formulas from cubing. There is no separate technique for each; there is one technique, applied at different exponents.

## Rotation about a point other than the origin

$z \mapsto wz$ rotates about $0$. To rotate about $c$, translate the centre to the origin, rotate, and translate back:

$$z \longmapsto w(z - c) + c.$$

That is a composition of three transformations, applied right to left in the order §3.7 requires — and it is the general form of a rotation-with-scaling in the plane.

Written in real coordinates the same map needs a $2\times2$ matrix, two vector additions, and four multiplications. In $\mathbb{C}$ it is one line, and the reason is that complex multiplication already encodes the rotation.

The fixed point is $c$ itself, and it is the **only** fixed point when $w \neq 1$: solving $w(z-c)+c = z$ gives $(w-1)(z-c) = 0$, so $z = c$. That matches the classification of plane isometries — a non-trivial rotation has exactly one fixed point — and here it is an algebraic consequence rather than a geometric axiom.

## Roots of unity

Solving $z^n = 1$ in polar form: $r^n e^{in\theta} = 1$ forces $r = 1$ and $n\theta \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, so
$$
\mu_n = \Big\{\, \omega_k = e^{2\pi ik/n} : k = 0,1,\ldots,n-1 \,\Big\}.
$$

Exactly $n$ solutions, at the vertices of a regular $n$-gon inscribed in the unit circle.

Multiplying two of these roots gives another, since the angles add and wrap around at $2\pi$ — the residue arithmetic of §4.4, on a circle. Every root is a power of $\omega_1$, so the whole set is generated by one rotation. Two immediate consequences:

- $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\omega_k = 0$ for $n \geq 2$ (sum a geometric series, or note the vertices of a regular polygon have centroid $0$);
- $z^n - 1 = \prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(z-\omega_k)$, the complete factorisation over $\mathbb{C}$.

<Aside>
Gauss proved at nineteen that a regular $n$-gon is constructible with ruler and compass exactly when $n$ is a power of $2$ times distinct Fermat primes — settling a question two thousand years old by translating it into one about the field $\mathbb{Q}(\mu_n)$. His construction of the regular 17-gon was the result that convinced him to become a mathematician, and he asked for it on his tombstone.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

This module's technique — **compute one thing two ways and equate real and imaginary parts** — is a general method, and the trigonometric identities are only its first application.

**Every identity in your precalculus course follows.** Angle addition, double angle, and triple angle all come from expanding $e^{i(\alpha+\beta)} = e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta}$ or $(e^{i\theta})^n = e^{in\theta}$ and separating parts. The identities are consequences of $i^2=-1$, not facts to be memorised independently, and the minus sign in $\cos(\alpha+\beta)$ is exactly where the $i^2$ went.

**Product-to-sum formulas come the same way**, and they are what make certain integrals tractable: $\int \cos 3x\cos 5x\,dx$ is elementary once the product is converted to a sum, and the conversion is a complex-exponential computation.

**In calculus, complex exponentials shorten integration.** $\int e^{ax}\cos bx\,dx$ is usually done by integrating by parts twice and solving for the integral. Written as $\operatorname{Re}\int e^{(a+bi)x}\,dx$, it is a single antiderivative followed by taking the real part — and the answer, including its awkward $\frac{a\cos bx + b\sin bx}{a^2+b^2}$ coefficient, falls out of dividing by $a+bi$.

</WhereThisGoes>
