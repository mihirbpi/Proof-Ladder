Here is what complex multiplication actually does, stated in the polar language of module 5.8.

> **To multiply two complex numbers: multiply the distances, and add the angles.**

*As in module 5.8: $r$ records how far a point is from the origin and $\theta$ (say "theta") records how far round it has turned.*

$$
(r_1, \theta_1) \cdot (r_2, \theta_2) = (r_1 r_2,\ \theta_1 + \theta_2)
$$

One line, and it replaces the four-term formula of module 5.3 entirely.

## Half of it is already proved

The distance half is module 5.6's theorem:
$$
|zw| = |z|\,|w|.
$$
That was proved with conjugates, and it needs nothing further.

The angle half — that arguments add — needs trigonometry to prove properly, and this course has not reached it. But you can **check** it in the cases you can see.

## Checking the angle rule

Module 5.5 found that multiplying by $i$ is a quarter turn. In polar terms $i$ is $(1, 90°)$, so the rule predicts that multiplying by $i$ adds $90°$ to the angle and leaves the distance alone. That is exactly a quarter turn. ✓

Now check $i^2 = -1$ against the rule:

$$
(1, 90°) \cdot (1, 90°) = (1 \cdot 1,\ 90° + 90°) = (1, 180°),
$$

and $(1, 180°)$ is the point one unit away in the direction of $180°$ — which is $-1$. ✓

**The defining property of $i$ is a statement about angles adding.** Two quarter turns make a half turn.

One more, using distances as well:
$$
2i \cdot 3i = (2, 90°)\cdot(3,90°) = (6, 180°) = -6,
$$
and directly, $2i \cdot 3i = 6i^2 = -6$. ✓

## Powers

Multiplying $z$ by itself $n$ times applies the rule $n$ times: the distance is multiplied $n$ times, and the angle is added $n$ times.

$$
z = (r, \theta) \Rightarrow z^n = (r^n,\ n\theta).
$$

This is **De Moivre's formula**. In words: **raising to the $n$th power raises the distance to the $n$th power and multiplies the angle by $n$.**

Check it on $i$: $i^4$ should be $(1^4, 4 \cdot 90°) = (1, 360°)$, which is a full turn back to $1$. And indeed $i^4 = 1$, as module 5.3 computed.

<Warning title="Rotation is about the origin, not about z">
Multiplying by a number of distance $1$ rotates the whole plane about the **origin**.

It is not a rotation about $z$, and it is not a translation. Adding a complex number slides the plane; multiplying by one turns and stretches it about the origin. Two different operations with two different pictures — module 5.5 drew the first, and this module the second.
</Warning>

## Why this matters later

Two payoffs, both out of reach until you have trigonometry, both worth knowing are coming.

**The multiple-angle identities.** Compute $z^2$ two ways — once by the polar rule and once by expanding $(a+bi)^2$ — and set the results equal. Matching the real parts and the imaginary parts separately (legal by module 5.2) produces the double-angle formulas for $\cos$ and $\sin$ in two lines. Those formulas are usually derived by a page of geometry and memorised; here they fall out of arithmetic.

**Roots.** To find a number whose cube is $8$, the rule says: find a distance whose cube is $8$, and an angle whose triple is $0°$. The distance is $2$. The angle can be $0°$, $120°$, or $240°$ — since tripling any of those gives a whole number of turns. **Three cube roots, evenly spaced around a circle**, and the polar rule found them all where the algebra would have shown you one.

<Aside>
This is the module where complex numbers stop being a repair job and start being useful.

They were introduced in module 5.1 to solve one equation nobody cared much about. What has emerged is that **complex multiplication is the arithmetic of rotation and scaling** — the two transformations your geometry course spends a term on.

So a problem about turning and resizing figures can be converted into a problem about multiplying numbers, where the tools are much better. That conversion is why complex numbers appear in electrical engineering, signal processing and quantum mechanics, none of which is about solving $x^2+1=0$.
</Aside>
