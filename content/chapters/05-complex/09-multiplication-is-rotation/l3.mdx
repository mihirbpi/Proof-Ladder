Multiplying complex numbers in grid form means expanding brackets and collecting terms (module 5.3). In polar form (module 5.8) it becomes something much simpler.

*Carried over from module 5.8: a point is named by the pair $(r, \theta)$, where $r$ is how far it is from the center and $\theta$ is how far round it has turned. The Greek letter $\theta$ is said "THAY-ta" and is just a name for the turn.*

## The rule

$$
(r_1, \theta_1) \times (r_2, \theta_2) = (r_1 \times r_2, \;\theta_1 + \theta_2).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: the little $1$ and $2$ below the letters just say "the first one" and "the second one". So the line reads: to multiply two points, multiply their distances and add their turns.*

In words:

> **Multiply the distances. Add the angles.**

That is the entire rule for multiplication.

## Check it on the simplest case

Multiplying by $i$ means multiplying by the number at distance $1$, angle a quarter turn.

- Distance: multiply by $1$ — unchanged.
- Angle: add a quarter turn.

So multiplying by $i$ **rotates by a quarter turn and does nothing else**. That is exactly what module 5.5 found by plotting points.

And $i \times i$: distances $1 \times 1 = 1$, angles a quarter plus a quarter equals a half. Distance $1$ at a half turn is $-1$. ✓

**So $i \times i = -1$ is a statement about turning.** It was never an arbitrary rule.

## A worked example

Multiply $(2, 60°)$ by $(3, 90°)$.

- Distances: $2 \times 3 = 6$.
- Angles: $60° + 90° = 150°$.

Answer: $(6, 150°)$.

Try doing that by expanding brackets and you will appreciate the polar version.

## Why the rule is true

Proving it needs a tool that arrives in a later course, so this page will not pretend otherwise. What can be said now is what the proof comes down to: multiplying by a number at distance $1$ turns the plane without stretching it, and multiplying by a plain number at turn $0°$ stretches the plane without turning it. Every complex number is one of each, done one after the other — so distances stretch and turns add.

## Checking the rule where you already know the answer

A rule is only worth having if it agrees with what you can see. So test it on the one case you have checked by hand.

Take $i$ itself: distance $1$, a quarter turn round. Multiply it by itself.

**By the rule:** multiply the distances, $1 \times 1 = 1$. Add the angles, a quarter turn plus a quarter turn — a half turn.

**By the picture (module 5.1):** a half turn leaves you pointing backwards, which is where $-1$ lives.

So the rule says $i \times i$ sits at distance $1$, pointing backwards. That is exactly $-1$, and it is what module 5.3 told you to write.

**The rule and the picture agree.** Try the same test on $i \times i \times i$: three quarter turns, so three-quarters of the way round, still at distance $1$ — and that is $-i$.

<Warning title="Distances multiply, angles add">
Do not multiply the angles. A point at distance $2$ turned a tenth of the way round, times a point at distance $3$ turned a fifth of the way round, sits at distance $6$ turned *three tenths* of the way round — not some much larger turn.

The two components behave differently, and that asymmetry is the whole point of the rule.
</Warning>
