Multiplying complex numbers in grid form means expanding brackets and collecting terms (module 5.3). In polar form (module 5.8) it becomes something much simpler.

## The rule

$$
\big(r_1 e^{i\theta_1}\big)\big(r_2 e^{i\theta_2}\big) = (r_1 r_2)\, e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}.
$$

In words:

> **Multiply the distances. Add the angles.**

That is the entire rule for multiplication.

## Check it on the simplest case

Multiplying by $i$ means multiplying by the number at distance $1$, angle a quarter turn.

- Distance: multiply by $1$ — unchanged.
- Angle: add a quarter turn.

So multiplying by $i$ **rotates by a quarter turn and does nothing else**. That is exactly what module 5.5 found by plotting points.

And $i \times i$: distances $1 \times 1 = 1$, angles a quarter plus a quarter equals a half. Distance $1$ at a half turn is $-1$. ✓

**So $i^2 = -1$ is a statement about turning.** It was never an arbitrary rule.

## A worked example

Multiply $2e^{i \cdot 60°}$ by $3e^{i\cdot 90°}$.

- Distances: $2 \times 3 = 6$.
- Angles: $60° + 90° = 150°$.

Answer: $6e^{i\cdot 150°}$.

Try doing that by expanding brackets and you will appreciate the polar version.

## Why the rule is true

The reason is a fact about exponents that you already know:
$$
e^{A} \times e^{B} = e^{A+B}.
$$
Multiplying powers means adding the exponents. Here the exponents are $i\theta_1$ and $i\theta_2$, so they add to $i(\theta_1+\theta_2)$ — and the angles add.

The distances $r_1$ and $r_2$ are just ordinary numbers out front, so they multiply as usual.

## The payoff: a trigonometry identity for free

Take a number at distance $1$ and angle $\theta$, and square it.

<Proof>
By the rule, squaring doubles the angle and leaves the distance at $1$:
$$
\big(e^{i\theta}\big)^2 = e^{i(2\theta)}.
$$

Now write both sides out using Euler's formula (module 5.7).

**Right side:** $e^{i(2\theta)} = \cos(2\theta) + i\sin(2\theta)$.

**Left side:** $\big(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta\big)^2$. Expand it:
$$
= \cos^2\theta + 2i\cos\theta\sin\theta + i^2\sin^2\theta
= \big(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta\big) + i\big(2\cos\theta\sin\theta\big).
$$

The two sides are equal, so their real parts match and their imaginary parts match (module 5.2):
$$
\cos(2\theta) = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta, \qquad
\sin(2\theta) = 2\cos\theta\sin\theta.
$$
</Proof>

Those are the **double-angle formulas**. If you have met them before, you probably memorised them. Here they fell out of one line of algebra — and *both at once*, because one complex equation carries two real ones.

<Warning title="Distances multiply, angles add">
Do not multiply the angles. $2e^{i\cdot30°} \times 3e^{i\cdot 40°}$ is $6e^{i\cdot70°}$, not $6e^{i\cdot 1200°}$.

The two components behave differently, and that asymmetry is the whole point of the rule.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The same trick with cubes gives the triple-angle formulas, and with $n$th powers gives all of them. The general statement is called De Moivre's formula, and it means you never have to memorise a multiple-angle identity again — you can derive any of them in a minute.
</Aside>
