In module 5.8 you learned that a complex number can be described by a **distance** and a **direction**.

Here is the wonderful thing that happens when you multiply them.

## Turns add

Face east. Multiply by $i$ — that is a quarter turn, so now you face north.

Multiply by $i$ again — another quarter turn — and now you face west.

A quarter and a quarter make a half. **Turns add.**

## Distances multiply

Now what about the distances?

Start at distance $2$. Multiply by something at distance $3$.

You end up at distance $6$ — three copies of the $2$, laid end to end.

So the two parts behave differently, and each in the simplest possible way:

> **Distances multiply. Turns add.**

That is the whole rule for multiplying complex numbers, and it is much easier than expanding brackets.

## A worked one

Take the number at distance $2$ facing north. Multiply it by the number at distance $3$ facing north.

- Distances: three copies of $2$ is $6$.
- Turns: north and north is a quarter turn plus a quarter turn, which is a half turn. So the answer faces **west**.

Answer: distance $6$, facing west.

You never had to remember a single rule about $i$. You just added the turns and counted the copies.

<Warning title="Add the turns, do not multiply them">
The two parts behave differently and it is easy to mix them up.

Distances: **multiply**. Turns: **add**.

If you multiplied the turns you would get nonsense — turning by nothing would wipe out everything, since anything times zero is zero.
</Warning>

<Aside>
This is why $i$ was never really strange. $i$ is the quarter turn. Two quarter turns make a half turn. And a half turn just points you the opposite way. So the rule that defined $i$ was a fact about turning all along.
</Aside>
