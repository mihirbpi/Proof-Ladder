**Multiply the distances, add the angles.**

That is the whole chapter's payoff, and it says something about your geometry course: the two transformations you study separately — dilation and rotation — are a single complex multiplication.

## Why it holds

Both halves are already proved. The distances multiply because the modulus is multiplicative (module 5.6). The angles add because multiplying two circle points adds their angles (module 5.7).

Writing each number as a distance times a circle point and collecting the two factors separately is the whole argument.

## The geometric statement

> **Theorem.** Multiplication by a fixed non-zero $w$ is the transformation of the plane that rotates by $\arg w$ about the origin and dilates by $|w|$.

Three special cases you already know:

- $|w|=1$: a **pure rotation**. Multiplying by $\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ turns the plane through $\theta$ and changes no distance.
- $w$ real and positive: a **pure dilation**, since $\arg w = 0$.
- $w = i$: a $90°$ rotation, since $|i|=1$ and $\arg i = \pi/2$ — module 5.5's observation, now a special case.

And $w = -1$ gives a $180°$ rotation, so $i^2 = -1$ is the statement that two quarter turns make a half turn (module 5.1). Every fact about $i$ in this chapter has been the same fact about rotation.

<Warning title="Rotation about the origin only">
$z\mapsto wz$ rotates about $0$. To rotate about a different centre $c$, translate, rotate, translate back:

$$z \mapsto w(z - c) + c.$$

That is a composition of three transformations, in the order module 3.7 requires — and it is the general form of a rotation in the plane.

Your course does the same thing when it moves a figure to the origin before applying a transformation rule. The complex version is one line where the matrix version needs three.
</Warning>

## Deriving the double-angle formulas

Here is where the chapter pays a debt to a course you have already taken.

  <div slot="want">Formulas for $\cos2\theta$ and $\sin2\theta$ in terms of $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Write the circle point at angle $2\theta$ two ways — straight off, and as the square of the point at angle $\theta$ — then match the parts. Two identities from one line of algebra.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
The circle point at angle $2\theta$ is
$$\cos2\theta + i\sin2\theta.$$

By De Moivre with $n=2$, it is also the square of the point at angle $\theta$:
$$(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)^2 = \cos^2\theta + 2i\cos\theta\sin\theta + i^2\sin^2\theta$$
$$= \left(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta\right) + i\left(2\cos\theta\sin\theta\right).$$

These are equal, so their real parts and imaginary parts agree:
$$\cos2\theta = \cos^2\theta-\sin^2\theta, \qquad \sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta.$$
</Proof>

**Two identities, from expanding a square.** You memorised these in trigonometry, and here they drop out of $i^2 = -1$ and the rule that equal complex numbers have equal parts.

And this time nothing was assumed about them. Module 5.7 proved that multiplying adds the turns using **similar triangles**, not trigonometry, so these identities are being derived rather than shuffled — complex numbers repaying a debt to a course you have already taken.

The same machine keeps producing. Take $n=3$ in De Moivre, expand the cube, and the triple-angle formulas

$$\cos3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta, \qquad \sin3\theta = 3\sin\theta - 4\sin^3\theta$$

fall out of one binomial expansion — formulas nobody memorises and everybody occasionally needs.

**And the minus sign in the double-angle formula, the one everybody misplaces, is $i^2=-1$ arriving.** Knowing where a sign comes from beats knowing a mnemonic for it.

## De Moivre's formula

Repeating the multiplication rule $n$ times gives module 5.7's formula again:

$$
\left(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\right)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta.
$$

**Geometrically: rotating $n$ times through $\theta$ is rotating once through $n\theta$.** Obvious as a statement about turning, and not at all obvious as a statement about expanding a binomial to the $n$-th power.

Chapter 6 proves De Moivre properly, by induction on $n$ — the domino argument is exactly right for a claim indexed by the whole numbers.

## Roots

De Moivre run backwards solves $z^n = 1$. The solutions are the points at angles

$$\theta_k = \frac{2\pi k}{n}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1,$$

since raising the point at angle $2\pi k/n$ to the $n$th power multiplies its angle by $n$, landing on $2\pi k$ — a whole number of turns, which is $1$.

**$n$ solutions, evenly spaced on the unit circle** — the vertices of a regular $n$-gon with one vertex at $1$.

So the regular polygons of your geometry course are solution sets of polynomial equations, and their symmetry is the symmetry of multiplication: multiplying two vertices of the hexagon gives another vertex, because the angles add mod $2\pi$ — module 4.4's residue arithmetic, on a circle.

## What the chapter established

Start to finish:

1. $x^2+1$ has no real root, so extend $\mathbb{R}$ (5.1).
2. $\mathbb{C}$ is $\mathbb{R}^2$ with a multiplication, and all four operations work there (5.2, 5.3).
3. Conjugation is reflection in the real axis (5.4).
4. Points of $\mathbb{C}$ are points of the plane (5.5).
5. Modulus is the distance formula (5.6).
6. $\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ walks round the unit circle, and multiplying such points adds their angles (5.7, 5.8).
7. Multiplication is rotation plus dilation (5.9).

**An algebraic repair turned into a geometry.** The number invented to solve one equation encodes the transformation group your course spends a unit on, and the trigonometric identities you memorised are consequences of $i^2=-1$.

<Aside>
Multiplication by $w$ preserves angles between curves, even though it changes distances. A map with that property is called **conformal**, and complex multiplication is the simplest example.

Conformal maps are how the Mercator projection works: it distorts area wildly — Greenland is not the size of Africa — while preserving angles exactly, which is what a navigator needs, since a constant compass bearing must appear as a straight line.

The same functions describe fluid flowing around an obstacle and the electrical force near a charged conductor. In each case the physics is governed by an equation whose solutions are exactly the well-behaved complex functions, and problems that are hard in real coordinates become routine once the plane is treated as $\mathbb{C}$.
</Aside>
