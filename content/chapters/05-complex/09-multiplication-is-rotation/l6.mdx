$$
\big(r_1,\ \theta_1\big)\cdot\big(r_2,\ \theta_2\big) = \big(r_1r_2,\ \theta_1+\theta_2\big).
$$

**Multiply the distances, add the turns.**

That is module 5.7's theorem in the polar notation of module 5.8, and it says something about your geometry course: the two transformations you studied separately — dilation and rotation — are a single complex multiplication.

## The geometric statement

> **Theorem.** Multiplication by a fixed non-zero $w$ is the transformation of the plane that turns every point by $\arg w$ about the origin and stretches every distance by $|w|$.

Both halves are already proved. Distances multiply because the absolute value is multiplicative (module 5.6); turns add because module 5.7 compared two triangles and found them similar.

Three special cases you already know:

- $|w| = 1$: a **pure rotation**. The distances are multiplied by $1$, so nothing moves nearer or further; only the direction changes.
- $w$ a positive real: a **pure dilation**, since $\arg w = 0°$ and no turning happens.
- $w = i$: a $90°$ rotation, since $|i| = 1$ and $\arg i = 90°$ — module 5.5's observation, now a special case of a theorem.

And $w = -1$ gives a $180°$ rotation, so $i^2 = -1$ is the statement that two quarter turns make a half turn (module 5.1). Every fact about $i$ in this chapter has been the same fact about rotation.

<Warning title="Rotation about the origin only">
Multiplying by $w$ rotates about $0$, and about nothing else. To rotate about a different center $c$, slide $c$ to the origin, rotate, and slide back:

$$\text{new position} = w\,(z - c) + c.$$

Three transformations composed, in that order and no other — module 3.7's point about composition, in a setting where getting the order wrong visibly puts your figure in the wrong place.

Your geometry course does the same thing when it moves a figure to the origin before applying a rule. The complex version is one line.
</Warning>

## De Moivre's formula

Apply the theorem with $z = w$: squaring squares the distance and doubles the turn. Apply it again and cubing cubes the distance and triples the turn. In general:

> **De Moivre's formula.** $\big(r, \theta\big)^n = \big(r^n,\ n\theta\big)$ for every whole number $n \geq 1$.

**Rotating $n$ times through $\theta$ is rotating once through $n\theta$** — obvious as a statement about turning, and not at all obvious as a statement about multiplying an expression out $n$ times.

<Proof title="Why the pattern does not stop">
Formally this is induction on $n$, which chapter 6 handles properly. The step is one line: if $(r,\theta)^k = (r^k, k\theta)$, then multiplying once more by $(r,\theta)$ multiplies the distances and adds the turns, giving $(r^k\cdot r,\ k\theta+\theta) = (r^{k+1}, (k+1)\theta)$.

A claim indexed by the whole numbers, with each case following from the one before, is exactly what the domino argument is for.
</Proof>

## Roots

Run De Moivre backwards and it solves $z^n = 1$.

A solution must have $r^n = 1$ with $r > 0$, so $r = 1$: every solution sits on the circle of radius $1$. And its turn, multiplied by $n$, must be a whole number of full turns. So

$$\theta_k = \frac{360°\,k}{n}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1,$$

and taking $k = n$ or beyond just repeats angles already listed.

**So $z^n = 1$ has exactly $n$ solutions, evenly spaced around the circle** — the corners of a regular $n$-gon with one corner at $1$.

Notice what that answer is made of. An **algebraic** question — solve an equation — came back with a **geometric** answer: a regular polygon. Neither subject predicts the other, and the bridge between them is the theorem of module 5.7.

There is more structure in the picture than the count. Multiply two corners of the hexagon and the turns add, landing on another corner, because $60° + 120° = 180°$ and every sum comes back into the list once whole turns are discarded. That discarding is module 4.4's remainder arithmetic, running around a circle instead of along a line.

## What the chapter proved

Start to finish:

1. $x^2+1$ has no real solution, so extend the reals (5.1).
2. A complex number is a pair of reals with a multiplication attached, and all four operations work (5.2, 5.3).
3. Conjugation is reflection in the real axis (5.4).
4. Complex numbers are points of the plane (5.5).
5. Absolute value is the distance formula (5.6).
6. Multiplying multiplies distances and adds turns — proved from similar triangles (5.7), and read off polar form (5.8).
7. So multiplication is rotation together with stretching (5.9).

**An algebraic repair turned into a geometry.** A number invented to solve one equation turned out to encode the transformations your geometry course spends a unit on.

<Aside>
One more thing waits for you here.

Next year you will meet two rules that convert an angle into the across and up amounts of a point — and a long list of formulas relating them, which most people memorize. Every one of those formulas is this module's theorem in disguise: write two points in polar form, multiply them in *both* descriptions, and match the parts.

The whole list collapses to one line of algebra and $i^2 = -1$. That is worth remembering when the list arrives, because a formula you can re-derive in thirty seconds is not a formula you have to trust your memory for.
</Aside>
