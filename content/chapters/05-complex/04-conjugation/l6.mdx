Module 5.3 used $a-bi$ to make a denominator real. That operation has a name and a great many properties.

## Definition

For $z = a+bi$, the **conjugate** is
$$\bar z = a - bi.$$

Flip the sign of the imaginary part. $\overline{3+4i} = 3-4i$, $\overline{5} = 5$, $\overline{2i} = -2i$.

## It is a reflection

In the plane of module 5.5, $z = a+bi$ is the point $(a,b)$ and $\bar z$ is $(a,-b)$.

**That is reflection in the real axis** — the same transformation your course writes as $(x,y)\mapsto(x,-y)$ (module 3.1).

Everything below follows from that reading, and the geometry explains the algebra rather than merely illustrating it.

## Doing it twice gets you back

$$\overline{\bar z} = z.$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

<Proof>
$\overline{\overline{a+bi}} = \overline{a - bi} = a-(-b)i = a+bi$.
</Proof>

Conjugating twice returns you to where you started, which is what a reflection does (module 3.9). An operation that is **its own inverse** like this is worth collecting, and you have met two others: $\overline{\overline S}=S$ for set complements (module 2.5) and $\neg\neg p \equiv p$ for negation (module 1.2).

## The fixed points

> **Proposition.** $z$ is real **if and only if** $z = \bar z$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$z = a+bi$ with $a,b$ real. "Real" means $b = 0$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Both directions — module 1.9's check both boxes.</div>
  <div slot="how">Forward is a computation. Backward uses the equality rule from module 5.2: equal complex numbers have equal imaginary parts, giving $b = -b$, and only $0$ satisfies that in $\mathbb{R}$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Write $z = a+bi$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{R}$.

**(forward)** Suppose $z$ is real, so $b = 0$ and $z = a$. Then $\bar z = a - 0i = a = z$.

**(backward)** Suppose $z = \bar z$, that is, $a+bi = a-bi$. Equating imaginary parts gives $b = -b$, so $2b = 0$ and so $b = 0$.

Therefore $z = a$ is real.
</Proof>

**The geometry says the same thing.** The fixed points of a reflection are exactly the points on the mirror (module 3.3), and here the mirror is the real axis. A complex number is unmoved by conjugation exactly when it lies on the real line.

Two derivations of one fact — one algebraic, one geometric — and the second explains the first.

## Recovering the parts

$$\operatorname{Re}(z) = \frac{z+\bar z}{2}, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z) = \frac{z-\bar z}{2i}.$$

<Proof>
With $z = a+bi$: $z + \bar z = (a+bi)+(a-bi) = 2a$, so $\frac{z+\bar z}{2} = a = \operatorname{Re}(z)$.

And $z - \bar z = 2bi$, so $\frac{z-\bar z}{2i} = b = \operatorname{Im}(z)$.
</Proof>

The first formula is a midpoint: $\frac{z+\bar z}{2}$ is the midpoint of $z$ and its mirror image, which lands on the mirror. That is the midpoint formula from your coordinate work, and it is the same reason the perpendicular bisector construction works.

## Conjugation respects arithmetic

$$\overline{z+w} = \bar z + \bar w, \qquad \overline{zw} = \bar z\,\bar w, \qquad \overline{z/w} = \bar z/\bar w \ (w\neq0).$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

<Proof>
Let $z = a+bi$ and $w = c+di$.

**Sums.** $\overline{z+w} = \overline{(a+c)+(b+d)i} = (a+c)-(b+d)i$, and $\bar z + \bar w = (a-bi)+(c-di) = (a+c)-(b+d)i$. Equal.

**Products.** $zw = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i$, so $\overline{zw} = (ac-bd)-(ad+bc)i$.

Meanwhile $\bar z\bar w = (a-bi)(c-di) = ac - adi - bci + bdi^2 = (ac-bd) - (ad+bc)i$.

Equal.
</Proof>

**Conjugation preserves the operations**: it does not merely happen to pass through $+$ and $\times$ on a few examples, it does so for every pair of complex numbers, and the two proofs above prove exactly that. It is also a bijection, being its own inverse, and it leaves every real number where it is.

Put together, those three facts say $i$ and $-i$ are genuinely interchangeable. Swap them everywhere and every true equation stays true, because the swap respects all the arithmetic and touches none of the reals.

<Warning title="Which root is i?">
$i$ was introduced as *a* square root of $-1$. There are two, $i$ and $-i$, and no algebraic property distinguishes them: replace $i$ by $-i$ everywhere and every true equation stays true.

That is exactly what the product rule says. Conjugation is the swap, and it preserves everything.

The choice of which root to call $i$ is a labeling convention — like choosing which rotation direction to call counterclockwise. It has no mathematical content, and the fact that nothing depends on it is a theorem.
</Warning>

## Two consequences

**Real coefficients give paired roots.** If $p$ is a polynomial with real coefficients and $p(z)=0$, then $p(\bar z)=0$.

The reason is the product and sum rules: conjugating $a_nz^n+\dots+a_0 = 0$ term by term gives $a_n\bar z^n + \dots + a_0 = 0$, since each real coefficient is its own conjugate.

You have seen this without the explanation. The quadratic formula with negative discriminant produces $\frac{-b}{2a}\pm\frac{\sqrt{4ac-b^2}}{2a}i$ — a conjugate pair, always. **Non-real roots of a real polynomial come in pairs**, which is why a cubic with real coefficients always has at least one real root: three roots cannot pair up evenly.

**The modulus.** $z\bar z = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2+b^2$, a non-negative real. Module 5.6 defines $|z| = \sqrt{z\bar z}$ on the strength of this, and the Pythagorean theorem is visible in the formula.

<Aside>
Conjugation gives a compact way to write sets of points in the plane.

$z\bar z = r^2$ is the circle of radius $r$ centerd at the origin, since $z\bar z = a^2+b^2$.

$z = \bar z$ is the real axis, by the proposition above. $z = -\bar z$ is the imaginary axis.

$|z - w| = |z - v|$ is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining $w$ and $v$ — module 2.2's set of points, written in one complex equation instead of two real ones.

Complex notation compresses coordinate geometry substantially, and the compression is not cosmetic: transformations that need a whole grid of coefficients in real coordinates — a *matrix*, in the Algebra II sense — are often a single multiplication in $\mathbb{C}$.
</Aside>
