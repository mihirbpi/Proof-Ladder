$$
\overline{a+bi} = a - bi.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

Geometrically, reflection in the real axis (§5.5).

## Properties

> **Proposition.** For $z, w \in \mathbb{C}$:
> $$\overline{\bar z} = z, \quad \overline{z+w} = \bar z + \bar w, \quad \overline{zw} = \bar z \bar w, \quad z\bar z = |z|^2 \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq0},$$
> $$\operatorname{Re} z = \tfrac{z+\bar z}{2}, \quad \operatorname{Im} z = \tfrac{z-\bar z}{2i}, \quad z \in \mathbb{R} \iff z = \bar z.$$

<Proof>
Write $z = a+bi$, $w = c+di$.

*Conjugating twice.* $\overline{\bar z} = \overline{a-bi} = a+bi = z$.

*Additive.* $\overline{z+w} = \overline{(a+c)+(b+d)i} = (a+c)-(b+d)i = \bar z + \bar w$.

*Multiplicative.* $zw = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i$, so $\overline{zw} = (ac-bd)-(ad+bc)i$. And $\bar z\bar w = (a-bi)(c-di) = (ac-bd)-(ad+bc)i$. Equal.

*Norm.* $z\bar z = a^2 - (bi)^2 = a^2+b^2 \geq 0$, vanishing iff $z = 0$.

*Parts.* $z+\bar z = 2a$ and $z - \bar z = 2bi$.

*Fixed points.* If $z \in \mathbb{R}$ then $b = 0$ and $\bar z = z$. Going the other way, $z = \bar z$ gives $b = -b$, so $b = 0$ and $z \in \mathbb{R}$.
</Proof>

The first three properties say conjugation **preserves both operations** — it is a relabeling of $\mathbb{C}$ that arithmetic cannot detect; the last says the numbers it leaves fixed are exactly the reals. Those two facts together are stronger than they look: conjugation and doing nothing are the *only* two relabelings with that property, which is why complex roots of real polynomials pair up and never come in threes.

There is a philosophical point in this. Nothing algebraic distinguishes $i$ from $-i$: both are roots of $x^2+1$, and conjugation is exactly the symmetry that exchanges them. The choice of which to call $i$ is a labeling convention with no mathematical content, and conjugation is the record of that fact.

## The conjugate root theorem

> **Theorem.** Let $p \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ and $z \in \mathbb{C}$ with $p(z) = 0$. Then $p(\bar z) = 0$.

<Proof>
Write $p(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n c_kx^k$ with $c_k \in \mathbb{R}$. Since conjugation preserves sums and products and fixes reals,
$$
p(\bar z) = \sum c_k \bar z^{\,k} = \sum \overline{c_k} \,\overline{z^k} = \overline{\sum c_k z^k} = \overline{p(z)} = \bar 0 = 0.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

</Proof>

Consequences: non-real roots of real polynomials occur in conjugate pairs; so every real polynomial of odd degree has a real root; so every real polynomial factors over $\mathbb{R}$ into linear and irreducible quadratic factors — the quadratics being $(x-z)(x-\bar z) = x^2 - 2\operatorname{Re}(z)x + |z|^2$, which has real coefficients by the proposition.

That last factorisation is what makes partial fractions work over $\mathbb{R}$, and so what makes every rational function integrable in closed form.

<Warning title="Real coefficients are essential">
$p(x) = x - i$ has root $i$ but not $\bar i = -i$. The theorem uses $\overline{c_k} = c_k$, which holds only for real coefficients.

Relatedly, $\overline{f(z)} = f(\bar z)$ fails for functions in general. It holds when the rule is built out of real numbers and the arithmetic operations — a polynomial with real coefficients, say — and fails as soon as it is not: for $f(z) = iz$, conjugating the input and conjugating the output give opposite answers.
</Warning>

## Conjugation as reflection

Plotting $z = a+bi$ at $(a,b)$ (§5.5), the conjugate $\bar z$ is at $(a,-b)$: **reflection in the real axis.**

That reading explains the algebra rather than merely illustrating it.

**Why $\overline{\bar z} = z$.** Reflecting twice returns every point. That it undoes itself is a geometric fact, and the same fact holds for set complements and for logical negation.

**Why $z = \bar z$ exactly for real $z$.** The fixed points of a reflection are the points of the mirror, and the mirror is the real axis. The six-line algebraic proof and the one-line geometric observation prove the same thing, and the second explains it.

**Why $|\bar z| = |z|$.** A reflection is a rigid motion; it preserves distance from the origin.

**Why conjugation preserves products.** Reflecting both factors and multiplying gives the same result as multiplying and then reflecting — because multiplication adds arguments (§5.9), and reflection negates them. $-(\theta_1+\theta_2) = (-\theta_1)+(-\theta_2)$, which is the whole content.

The last one is the most useful, and it is the reason the conjugate-root theorem holds: conjugating a polynomial identity term by term is legitimate exactly because conjugation respects both operations.

## Conjugation is not continuous-linear

$z \mapsto \bar z$ is $\mathbb{R}$-linear but not $\mathbb{C}$-linear: $\overline{iz} = -i\bar z \neq i\bar z$ in general. It is therefore not complex-differentiable anywhere, despite being smooth as a map $\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$ — the standard first example showing that complex differentiability is far stronger than real differentiability.

This has a consequence you will meet much later: the operations that pick out a part — real part, imaginary part, distance, conjugate — all behave badly under the calculus of complex numbers, exactly because each of them ignores some of the structure. Recognizing them at a glance saves a great deal of time in that course.

<Aside>
Conjugation is one of exactly two *continuous* operation-preserving relabelings of $\mathbb{C}$. Dropping continuity there are vastly more, constructed non-constructively; all but the identity and conjugation are non-measurable and unwritable. That $\mathbb{R}$ by contrast has *only* the identity — because its order, so its notion of nearness, is determined algebraically by which elements are squares — is a small and striking asymmetry.
</Aside>

Conjugation is the operation that keeps real answers real, and both courses ahead rely on it.

**Complex roots of real polynomials come in conjugate pairs.** If $p$ has real coefficients and $p(z)=0$, then conjugating term by term gives $p(\bar z) = 0$, since each real coefficient is its own conjugate.

Three consequences you will use:

- **A real polynomial of odd degree has a real root**, because the non-real roots pair up and an odd count cannot pair evenly. That is why every real cubic crosses the $x$-axis.
- **Real quadratic factors come from conjugate pairs.** Multiplying $(x - z)(x-\bar z)$ gives $x^2 - 2\operatorname{Re}(z)x + |z|^2$, with real coefficients — which is exactly the irreducible quadratic block in a partial-fraction decomposition.
- **Real answers get extracted from conjugate pairs.** Later courses routinely take a detour through complex numbers to answer a question that was about real ones all along, and the return trip is the pair of formulas proved in this module: $\operatorname{Re}(z) = \frac{z+\bar z}{2}$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z) = \frac{z-\bar z}{2i}$. Average a number with its conjugate and the imaginary parts cancel — which is why the detour is safe.

</WhereThisGoes>
