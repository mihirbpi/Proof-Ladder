For $z = a+bi$, the **conjugate** is
$$
\bar z = a - bi.
$$

Geometrically (module 5.5): reflection in the real axis.

## Four properties

> **Proposition.** For all $z \in \mathbb{C}$:
> (i) $\overline{\bar z} = z$;
> (ii) $z\bar z = a^2+b^2 \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, with equality iff $z = 0$;
> (iii) $\operatorname{Re}(z) = \dfrac{z+\bar z}{2}$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z) = \dfrac{z - \bar z}{2i}$;
> (iv) $z \in \mathbb{R} \iff z = \bar z$.

<Proof>
Write $z = a+bi$ with $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$.

(i) $\overline{\bar z} = \overline{a - bi} = a + bi = z$. So conjugation is an **involution** — it is its own inverse, in the sense of module 3.9.

(ii) $z\bar z = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 - (bi)^2 = a^2 - b^2i^2 = a^2+b^2$. Both squares are non-negative reals, so the sum is a non-negative real, and it vanishes iff $a = b = 0$.

(iii) $z + \bar z = 2a$, so $(z+\bar z)/2 = a = \operatorname{Re}(z)$. And $z - \bar z = 2bi$, so $(z-\bar z)/(2i) = b = \operatorname{Im}(z)$.

(iv) See below.
</Proof>

Property (ii) is the workhorse: it is what makes $1/z = \bar z/(z\bar z)$ computable, and in module 5.6 it becomes $z\bar z = |z|^2$.

Property (iii) says conjugation lets you extract real and imaginary parts by *arithmetic* rather than by inspecting a representation — which matters when $z$ is defined by a formula rather than written as $a+bi$.

## The fixed points

> **Proposition.** $z \in \mathbb{R} \iff z = \bar z$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">A biconditional, so two proofs (module 1.9). One direction is a substitution; the other needs comparison of parts.</div>
  <div slot="want">Both implications.</div>
  <div slot="how">(forward) If $z$ is real, its imaginary part is $0$, and negating $0$ changes nothing. (backward) Equate $a+bi$ with $a - bi$ and compare imaginary parts to force $b = 0$.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**(forward)** Suppose $z \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $z = a + 0i$, so $\bar z = a - 0i = a = z$.

**(backward)** Suppose $z = \bar z$, i.e. $a + bi = a - bi$. Comparing imaginary parts (module 5.2), $b = -b$, so $2b = 0$ and hence $b = 0$. Therefore $z = a \in \mathbb{R}$.
</Proof>

This is a clean payoff for module 1.9: a biconditional whose two halves genuinely need different arguments. Geometrically it says the fixed points of a reflection are the points on the mirror — the real axis.

## Conjugation respects arithmetic

> **Proposition.** $\overline{z+w} = \bar z + \bar w$ and $\overline{zw} = \bar z\,\bar w$.

<Proof>
For the product, let $z = a+bi$, $w = c+di$. Then $zw = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i$, so
$$\overline{zw} = (ac-bd) - (ad+bc)i.$$
On the other side,
$$\bar z\,\bar w = (a-bi)(c-di) = (ac - bd) + (-ad-bc)i,$$
which is the same. The sum case is immediate.
</Proof>

So conjugation preserves both operations and fixes every real number. In the language of module 4.5, it is a map of $\mathbb{C}$ to itself that respects every operation and leaves $\mathbb{R}$ untouched — and it is the only not obvious one that behaves reasonably.

<Warning title="Conjugation is not negation">
$\bar z$ flips the sign of $\operatorname{Im}(z)$ only; $-z$ flips both parts.

$\overline{3+4i} = 3-4i$ (a reflection), while $-(3+4i) = -3-4i$ (a half turn). Their fixed points differ: $\bar z = z$ holds on all of $\mathbb{R}$, while $-z = z$ holds only at $0$.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Take any polynomial with **real** coefficients. Conjugation passes through sums and products and leaves real numbers alone, so conjugating the whole expression is the same as replacing $z$ by $\bar z$ everywhere. If the expression is $0$ at $z$, it is therefore $0$ at $\bar z$ too — **non-real roots of real polynomials come in conjugate pairs.** That is why a cubic with real coefficients always has at least one real root: the non-real ones pair up, and three is odd.
</Aside>
