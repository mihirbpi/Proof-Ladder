The **flip** takes a complex number and reflects it across the middle line.

The across part stays exactly the same. The up part turns into a down part.

$$3 + 4i \quad\text{flips to}\quad 3 - 4i.$$

$$1 + 5i \quad\text{flips to}\quad 1 - 5i.$$

$$5 + 0i \quad\text{flips to}\quad 5 - 0i = 5.$$

That last one did not change! Because $5$ is an ordinary number, sitting right on the middle line.

## Flip twice and you are home

Take $3 + 4i$. Flip: $3 - 4i$. Flip again: $3 + 4i$.

You are back where you started. Every time. Two flips always undo each other, just like looking in a mirror at a mirror.

## Which numbers do not move?

A number stays put under the flip exactly when it has **no up part** — that is, exactly when it is an ordinary number.

Makes sense: those are the ones sitting on the mirror line itself.

<Warning title="Only the up part changes sign">
$3 + 4i$ flips to $3 - 4i$. The across part is left completely **alone** — it stays at $3$.

Only the up part changes. If you changed both, you would have spun the point halfway round the page instead of folding it over.
</Warning>

## A surprise

Now try something. Take a spot and its flip, and put them together the joining way.

For $3 + 4i$ and its flip, the answer comes out **$25$** — a perfectly ordinary number, with no up part left at all.

For $1 + 2i$ and its flip, the answer is **$5$**. Ordinary again!

The up parts cancel out completely, every single time.

A number times its own flip is always ordinary. That turns out to be extremely useful, and you will see why in a couple of modules.

<Aside>
Look closely at $25$. It is $9 + 16$ — a $3$-by-$3$ square plus a $4$-by-$4$ square. And $5$ is $1 + 4$ — a $1$-by-$1$ square plus a $2$-by-$2$ square.

Every time, you get a square built on the across part plus a square built on the up part. If that reminds you of something about right triangles, hold that thought until module 5.6.
</Aside>
