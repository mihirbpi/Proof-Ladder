The **conjugate** of a complex number keeps the across part exactly as it is, and flips the sign of the turn part.

Flip the sign of the imaginary part. Leave the real part alone.

$$\overline{3+4i} = 3-4i, \qquad \overline{-2 + i} = -2 - i, \qquad \overline{5} = 5, \qquad \overline{2i} = -2i.$$

Geometrically it is a reflection in the horizontal axis (module 5.5 draws this properly): across stays, up becomes down.

## Doing it twice gets you back

$$
\overline{\overline{z}} = z.
$$

Because flipping the sign of $b$ twice returns $b$. An operation that undoes itself like this is called an *involution* — and you have met others: negation ($-(-a) = a$), taking the outside of a set (module 2.5), and turning an arrow diagram round twice (module 3.9).

## $z$ times $\overline{z}$ is always real

This is the property that makes conjugates useful.

<Proof>
Take any complex number and multiply it by its own conjugate. Expand the brackets as usual.

You get four pieces. Two of them are the across part multiplied by itself, and the turn part multiplied by itself.

The other two pieces are the mixed ones — and they are identical except for their signs, so they cancel out completely.

What is left is: **the across part squared, plus the turn part squared.**

Both of those are ordinary numbers multiplied by themselves, so both are zero or bigger. Their sum is an ordinary number, and it is never below zero.
</Proof>

Examples: $(3+4i)(3-4i) = 9 + 16 = 25$. And $(1+2i)(1-2i) = 1+4 = 5$.

## Recovering the parts

Conjugation lets you pull out the real and imaginary parts by arithmetic:
$$
\text{Re}(z) = \frac{z + \overline{z}}{2}.
$$

Check it: adding a number to its conjugate makes the turn parts cancel and doubles the across part. Halving then gives the across part back. ✓

The imaginary part comes out similarly, though you have to divide by $2i$ rather than $2$:
$$
\text{Im}(z) = \frac{z - \overline{z}}{2i},
$$
since $z - \overline{z} = 2bi$, and dividing by $2i$ leaves $b$.

## The numbers that do not move

> **Claim.** $z$ is an ordinary number if and only if $z = \overline{z}$.

This is an *if and only if*, so it needs both directions — **check both boxes** (module 1.9).

<Proof>
**(Box 1)** Suppose $z$ is ordinary. Then $z = a + 0i$ for some $a$, so $\overline{z} = a - 0i = a = z$. ✓

**($\Leftarrow$)** Suppose $z = \overline{z}$. Writing out both sides, the across parts already match, so the two turn parts must match as well — and one is the other with its sign flipped.

Comparing imaginary parts (module 5.2), $b = -b$. So $2b = 0$, and $b = 0$.

Hence $z = a + 0i$, an ordinary number. ✓

Both directions hold, so the claim is proved.
</Proof>

Geometrically this is obvious once drawn: the points fixed by a reflection are exactly the points on the mirror line, and the mirror line *is* the ordinary numbers. Algebra and geometry saying the same thing is a recurring feature of this chapter.

<Warning title="Only the imaginary part flips">
$\overline{3+4i} = 3 - 4i$, not $-3-4i$. Negating both parts gives $-z$, which is a half-turn about the origin, not a reflection. They are different operations with different fixed points: $\overline z = z$ picks out the reals, while $-z = z$ picks out only $0$.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Because $z\overline z$ is always real and positive (for $z \neq 0$), you can divide by it. That is exactly the trick for computing $1/z$ — multiply top and bottom by $\overline z$ and the bottom becomes real. Conjugation is what makes division of complex numbers possible at all.
</Aside>
