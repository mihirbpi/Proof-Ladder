$$
\mathbb{C} = \{\, a + bi \mid a, b \in \mathbb{R} \,\}, \qquad i^2 = -1.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\mathbb{C}$ is the complex numbers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: the complex numbers are every expression $a+bi$ — a real number, plus a real number times $i$ — where $i$ multiplied by itself gives $-1$. The number $a$ is called the **real part** and $b$ the **imaginary part**, and both of them are ordinary real numbers.*

For a complex number $z = a+bi$, the number $a$ is its **real part** and $b$ is its **imaginary part**, written $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z)$. **Both of them are ordinary real numbers.**

Two complex numbers are equal exactly when both parts match:
$$
a + bi = c + di \iff a = c \ \wedge\ b = d.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\wedge$ is "and". So the line reads: two complex numbers are equal exactly when their real parts match **and** their imaginary parts match.*

This is the working fact of the chapter. A single complex equation is *two real equations*, and "comparing real and imaginary parts" is the standard way to extract them — the move that produces the double-angle identities in module 5.9.

<Warning title="Im(z) carries no i">
The imaginary part of $3+4i$ is $4$, **not** $4i$. The $i$ is not part of it.

Both *real part* and *imaginary part* are functions in the sense of Chapter 3: feed in a complex number, get back a real one.

Two consequences worth checking against the definition: every complex number is its real part plus $i$ times its imaginary part; and a complex number is real exactly when its imaginary part is zero.
</Warning>

## The reals sit inside the complex numbers

> **Proposition.** Every real number is a complex number — and there are complex numbers that are not real.

<Proof>
Take any real number $a$. Then $a = a + 0i$, which is a real number plus a real number times $i$ — exactly the shape a complex number has. So $a$ is complex. Nothing was assumed about $a$, so every real number is.

For the second half, one example is enough: $i$ itself is $0 + 1i$, and $i$ is not real, because squaring it gives $-1$ while squaring any real number gives something at zero or above (module 5.1).
</Proof>

Three lines of following an element through (module 2.3), plus one example (module 1.11). **Every containment in the tower below has exactly that shape.**

## The complete tower

$$
\mathbb{N} \subsetneq \mathbb{Z} \subsetneq \mathbb{Q} \subsetneq \mathbb{R} \subsetneq \mathbb{C}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers, $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\mathbb{C}$ is the complex numbers. So the line reads: each system sits strictly inside the next, and each was built because the one before it could not do something.*

| Link | Inclusion proof | Properness witness |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{N} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ | by construction | $-1$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}$ | $n = n/1$ | $1/2$ |
| $\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ | by construction | $\sqrt2$ (module 4.7) |
| $\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}$ | $a = a+0i$ | $i$ |

Sitting-inside chains (module 2.3), so every counting number is a complex number, and every number in this course lives somewhere in that tower. Only one of the four examples in the last column took serious work — and it was $\sqrt2$.

## The complex numbers have no compatible order

Something is lost at the last step, and it is worth being explicit about.

On the number line, the ordering fits the arithmetic: you can add the same amount to both sides of an inequality, multiply both sides by a positive number, and so on. **The complex numbers have no ordering that behaves that way**, and here is why not.

In any system where the ordering fits the arithmetic, squares are never negative (module 5.1 proves it using only the ordering rules). But $i$ squared is $-1$. So $-1$ would have to be zero or above. And $1$ is $1$ squared, so $1$ would have to be zero or above as well. Put those together and $1$ is squeezed to $0$ — which is false.

So the question *is $i$ bigger than $1$?* has no answer, and never will. This is not a gap awaiting repair: it is the price paid for being able to solve every equation built from powers of $x$, and it is why complex numbers are compared by *size* (module 5.6) rather than by order.

## Purely imaginary

A complex number is **purely imaginary** when its real part is zero — when it looks like $bi$ with nothing added.

So two special families sit inside the complex numbers: the reals, whose imaginary part is zero, and the purely imaginary numbers, whose real part is zero. Module 5.5 draws them as two lines crossing at the origin.

<Aside>
A complex number is pinned down by its two parts, so sending each complex number to the pair (real part, imaginary part) matches them up perfectly with the pairs of real numbers — the crossing from module 2.9.

As collections, and as far as *addition* is concerned, the complex numbers and the pairs of real numbers are the same thing. What the complex numbers have that plain pairs do not is a **multiplication** — and every remarkable fact in this chapter comes out of it.
</Aside>
