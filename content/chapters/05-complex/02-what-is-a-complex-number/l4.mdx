$$
\mathbb{C} = \{\, a + bi \mid a, b \in \mathbb{R} \,\}, \qquad i^2 = -1.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\mathbb{C}$ is the complex numbers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: the complex numbers are every expression $a+bi$ — a real number, plus a real number times $i$ — where $i$ multiplied by itself gives $-1$. The number $a$ is called the **real part** and $b$ the **imaginary part**, and both of them are ordinary real numbers.*

For $z = a+bi$, define $\operatorname{Re}(z) = a$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z) = b$. **Both are real numbers.**

Equality is componentwise:
$$
a + bi = c + di \iff a = c \ \wedge\ b = d.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\wedge$ is "and".*

This is the working fact of the chapter. A single complex equation is *two real equations*, and "comparing real and imaginary parts" is the standard way to extract them — the move that produces the double-angle identities in module 5.9.

<Warning title="Im(z) carries no i">
$\operatorname{Im}(3+4i) = 4$, not $4i$. Both $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ are functions $\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}$ in the sense of Chapter 3 — their codomain is $\mathbb{R}$.

Consequences worth checking: $z = \operatorname{Re}(z) + i\operatorname{Im}(z)$, and $z$ is real iff $\operatorname{Im}(z) = 0$.
</Warning>

## The reals sit inside the complex numbers

> **Proposition.** $\mathbb{R} \subseteq \mathbb{C}$, properly.

<Proof>
Take any $a \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $a = a + 0i$ with $a, 0 \in \mathbb{R}$, so $a \in \mathbb{C}$ by definition. Since nothing was assumed about $a$, $\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}$.

For properness: $i = 0 + 1i \in \mathbb{C}$, and $i \notin \mathbb{R}$, since $i^2 = -1 < 0$ while $x^2 \geq 0$ for all real $x$ (module 5.1).
</Proof>

Three lines of element chasing (module 2.3) plus one witness (module 1.11). The pattern is worth noting: **every containment in the tower has exactly this shape.**

## The complete tower

$$
\mathbb{N} \subsetneq \mathbb{Z} \subsetneq \mathbb{Q} \subsetneq \mathbb{R} \subsetneq \mathbb{C}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{N}$ is the counting numbers, $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{Q}$ is the fractions, $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\mathbb{C}$ is the complex numbers. So the line reads: each system sits strictly inside the next, and each was built because the one before it could not do something.*

| Link | Inclusion proof | Properness witness |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{N} \subseteq \mathbb{Z}$ | by construction | $-1$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}$ | $n = n/1$ | $1/2$ |
| $\mathbb{Q}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ | by construction | $\sqrt2$ (module 4.7) |
| $\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}$ | $a = a+0i$ | $i$ |

By transitivity of $\subseteq$ (module 2.3), $\mathbb{N}\subseteq\mathbb{C}$, and every number in this course lives somewhere in the chain. Only one properness witness required serious work, and it was $\sqrt2$.

## The complex numbers have no compatible order

Something is lost at the last step, and it is worth being explicit about.

$\mathbb{R}$ is a system whose ordering fits its arithmetic: $<$ interacts with $+$ and $\times$ by rules you use constantly. $\mathbb{C}$ has no such order.

Why not? In any such system, squares are non-negative (module 5.1's proof used only the order axioms). But in $\mathbb{C}$ we have $i^2 = -1$, so we would need $-1 \geq 0$, and also $1 = 1^2 \geq 0$ — giving $1 \leq 0 \leq 1$, so $1 = 0$, which is false.

So questions like "is $i$ bigger than $1$?" have no answer, and never will. This is not a gap awaiting repair: it is the price paid for being able to solve every polynomial equation, and it is why complex numbers are compared by *size* (module 5.6) rather than by order.

## Purely imaginary

$z$ is **purely imaginary** if $\operatorname{Re}(z)=0$, i.e. $z = bi$. So $\mathbb{C}$ contains two distinguished lines through the origin: the reals ($\operatorname{Im}=0$) and the purely imaginary numbers ($\operatorname{Re}=0$). Module 5.5 draws them as the two axes.

<Aside>
Since $z$ is determined by the pair $(\operatorname{Re}(z), \operatorname{Im}(z)) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, the map sending $z$ to $(\operatorname{Re} z, \operatorname{Im} z)$ is a bijection $\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ — a product set from module 2.9. As *sets* and as *additive* structures, $\mathbb{C}$ and $\mathbb{R}^2$ are the same thing. What $\mathbb{C}$ has that $\mathbb{R}^2$ lacks is a multiplication, and every remarkable fact in this chapter comes from that multiplication.
</Aside>
