Module 5.1 introduced $i$, the number whose job is to turn you a quarter turn. Now we build the number system around it.

## The definition

A **complex number** is a pair of instructions: *go this far across*, and *go this far in the turned direction*.

We write it like this:
$$
3 + 4i
$$
which means: go $3$ across, then $4$ in the direction $i$ points.

</Figure>

The number in front is the **across part**. The number attached to $i$ is the **turn part**.

Examples: $3 + 4i$ has across part $3$ and turn part $4$. And $2 + 7i$ has across part $2$ and turn part $7$.

<Warning title="The turn part is an ordinary number">
In $3 + 4i$, the turn part is $4$ — **not** $4i$.

Both parts are ordinary numbers you already know. The $i$ is a label saying *which slot this one is in*, like the word "apples" in "3 apples". The number is $3$; *apples* just tells you what kind.

Getting this wrong makes every later formula come out wrong.
</Warning>

## Ordinary numbers are already in there

Take any ordinary number — say $5$. Write it as
$$
5 + 0i,
$$
which means: go $5$ across, and do not turn at all.

That is a complex number! So **every ordinary number is already a complex number** — the kind with no turn part.

Nothing was lost when the new numbers arrived. Everything you had is still here, sitting along the across direction.

And the new numbers really are new: $i$ itself is $0 + 1i$, which is no across and one turn. That is not anywhere on the old number line.

## The whole tower

You can now stack every number system in this course inside the next one.

| Collection | Something new it adds |
|---|---|
| counting numbers | — |
| fractions | pieces, like $\tfrac12$ |
| numbers on the number line | lengths no fraction fits, like the diagonal (module 4.7) |
| complex numbers | turning |

Each one sits inside the next, and by transitivity (module 2.3) the counting numbers sit inside all of them. Every number you have ever met is somewhere in that picture.

## When are two of them the same?

Two complex numbers are equal exactly when **both parts match**: same across part, and same turn part.

That looks obvious and it is worth stating, because it lets you do something powerful — **compare the parts.** One statement about complex numbers turns into two statements about ordinary numbers, and you get both for the price of one.

You will use that constantly from module 5.9 onwards.

<Aside>
A complex number with no across part, like $7i$, is pure turn. A complex number with no turn part is just an ordinary number. Everything else is a bit of both — which is exactly why they fill a whole *plane* instead of a line.
</Aside>
