$$
\mathbb{C} = \{a + bi : a,b \in \mathbb{R}\}, \qquad i^2 = -1,
$$
with $\operatorname{Re}(a+bi) = a$ and $\operatorname{Im}(a+bi) = b$, both **real-valued**. So $\operatorname{Re}, \operatorname{Im} : \mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}$ are functions in the sense of Chapter 3, and $z = \operatorname{Re}(z) + i \operatorname{Im}(z)$.

$$
a+bi = c+di \iff a = c \wedge b = d.
$$

The representation is unique, which is what makes $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ well defined (§3.1) — and it is the source of the chapter's most productive technique: **one complex equation is two real equations**, extracted by comparing parts.

<Warning title="Im(z) ∈ ℝ">
$\operatorname{Im}(3+4i) = 4$, not $4i$. This is not a quibble: $\operatorname{Im}$ appears in $|z|^2 = \operatorname{Re}(z)^2 + \operatorname{Im}(z)^2$ and in $\operatorname{Im}(z) = (z-\bar z)/(2i)$, and both formulas are wrong by a factor of $i$ under the other convention.
</Warning>

## The tower

> **Proposition.** $\mathbb{R} \subsetneq \mathbb{C}$.

<Proof>
Let $a \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $a = a + 0i \in \mathbb{C}$, so $\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}$.

$i \in \mathbb{C}$, and $i \notin \mathbb{R}$: if $i$ were real then $i^2 \geq 0$, contradicting $i^2 = -1$.
</Proof>

$$
\mathbb{N} \subsetneq \mathbb{Z} \subsetneq \mathbb{Q} \subsetneq \mathbb{R} \subsetneq \mathbb{C},
$$
each inclusion a one-line element chase and each properness a single witness ($-1$, $\tfrac12$, $\sqrt2$, $i$), with only $\sqrt2$ requiring real work (§4.7).

Strictly these are **canonical embeddings** rather than literal containments: constructed carefully, the integer $3$, the rational $3/1$, the Dedekind cut $3$, and the pair $(3,0)$ are four distinct objects. What holds is that each system embeds injectively and operation-preservingly in the next, and everyone identifies along the embeddings.

## $\mathbb{C}$ is not orderable

> **Proposition.** There is no order on $\mathbb{C}$ making it an ordered field.

<Proof>
Suppose such an order existed. In any ordered field $x^2 \geq 0$ for all $x$, by trichotomy and the sign rules. Then $-1 = i^2 \geq 0$ and $1 = 1^2 \geq 0$, so adding, $0 = (-1) + 1 \geq 0 + 0$ gives nothing — instead argue directly: $-1 \geq 0$ implies $0 \geq 1$ by adding $1$, while $1 \geq 0$; hence $0 = 1$, contradicting the field axiom $1 \neq 0$.
</Proof>

This is a genuine loss, and the trade is explicit: $\mathbb{C}$ buys algebraic closure at the cost of order. Practically, comparisons in $\mathbb{C}$ go through the modulus $|z|$ (§5.6), which is real-valued and ordered — and $|z| < |w|$ is a statement about sizes, not a partial order on $\mathbb{C}$ compatible with multiplication.

Note there *are* orders on $\mathbb{C}$ as a set (lexicographic, say). The proposition says none of them respects the arithmetic, which is the only kind worth having.

## Equating parts, and why it is legitimate

$$a+bi = c+di \iff a = c \ \text{ and }\ b = d.$$

One complex equation is two real equations. The technique this licenses — *equate real and imaginary parts* — is used constantly, and §5.9 derives the double-angle formulas from it in three lines.

The justification is worth stating. $\{1, i\}$ is a basis of $\mathbb{C}$ as a real vector space, and coordinates with respect to a basis are unique (§3.8). So $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ are well-defined functions $\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}$, and equal inputs give equal outputs.

<Warning title="Only for real coefficients">
The technique requires $a, b, c, d$ to be **real**. From $z_1 = z_2$ with $z_1, z_2$ complex expressions, you may equate parts only after each side has been written in the form (real) $+$ (real)$\,i$.

Writing $zw = 1$ and "equating parts" to get $z = 1$ and $w = 1$ is nonsense — neither side has been separated, and the conclusion is plainly false at $z = i$, $w = -i$.

The step that is easy to skip is the separation, and it is where the algebra lives.
</Warning>

## $\mathbb{C}$ versus $\mathbb{R}^2$

The map $z \mapsto (\operatorname{Re} z, \operatorname{Im} z)$ is a bijection $\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}^2$ and an isomorphism of additive groups and of real vector spaces. $\mathbb{C}$ is a two-dimensional $\mathbb{R}$-vector space with basis $\{1, i\}$.

What is new is the **multiplication**. $\mathbb{R}^2$ has no natural one; $\mathbb{C}$ has one making it a field, and it is essentially unique — the only ways to multiply on $\mathbb{R}^2$ making it a real division algebra give $\mathbb{C}$ up to isomorphism. Every distinctive fact in this chapter (rotation, Euler, De Moivre, the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra) comes from that multiplication.

<Aside>
There are exactly two ways to relabel $\mathbb{C}$ preserving both operations and fixing every real: do nothing, and conjugate (§5.4). So $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. There is no way to distinguish $i$ from $-i$ by any algebraic property — both satisfy $x^2 = -1$ and nothing separates them. The choice of which root to call $i$ is a labelling convention, and conjugation is the symmetry that swaps the two labels.

Without continuity the situation is wilder: there are vastly more such relabellings, all but two of them pathological and requiring a non-constructive choice — one that proves the relabelling exists without exhibiting it — to build.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
**$\mathbb{C}$ is $\mathbb{R}^2$ with a multiplication**, and both halves of that sentence get used.

As a set it is $\mathbb{R}^2$, so it is a two-dimensional real vector space with basis $\{1, i\}$. Complex addition is vector addition; multiplication by a real scalar is scalar multiplication. Everything you will prove about $\mathbb{R}^2$ — coordinates, linear independence, dimension — applies verbatim.

**Multiplication by a fixed $z = a+bi$ is a linear transformation of $\mathbb{R}^2$** — a map that respects adding and scaling —, and it has a matrix:
$$M_z = \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a\end{pmatrix}.$$

Check it: $M_z\binom{x}{y} = \binom{ax-by}{bx+ay}$, which is exactly the real and imaginary parts of $(a+bi)(x+yi)$. So complex multiplication *is* a family of $2\times2$ matrices, closed under multiplication and addition, and $\det M_z = a^2+b^2 = |z|^2$ (§5.6).

That determinant is the area scaling factor, which is why $|zw| = |z||w|$ becomes $\det(M_zM_w) = \det M_z\det M_w$ — a fact about matrices you will prove independently.

**Equating real and imaginary parts is a linear-algebra move.** One complex equation is two real equations, because $\{1,i\}$ is a basis and coordinates with respect to a basis are unique (§3.8). §5.9 uses it to derive trigonometric identities, and it is legitimate for exactly that reason.

**And the lack of an order matters in calculus.** Inequalities are the currency of the subject, and $\mathbb{C}$ has none — which is why complex sequences converge in terms of $|z_n - L|$, a real quantity, and why every convergence criterion you meet is stated with moduli.

*You have matrices, determinants and systems; linear algebra builds its vocabulary on top of them. Above: **linear independence** — no vector being a combination of the others; **vector space** — any system where you can add and scale; **basis** — a smallest set of vectors that builds all the others.*
</WhereThisGoes>
