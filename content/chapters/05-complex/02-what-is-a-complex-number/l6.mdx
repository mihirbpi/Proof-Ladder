$$
\mathbb{C} = \{\,a + bi \;:\; a, b \in \mathbb{R}\,\}, \qquad i^2 = -1.
$$

Set-builder notation from module 2.2, with $\mathbb{R}$ as the collection you are drawing from for both coefficients.

For $z = a+bi$:

$$\operatorname{Re}(z) = a \qquad\text{the \textbf{real part}}$$
$$\operatorname{Im}(z) = b \qquad\text{the \textbf{imaginary part}}$$

Both are **real numbers**. $\operatorname{Im}(3+4i) = 4$, not $4i$ — the $i$ is not included, and this trips people up constantly.

## Two functions

$\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ are functions $\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{R}$ in the sense of module 3.1. They are surjective and not injective: $\operatorname{Re}(3+4i)=\operatorname{Re}(3+7i)=3$.

Naming them as functions is not pedantry — module 5.9 will *equate real and imaginary parts* of two complex numbers, and that step is legitimate because $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ are well defined, which requires the uniqueness result below.

## Equality

$$
a + bi = c+di \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad a = c \ \text{ and }\ b = d.
$$

**One complex equation is two real equations.** This is the same defining property as ordered pairs (module 2.9), and it is what makes $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ single-valued: a complex number has exactly one real part.

The technique it licenses — *equate real and imaginary parts* — is one of the most useful in the subject, and module 5.9 uses it to derive trigonometric identities you learned by rote.

## The reals sit inside the complex numbers

> **Proposition.** $\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}$.

<Proof>
Let $x$ be an arbitrary real number. Then
$$x = x + 0i,$$
which has the form $a + bi$ with $a = x\in\mathbb{R}$ and $b = 0\in\mathbb{R}$.

So $x \in\mathbb{C}$. Since $x$ was arbitrary, $\mathbb{R}\subseteq\mathbb{C}$.
</Proof>

Element chasing from module 2.3, three lines. The containment is **proper**: $i = 0+1i$ is complex and not real, since module 5.1 showed no real number squares to $-1$.

Chapter 4's chain now extends:
$$\mathbb{N}\subsetneq\mathbb{Z}\subsetneq\mathbb{Q}\subsetneq\mathbb{R}\subsetneq\mathbb{C}.$$

Five systems, each properly containing the last, each built to repair a specific failure of its predecessor. Every containment here is a theorem, and every properness claim needs a witness — $-1$, $\frac12$, $\sqrt2$, $i$.

## Purely imaginary

$z$ is **purely imaginary** when $\operatorname{Re}(z)=0$ and $z\neq0$: the numbers $bi$ with $b$ real and non-zero.

$$\text{real: } \operatorname{Im}(z) = 0 \qquad\text{purely imaginary: } \operatorname{Re}(z)=0,\ z\neq0$$

The two sets are disjoint, and their union is not all of $\mathbb{C}$ — $3+4i$ is neither. That is different from the even/odd split of module 4.2, where the two cases were exhaustive. Here most complex numbers are in neither class.

<Warning title="Nothing is being added to a real number">
"$3+4i$" looks like an unfinished sum, and students want to simplify it. There is nothing to simplify.

The $+$ is genuine addition — module 5.3 defines it — but $3$ and $4i$ are not like terms, exactly as $3x$ and $4y$ are not. The expression is already in simplest form, and the two components are independent.

Compare $(3,4)$ as an ordered pair. Nobody expects to combine the entries, and $3+4i$ is the same object with different notation.
</Warning>

## No order

$\mathbb{R}$ is a system whose ordering fits its arithmetic. **the complex numbers support all four operations with no compatible order at all.**

The argument was in module 5.1: in a system whose ordering fits its arithmetic every square is $\geq 0$, and $i^2 = -1$ makes that impossible. So the following are meaningless:

$$i > 0, \qquad i<0, \qquad 3+4i > 2+9i.$$

Not false — meaningless, like asking whether one triangle is greater than another.

This is a genuine loss. Inequalities are how analysis works, and every theorem you will meet about increasing functions, maxima, and limits from the left refers to an order. **Module 5.6 supplies the replacement**: the modulus $|z|$ is a real number measuring distance from the origin, and comparing moduli is comparing reals, which is legitimate.

Your geometry course already prefers distances to coordinates for the same reason — distance is one number and comparable, position is two numbers and is not.

<Aside>
$a+bi$ still leaves $i$ unexplained. There is a construction that does not.

Define $\mathbb{C}$ to be $\mathbb{R}^2$ — ordered pairs of reals, module 2.9 — with

$$(a,b)+(c,d) = (a+c,\ b+d)$$
$$(a,b)\cdot(c,d) = (ac-bd,\ ad+bc)$$

That multiplication looks arbitrary until you check $(0,1)\cdot(0,1) = (-1, 0)$. So the pair $(0,1)$ squares to $(-1,0)$, and if we agree to write $(x,0)$ as $x$ and $(0,1)$ as $i$, then $(a,b) = a+bi$ and $i^2=-1$.

**No new objects were assumed.** Everything is built from real numbers and pairs, both of which existed already, so $\mathbb{C}$ is exactly as legitimate as $\mathbb{R}$ — which is what answers module 5.1's "isn't this cheating?"

Hamilton gave this construction in 1837, roughly three centuries after complex numbers came into use.
</Aside>

## The plane, previewed

$\mathbb{C}$ is $\mathbb{R}^2$ with a multiplication. As a *set*, it is the coordinate plane you have been working in all year.

That means every complex number is a point, every complex equation describes a locus, and the transformations of module 3.1 can be written as complex arithmetic. Module 5.5 develops this, and it is where the chapter starts paying for itself in your own subject.
