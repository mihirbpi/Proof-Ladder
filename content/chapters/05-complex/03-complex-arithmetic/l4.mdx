## Addition and multiplication

$$
(a+bi) + (c+di) = (a+c) + (b+d)i,
$$
$$
(a+bi)(c+di) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)i.
$$

The second is not a rule to memorise; it is what expansion plus $i^2=-1$ produces.

<Proof title="Derivation of the product">
Expanding the brackets using the distributive law:
$$
(a+bi)(c+di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\,i^2.
$$
Since $i^2 = -1$, the last term is $-bd$. Collecting the real terms and the $i$ terms:
$$
= (ac - bd) + (ad+bc)i. \qquad
$$
</Proof>

The only new ingredient anywhere in complex arithmetic is the substitution $i^2 = -1$. Everything else is ordinary algebra over $\mathbb{R}$.

**Worked:** $(2+3i)(4+5i) = (8 - 15) + (10+12)i = -7 + 22i$.

<Warning title="The sign on i²">
$3i \cdot 5i = 15i^2 = -15$, not $+15$. Dropped signs here are the single commonest arithmetic error in the chapter. If a product comes out with an unexpectedly large real part, check that term first.
</Warning>

## Inverses

To show the complex numbers support all four operations we need: every $z \neq 0$ has a multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{C}$.

The obstacle is that $\tfrac{1}{a+bi}$ is not visibly of the form (real) + (real)$i$. The fix is to multiply above and below by $a - bi$, the **conjugate** (module 5.4).

<Proof>
Let $z = a+bi \neq 0$, so $a$ and $b$ are not both zero.

Then
$$
\frac{1}{a+bi} = \frac{1}{a+bi}\cdot\frac{a-bi}{a-bi} = \frac{a-bi}{(a+bi)(a-bi)}.
$$

The denominator expands to $a^2 - (bi)^2 = a^2 - b^2i^2 = a^2+b^2$, which is a **real** number. So
$$
z^{-1} = \frac{a}{a^2+b^2} - \frac{b}{a^2+b^2}\,i,
$$
which has real coefficients and is therefore in $\mathbb{C}$.

It remains to check the denominator is nonzero. Since $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$, both $a^2 \geq 0$ and $b^2 \geq 0$; their sum is zero only if both are, i.e. only if $a = b = 0$, i.e. only if $z = 0$. As $z \neq 0$, we have $a^2 + b^2 > 0$.
</Proof>

The nonzero check is not a formality: it is exactly where the hypothesis $z \neq 0$ is spent, and it uses the real fact $x^2 \geq 0$ that made $i$ necessary in the first place.

**Worked:** $\dfrac{1}{3+4i} = \dfrac{3-4i}{9+16} = \dfrac{3}{25} - \dfrac{4}{25}i$.

Division is then $z/w := z \cdot w^{-1}$, computed the same way:
$$
\frac{2+i}{1-i} = \frac{(2+i)(1+i)}{(1-i)(1+i)} = \frac{2+2i+i-1}{1+1} = \frac{1+3i}{2} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac32 i.
$$

## All four operations work

Run the checklist from module 4.5:

| Axiom | Status |
|---|---|
| closed under $+$, $-$, $\times$ | by the formulas above |
| $+$ associative, commutative, identity $0$, inverses | componentwise from $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $\times$ associative, commutative, identity $1$ | expansion plus $i^2=-1$ |
| distributive | inherited from $\mathbb{R}$ |
| every $z \neq 0$ invertible | proved above |

So the complex numbers support all four operations, and every algebraic manipulation you are used to over $\mathbb{Q}$ or $\mathbb{R}$ is legal over $\mathbb{C}$.

This is the "nothing broke" verification promised in module 5.1. Adjoining $i$ did not cost us any arithmetic — it cost us the order (module 5.2), and nothing else.

## Multiplication by $i$ is a quarter turn

$$
1 \cdot i = i, \qquad i \cdot i = -1, \qquad -1 \cdot i = -i, \qquad -i \cdot i = 1.
$$

Plotted (module 5.5), those four points are $(1,0), (0,1), (-1,0), (0,-1)$ — the compass points, visited counterclockwise. Multiplying by $i$ rotates by $90°$.

So $i^2 = -1$ says: *two quarter turns make a half turn*, and a half turn is multiplication by $-1$. The defining property of $i$ is a geometric statement, and module 5.9 shows every complex multiplication is a rotation combined with a stretch.

<Aside>
The inversion trick is the same one used to rationalise $\tfrac{1}{2+\sqrt3}$ by multiplying by $\tfrac{2-\sqrt3}{2-\sqrt3}$. In both cases you multiply by a conjugate to convert an awkward denominator into a real one. That is not a coincidence: $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt3)$ and $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}(i)$ are both degree-two extensions, and the conjugate is the not obvious symmetry of each.
</Aside>
