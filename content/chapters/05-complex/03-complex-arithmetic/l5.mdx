There is only one rule to remember: **treat $i$ as an ordinary symbol, and replace $i^2$ by $-1$ whenever it appears.**

Everything below follows from that plus the algebra you already have.

## Addition and subtraction

$$
(a+bi) + (c+di) = (a+c) + (b+d)i
$$

Collect like terms. Real parts add, imaginary parts add, and the two never interact.

$$
(3+2i) + (1-5i) = 4 - 3i.
$$

## Multiplication

Expand as you would any two binomials, then apply the rule:
$$
(a+bi)(c+di) = ac + adi + bci + bd\,i^2 = (ac - bd) + (ad+bc)i.
$$

**The minus sign is where $i^2 = -1$ entered**, and it is the only place the new rule does anything. Everything else is ordinary expansion.

$$
(3+2i)(1-5i) = 3 - 15i + 2i - 10i^2 = 3 - 13i + 10 = 13 - 13i.
$$

Notice both results were again of the form $a + bi$. That is not luck — the formula proves it:

> **Proposition.** $\mathbb{C}$ is closed under addition and multiplication.

<Proof>
Let $z = a+bi$ and $w = c+di$ with $a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{R}$.

$z + w = (a+c) + (b+d)i$, and $a+c$, $b+d$ are real by closure of $\mathbb{R}$.

$zw = (ac-bd) + (ad+bc)i$, and $ac - bd$, $ad+bc$ are real by closure of $\mathbb{R}$.

Both results have the form (real) $+$ (real)$i$, hence lie in $\mathbb{C}$.
</Proof>

As always, closure of the smaller system does the work — exactly as $\mathbb{Q}$'s closure proofs rested on $\mathbb{Z}$'s in module 4.5.

## Powers of i cycle

$$
i^1 = i, \quad i^2 = -1, \quad i^3 = i^2 \cdot i = -i, \quad i^4 = i^2 \cdot i^2 = 1, \quad i^5 = i, \ldots
$$

**Period four.** So $i^n$ depends only on the remainder of $n$ on division by $4$ — module 4.4's division algorithm, doing something useful.

To compute $i^{2026}$: since $2026 = 4 \cdot 506 + 2$,
$$
i^{2026} = \big(i^4\big)^{506}\cdot i^2 = 1 \cdot (-1) = -1.
$$

## Division

This is the one that needs a trick, and the trick is worth learning because it is the same one you used to rationalise denominators in Algebra I.

To simplify $\dfrac{1}{c+di}$, multiply top and bottom by $c - di$:
$$
\frac{1}{c+di} \cdot \frac{c-di}{c-di} = \frac{c-di}{c^2 + d^2}.
$$

The denominator became **real**, because
$$
(c+di)(c-di) = c^2 - (di)^2 = c^2 + d^2.
$$

Now split it up:
$$
\frac{1}{c+di} = \frac{c}{c^2+d^2} - \frac{d}{c^2+d^2}\,i,
$$
which is again of the form $a+bi$.

$$
\frac{1}{3+2i} = \frac{3-2i}{9+4} = \frac{3}{13} - \frac{2}{13}i.
$$

<Warning title="Why c² + d² ≠ 0 matters">
The division above is legal only when $c^2 + d^2 \neq 0$.

Since $c$ and $d$ are **real**, both squares are non-negative (module 5.1), so the sum is zero only when $c = d = 0$ — that is, only when $c + di = 0$.

So every nonzero complex number has a reciprocal, and $0$ does not. Exactly the situation in $\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathbb{R}$.

**Notice where realness was used.** The argument needs $c^2, d^2 \geq 0$, which holds for reals and not for complex numbers. The construction of $\mathbb{C}$ leans on the ordering of $\mathbb{R}$ even though $\mathbb{C}$ itself has no ordering (module 5.2).
</Warning>

## ℂ is a field

Run module 4.5's checklist:

| Property | Status |
|---|---|
| closed under $+$ | ✓ proved above |
| closed under $-$ | ✓ $-(a+bi) = (-a) + (-b)i$ |
| closed under $\times$ | ✓ proved above |
| reciprocals of nonzero | ✓ proved above |
| distributive law | ✓ inherited from $\mathbb{R}$ |

So $\mathbb{C}$ is a field, and **every algebraic technique from Algebra I transfers**: collecting terms, factoring, clearing denominators, solving linear equations.

What does *not* transfer is anything using inequalities, because module 5.2 removed the ordering. Sign charts, interval solutions, and "both sides positive" arguments all stop working.

<Aside>
Module 5.1 warned that inventing $\omega$ with $0\cdot\omega = 1$ collapses arithmetic. The table above is the answer to that worry for $i$.

Nothing broke. Every field property survived the extension, so the new system is at least as well behaved as the old one — and it is *strictly* better, since it can solve $x^2+1=0$.

**That is what makes $i$ legitimate and $\omega$ not**, and it is why this module is a checklist rather than a reassurance. The difference between a good extension and a bad one is decided by verification, and here is the verification.
</Aside>
