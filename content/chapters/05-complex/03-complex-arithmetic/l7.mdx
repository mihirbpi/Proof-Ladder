$$
(a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i, \qquad
(a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i.
$$

The multiplication formula is derived, not put forward: expand by distributivity and substitute $i^2 = -1$. Read structurally, multiplication is the unique $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear map on $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}\cdot1 \oplus \mathbb{R}\cdot i$ extending $1\cdot1 = 1$, $1 \cdot i = i$, $i^2 = -1$ — so the single relation $i^2=-1$ determines the whole product.

## Inversion

> **Proposition.** Every $z \in \mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ has a multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{C}$.

<Proof>
Let $z = a+bi \neq 0$. Then $(a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 - b^2i^2 = a^2+b^2 \in \mathbb{R}$.

Since $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$, $a^2+b^2 \geq 0$, with equality iff $a=b=0$, i.e. iff $z=0$. As $z \neq 0$, $a^2+b^2 > 0$ and we may divide by it in $\mathbb{R}$. Set
$$
w = \frac{a}{a^2+b^2} - \frac{b}{a^2+b^2}i \in \mathbb{C}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{C}$ is the complex numbers, $\in$ is "is in".*

Then $zw = \dfrac{(a+bi)(a-bi)}{a^2+b^2} = \dfrac{a^2+b^2}{a^2+b^2} = 1$.
</Proof>

Two remarks. First, the hypothesis $z \neq 0$ is spent exactly at $a^2+b^2 > 0$, and that step uses the order on $\mathbb{R}$ — the same fact ($x^2 \geq 0$) that made $i$ necessary is what makes division work in $\mathbb{C}$. Second, in the notation of §5.4 and §5.6 this reads $z^{-1} = \bar z/|z|^2$, which is the memorable form.

## All four operations work

All of §4.1's rules hold: addition is componentwise from $\mathbb{R}$; multiplicative associativity, commutativity, and distributivity follow from expansion and $i^2 = -1$; inverses exist by the proposition.

There is a shorter route to the same conclusion. Build the complex numbers as real polynomials with $x^2+1$ treated as zero (§5.1), and the whole verification collapses into one observation: because $x^2+1$ has no real root, nothing you can multiply by lands you at zero by accident, so division always works. The same construction adjoins a root of any polynomial that has none, which is how larger number systems get built.

This is the promised verification that adjoining $i$ breaks nothing. The only casualty is the order (§5.2).

<Warning title="The square-root rule breaks on complex numbers">
$$-1 = i\cdot i = \sqrt{-1}\sqrt{-1} = \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = 1.$$
The identity $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a\sqrt b$ holds for $a,b \geq 0$ real and fails on $\mathbb{C}$. Every complex number other than $0$ has two square roots, and §5.8 shows that no rule picks one of them consistently everywhere — so the two sides of the identity can pick different roots and disagree.

Practical rule: write $i$, never $\sqrt{-1}$; and never apply $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ to a complex number without saying which of the two roots you mean. The same caution applies to any fractional power.
</Warning>

## The powers of $i$, and why they cycle

$$i^0 = 1,\quad i^1 = i,\quad i^2 = -1,\quad i^3 = -i,\quad i^4 = 1,\ \dots$$

The pattern has period $4$, so $i^n$ depends only on the remainder $n$ leaves on division by $4$ — the sorting of §4.4, appearing in an unexpected place.

$$i^{2026}: \quad 2026 = 4\cdot506 + 2, \quad\text{so } i^{2026} = i^2 = -1.$$

The cycle is the mechanism behind Euler's formula (§5.7): expanding a product of circle points, the four-fold pattern is exactly what sorts the terms into two groups, one real and one carrying a single $i$. Those two groups turn out to be cosine and sine.

**Nothing about that split is a coincidence.** The sign alternation comes from $i^2 = -1$; the two-way separation comes from the period being $4$ rather than $2$. A number system whose special element cycled every three steps instead would split things three ways and produce a different family of functions.

Read geometrically, the cycle is four quarter-turns returning to the start, which is §5.9's subject and the reason the period is $4$ and not some other number.

## Multiplication by $i$

$$
1 \xmapsto{\ \cdot i\ } i \xmapsto{\ \cdot i\ } -1 \xmapsto{\ \cdot i\ } -i \xmapsto{\ \cdot i\ } 1.
$$

In the plane (§5.5) these are $(1,0) \to (0,1) \to (-1,0) \to (0,-1) \to (1,0)$: rotation by $\pi/2$ counterclockwise. In coordinates, multiplying by $i$ sends $(a,b)$ to $(-b,a)$, and doing that twice sends $(a,b)$ to $(-a,-b)$ — which is multiplying by $-1$ — the matrix statement of $i^2 = -1$.

More generally, multiplying by $a+bi$ acts on the plane as a rotation combined with a scaling — it turns everything by the angle of $a+bi$ and stretches everything by its distance from the origin. That is the geometric content of complex multiplication, and §5.9 makes it the main result.

<Aside>
Complex multiplication takes four real multiplications by the formula. **A standard trick** does it in three: with $t = (a+b)(c+d)$, $u = ac$, $v = bd$, the product is $(u - v) + (t - u - v)i$. The same idea applied recursively to integer multiplication gives an $O(n^{\log_2 3})$ algorithm, beating the schoolbook $O(n^2)$ — the first sign that arithmetic itself has not obvious complexity theory.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Complex arithmetic is the arithmetic the next two courses will actually run.

**Partial fractions over $\mathbb{C}$ are simpler than over $\mathbb{R}$.** Every denominator factors into linear terms, so every proper rational function decomposes into terms $\frac{A_k}{x - r_k}$ with no irreducible quadratics at all. The real decomposition then comes from pairing conjugate terms back together — which is where the $\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+px+q}$ blocks come from, and why they always arise in conjugate pairs.

**Conjugate roots explain the arctangent.** Integrating $\frac{1}{x^2+1}$ gives $\arctan x$; over $\mathbb{C}$ the same integral — the running total a rate builds up to, the other half of calculus — is a difference of two logarithms with conjugate arguments, and Euler's formula (§5.7) converts one form into the other. The two answers agree, and the complex route explains why an inverse trigonometric function appears in an integral with no trigonometry in sight.

</WhereThisGoes>
