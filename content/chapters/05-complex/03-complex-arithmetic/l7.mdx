$$
(a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i, \qquad
(a+bi)(c+di) = (ac-bd)+(ad+bc)i.
$$

The multiplication formula is derived, not posited: expand by distributivity and substitute $i^2 = -1$. Read structurally, multiplication is the unique $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear map on $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}\cdot1 \oplus \mathbb{R}\cdot i$ extending $1\cdot1 = 1$, $1 \cdot i = i$, $i^2 = -1$ — so the single relation $i^2=-1$ determines the whole product.

## Inversion

> **Proposition.** Every $z \in \mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ has a multiplicative inverse in $\mathbb{C}$.

<Proof>
Let $z = a+bi \neq 0$. Then $(a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 - b^2i^2 = a^2+b^2 \in \mathbb{R}$.

Since $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$, $a^2+b^2 \geq 0$, with equality iff $a=b=0$, i.e. iff $z=0$. As $z \neq 0$, $a^2+b^2 > 0$ and we may divide by it in $\mathbb{R}$. Set
$$
w = \frac{a}{a^2+b^2} - \frac{b}{a^2+b^2}i \in \mathbb{C}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{C}$ is the complex numbers, $\in$ is "is in".*

Then $zw = \dfrac{(a+bi)(a-bi)}{a^2+b^2} = \dfrac{a^2+b^2}{a^2+b^2} = 1$.
</Proof>

Two remarks. First, the hypothesis $z \neq 0$ is spent exactly at $a^2+b^2 > 0$, and that step uses the order on $\mathbb{R}$ — the same fact ($x^2 \geq 0$) that made $i$ necessary is what makes $\mathbb{C}$ a field. Second, in the notation of §5.4 and §5.6 this reads $z^{-1} = \bar z/|z|^2$, which is the memorable form.

## All four operations work

All axioms hold: additive structure is componentwise from $\mathbb{R}$; multiplicative associativity, commutativity, and distributivity follow from expansion and $i^2 = -1$; inverses exist by the proposition.

Structurally, $\mathbb{C} \cong \mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)$, and it is a field because $x^2+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$, hence generates a maximal ideal. That one sentence subsumes the whole verification, and it generalises: $K[x]/(p)$ is a field for any irreducible $p$, which is how every algebraic field extension is built.

This is the promised verification that adjoining $i$ breaks nothing. The only casualty is the order (§5.2).

<Warning title="The square-root rule breaks on complex numbers">
$$-1 = i\cdot i = \sqrt{-1}\sqrt{-1} = \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = 1.$$
The identity $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a\sqrt b$ holds for $a,b \geq 0$ real and fails on $\mathbb{C}$, because square roots there require a branch choice and no choice is consistent globally (§5.8).

Practical rule: write $i$, never $\sqrt{-1}$; and never apply $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ to a complex number without saying which root you mean. The same caution applies to $\log$ and to fractional powers.
</Warning>

## The powers of $i$, and why they cycle

$$i^0 = 1,\quad i^1 = i,\quad i^2 = -1,\quad i^3 = -i,\quad i^4 = 1,\ \dots$$

The pattern has period $4$, so $i^n$ depends only on $n \bmod 4$ — the residue classes of §4.4, appearing in an unexpected place.

$$i^{2026}: \quad 2026 = 4\cdot506 + 2, \quad\text{so } i^{2026} = i^2 = -1.$$

The cycle is the mechanism behind Euler's formula (§5.7): substituting $i\theta$ into the exponential series, the four-fold pattern is exactly what sorts the terms into two groups, one real with alternating signs and one imaginary with alternating signs. Those two groups are the cosine and sine series.

**Nothing about that split is a coincidence.** The sign alternation comes from $i^2 = -1$; the even/odd separation comes from the period being $4$ rather than $2$. A number system with $j^3 = 1$ would produce a three-way split and a different pair of functions — and does, in the theory of the Fourier transform over other groups.

Read geometrically, the cycle is four quarter-turns returning to the start, which is §5.9's subject and the reason the period is $4$ and not some other number.

## Multiplication by $i$

$$
1 \xmapsto{\ \cdot i\ } i \xmapsto{\ \cdot i\ } -1 \xmapsto{\ \cdot i\ } -i \xmapsto{\ \cdot i\ } 1.
$$

In the plane (§5.5) these are $(1,0) \to (0,1) \to (-1,0) \to (0,-1) \to (1,0)$: rotation by $\pi/2$ counterclockwise. As a real-linear map, multiplication by $i$ has matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, whose square is $-I$ — the matrix statement of $i^2 = -1$.

More generally, multiplication by $a+bi$ is the real-linear map
$$
\begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a\end{pmatrix},
$$
whose determinant is $a^2+b^2 = |z|^2$ and which is a rotation-scaling. This gives an alternative construction of $\mathbb{C}$: it is the subring of $M_2(\mathbb{R})$ consisting of such matrices, and the field axioms are then matrix facts. §5.9 develops the rotation reading in full.

<Aside>
Complex multiplication takes four real multiplications by the formula. **Karatsuba's trick** does it in three: with $t = (a+b)(c+d)$, $u = ac$, $v = bd$, the product is $(u - v) + (t - u - v)i$. The same idea applied recursively to integer multiplication gives an $O(n^{\log_2 3})$ algorithm, beating the schoolbook $O(n^2)$ — the first sign that arithmetic itself has not obvious complexity theory.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Complex arithmetic is the arithmetic the next two courses will actually run.

**Partial fractions over $\mathbb{C}$ are simpler than over $\mathbb{R}$.** Every denominator factors into linear terms, so every proper rational function decomposes into terms $\frac{A_k}{x - r_k}$ with no irreducible quadratics at all. The real decomposition is then obtained by pairing conjugate terms back together — which is where the $\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+px+q}$ blocks come from, and why they always arise in conjugate pairs.

**Conjugate roots explain the arctangent.** Integrating $\frac{1}{x^2+1}$ gives $\arctan x$; over $\mathbb{C}$ the same integral is a difference of two logarithms with conjugate arguments, and Euler's formula (§5.7) converts one form into the other. The two answers agree, and the complex route explains why an inverse trigonometric function appears in an integral with no trigonometry in sight.
