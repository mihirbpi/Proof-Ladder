Module 5.2 removed the ordering: there is no sensible way to say one complex number is *less than* another. But you can still ask how **big** one is.

> For $z = a+bi$, the **modulus** is
> $$ |z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}. $$

On the plane this is the distance from $z$ to the origin, by the Pythagorean theorem: the legs are $a$ and $b$, and $|z|$ is the hypotenuse.

$$
|3+4i| = \sqrt{9+16} = 5.
$$

The square root is defined because $a^2 + b^2 \geq 0$ for real $a, b$, and **$|z|$ is a real number** — which is the point. Comparing sizes is possible again, because the sizes live in $\mathbb{R}$ where $\leq$ still works.

## It extends absolute value

For a real number $a$, treating it as $a + 0i$ gives
$$
|a| = \sqrt{a^2 + 0} = \sqrt{a^2} = |a|,
$$
the ordinary **absolute value** of module 3.2. So the new notation agrees with the old one wherever both apply, and reusing the symbol is safe.

## The link to conjugation

Module 5.4 computed $z\bar z = a^2+b^2$. So
$$
|z|^2 = z\bar z.
$$

**This is the working form.** Proofs about moduli should almost always square first and use conjugates, rather than manipulating square roots — the algebra of $\bar z$ is easy, and the algebra of $\sqrt{\ }$ is not.

## Distance between two points

$$
|z - w| \ \text{ is the distance from } z \text{ to } w.
$$

Subtracting slides $w$ to the origin and carries $z$ along with it, so the gap is unchanged. That gives a compact way to describe shapes:

$$
|z| = 1 \quad\text{is the unit circle,}
$$
$$
|z - (2+i)| = 3 \quad\text{is the circle of radius } 3 \text{ centerd at } 2+i,
$$
$$
|z| < 1 \quad\text{is the open disk inside the unit circle.}
$$

Compare your geometry course, which defines a circle as the set of points at a fixed distance from a center. **That is the same definition**, written with $|z - w|$ instead of the word *distance*.

## Modulus is multiplicative

> **Theorem.** For all $z, w \in \mathbb{C}$: $\ |zw| = |z|\,|w|$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$|z|^2 = z\bar z$, and conjugation passes through products (module 5.4).</div>
  <div slot="want">The identity — and both sides are non-negative reals, so it is enough to prove the squares are equal.</div>
  <div slot="how">Square the left side, use $|z|^2 = z\bar z$, and rearrange. Nothing but commutativity is needed once the conjugate form is in play. Squaring first is what avoids all the radicals.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
$$
|zw|^2 = (zw)\overline{(zw)} = (zw)(\bar z\,\bar w) = (z\bar z)(w \bar w) = |z|^2|w|^2.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

So $|zw|^2 = \big(|z||w|\big)^2$. Both $|zw|$ and $|z||w|$ are non-negative reals, and two non-negative reals with equal squares are equal.

Hence $|zw| = |z||w|$.
</Proof>

Note the last step, which is doing real work: from $x^2 = y^2$ you may conclude $x = y$ **only** when you know both are non-negative. Otherwise $x = -y$ is available.

A consequence, by repeating the theorem: $|z^n| = |z|^n$.

## The triangle inequality

$$
|z+w| \leq |z| + |w|.
$$

The direct route from $z$ to $z + w$ is never longer than going via $z$'s head — which is your geometry course's statement that any side of a triangle is at most the sum of the other two, with the arrow picture of module 5.5 supplying the triangle.

Equality holds exactly when the two arrows point the same way, so the "triangle" is flat.

<Warning title="Modulus is not an ordering">
$|z| \leq |w|$ compares two **real numbers**, the sizes. It does not order $z$ and $w$ themselves, and module 5.2's obstruction is untouched.

Two different complex numbers can have the same modulus — $3$, $-3$, $3i$ and $-3i$ all have modulus $3$ — so the modulus loses information. It tells you how far from the origin a number sits and nothing at all about which direction.

Module 5.8 supplies the missing half.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Numbers of modulus $1$ form the unit circle, and they are closed under multiplication: if $|z| = |w| = 1$ then $|zw| = 1 \cdot 1 = 1$.

Multiplying by such a number therefore changes no distance from the origin. A transformation that fixes the origin and preserves all distances is, by your geometry course's classification, a **rotation**.

So the numbers of modulus $1$ are exactly the rotations of the plane — and module 5.5 already showed one case of this, when multiplying by $i$ turned out to be a quarter turn. Module 5.9 makes the general statement precise.
</Aside>
