$\mathbb{C}$ has no order (module 5.2), so complex numbers cannot be compared directly. What can be compared is their **size**, and size is a real number.

## Definition

For $z = a+bi$, the **modulus** is
$$|z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}.$$

That is the distance from the origin to the point $(a,b)$ — **the distance formula**, which is the Pythagorean theorem in coordinates.

$$|3+4i| = \sqrt{9+16} = 5.$$

The $3$–$4$–$5$ triangle from your first geometry unit, appearing as a complex modulus.

## It extends absolute value

For a real number $x = x+0i$:
$$|x| = \sqrt{x^2+0} = \sqrt{x^2} = |x|,$$
the ordinary absolute value. So the notation is consistent — the new $|\ \cdot\ |$ agrees with the old one on $\mathbb{R}$, which is what an extension must do.

## Relation to the conjugate

$$
z\bar z = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2+b^2 = |z|^2.
$$

So $|z| = \sqrt{z\bar z}$, and

$$
\frac1z = \frac{\bar z}{|z|^2} \quad (z\neq0),
$$

which is module 5.3's division formula in compact form. Also $|\bar z| = |z|$: reflection preserves distance, exactly as your course requires of a rigid motion.

## Multiplicativity

> **Theorem.** $|zw| = |z|\,|w|$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$|z|^2 = z\bar z$, and conjugation distributes over products (module 5.4).</div>
  <div slot="want">$|zw| = |z||w|$, an equation between non-negative reals.</div>
  <div slot="how">Squares are easier than square roots, and both sides are non-negative, so proving the squares equal is enough. Then the conjugate rule does the work in two lines — far cleaner than expanding components.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Both $|zw|$ and $|z||w|$ are non-negative reals, so it is enough to show their squares agree.

$$|zw|^2 = (zw)\overline{(zw)} = zw\,\bar z\bar w = (z\bar z)(w\bar w) = |z|^2|w|^2,$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

using $\overline{zw} = \bar z\bar w$ and commutativity.

Taking non-negative square roots, $|zw| = |z||w|$.
</Proof>

**Geometrically: multiplying scales distances.** Multiplying every point by $w$ multiplies every distance from the origin by $|w|$ — which is the definition of a dilation with factor $|w|$ (module 3.1).

That is half of module 5.9's result. The other half is what happens to angles.

<Warning title="Modulus does not distribute over sums">
$$|z+w| \neq |z|+|w| \quad\text{in general.}$$

Take $z = 3$ and $w = -3$: the left side is $0$ and the right is $6$.

The correct statement is an inequality, and it is one you know.
</Warning>

## The triangle inequality

> **Theorem.** $|z+w| \leq |z|+|w|$.

This is the Triangle Inequality from your geometry course: *the sum of two sides of a triangle exceeds the third.*

Plot $0$, $z$, and $z+w$. The three sides have lengths $|z|$, $|w|$ (the segment from $z$ to $z+w$), and $|z+w|$. The theorem says the direct route is never longer than the detour.

**Equality holds exactly when $z$ and $w$ point the same way** — when the "triangle" is degenerate and the three points are collinear. That matches your course's version, where the strict inequality requires an actual triangle.

The distance between two complex numbers is
$$d(z,w) = |z-w|,$$
and in components this is $\sqrt{(a-c)^2+(b-d)^2}$ — the distance formula, unchanged. Complex notation has not introduced a new geometry; it has compressed the one you have.

## Loci by modulus

$$|z| = r \quad\text{— circle, radius } r, \text{ center } 0$$
$$|z - c| = r \quad\text{— circle, radius } r, \text{ center } c$$
$$|z-c| < r \quad\text{— the open disk}$$
$$|z-c| \leq r \quad\text{— the closed disk}$$
$$|z-w| = |z-v| \quad\text{— perpendicular bisector of } \overline{wv}$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

Compare with module 2.2's set-builder version:
$$\{z\in\mathbb{C} : |z - c| = r\}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{C}$ is the complex numbers, $\in$ is "is in".*

Same locus, and the complex form needs one variable where real coordinates need two. That economy is why complex notation is standard in subjects — electrical engineering, fluid dynamics — where plane geometry is done constantly.

## The unit circle

$$U = \{z\in\mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\mathbb{C}$ is the complex numbers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: the complex numbers at distance exactly $1$ from the origin — a circle.*

This set is closed under multiplication: if $|z|=|w|=1$ then $|zw| = 1\cdot1 = 1$, by the theorem above. It contains $1$, and each element has an inverse in the set, since $|1/z| = 1/|z| = 1$.

So $U$ is closed under multiplication, contains $1$, and contains an inverse for each of its members. Those three properties are exactly what module 3.7's aside called a **group**.

**And this one is the rotations of the plane.** Multiplying by a number of modulus $1$ changes no distance, so it is a rigid motion fixing the origin, which by your course's classification is a rotation. Module 5.9 makes the correspondence exact.

<Aside>
The properties proved here — $|z|\geq0$ with equality only at $0$, $|zw|=|z||w|$, and the triangle inequality — are the axioms of a **norm**.

Once a system has a notion of size, it has distances; once it has distances, closeness can be defined; and once closeness is available, calculus becomes possible. That is the route by which the complex numbers get a calculus of their own — and it turns out to be a good deal better behaved than the real one.

The modulus is what makes any of it possible, and it is nothing but the distance formula you proved from the Pythagorean theorem.
</Aside>
