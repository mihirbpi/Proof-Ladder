For $z = a+bi$, the **modulus** is
$$
|z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2} \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in".*

This is the distance from the origin to $z$ in the complex plane — the right-triangle distance rule, with the across and up amounts as the two legs.

## Three identities

$$
|z|^2 = z\bar z, \qquad |z| \geq 0 \text{ with equality iff } z = 0, \qquad |a| = \sqrt{a^2} \text{ for } a \in \mathbb{R}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in".*

The first restates module 5.4's $z\bar z = a^2+b^2$; the second holds because a sum of real squares vanishes only when both do; the third says modulus **extends** absolute value rather than replacing it — the test any good extension must pass.

The identity $|z|^2 = z\bar z$ also cleans up inversion (module 5.3):
$$
z^{-1} = \frac{\bar z}{|z|^2}.
$$

## Multiplicativity

> **Proposition.** $|zw| = |z|\,|w|$ for all $z,w \in \mathbb{C}$.

<Proof>
Using $|u|^2 = u\bar u$ and the fact that conjugation preserves products (module 5.4):
$$
|zw|^2 = (zw)\overline{(zw)} = z w \bar z \bar w = (z\bar z)(w\bar w) = |z|^2|w|^2.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

Both $|zw|$ and $|z||w|$ are non-negative reals with equal squares, so they are equal.
</Proof>

Note how much shorter this is than expanding $\sqrt{(ac-bd)^2 + (ad+bc)^2}$ directly. Working with $|z|^2 = z\bar z$ rather than with square roots is the standard technique, and it is worth adopting as a habit.

Geometrically the proposition says multiplication scales distances by a fixed factor — the first half of module 5.9's "multiply the lengths, add the angles."

## Distance

$|z - w|$ is the distance between $z$ and $w$, since $z-w$ is the displacement vector from $w$ to $z$.

$$
|(4+5i)-(1+i)| = |3+4i| = 5.
$$

So the complex numbers come with a notion of **distance**, even though module 5.2 showed they cannot be put in order. You cannot say which of two complex numbers is bigger, but you can say how far apart they are, and analysis needs only the latter.

## Loci

| Equation | Locus |
|---|---|
| $\lvert z\rvert = r$ | circle, radius $r$, center $0$ |
| $\lvert z - w\rvert = r$ | circle, radius $r$, center $w$ |
| $\lvert z - w\rvert < r$ | open disc |
| $\lvert z-p\rvert = \lvert z-q\rvert$ | perpendicular bisector of $pq$ |

Deriving the first: $|z| = r$ with $z = x+yi$ means $\sqrt{x^2+y^2} = r$, and squaring gives $x^2+y^2 = r^2$ — the ordinary circle equation. Squaring is safe here because both sides are non-negative.

The **unit circle** $|z| = 1$ — every point exactly one unit from the origin — is the set module 5.8 describes by direction and module 5.9 turns things around.

## The triangle inequality

$$
|z + w| \leq |z| + |w|,
$$
with equality iff $z$ and $w$ point in the same direction. Geometrically: the direct route is never longer than going via a corner — which is exactly what the $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle showed, $5 \leq 3+4$.

<Warning title="Modulus is real, and squaring is the tool">
$|z| \in \mathbb{R}_{\geq0}$ always. Writing $|3+4i| = 5i$ or treating $|z|$ as complex is a type error.

And when proving a modulus identity, square both sides and use $|u|^2 = u\bar u$. Manipulating $\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ directly is almost always harder and sometimes impossible.
</Warning>

<Aside>
$|zw| = |z||w|$ has an arithmetic consequence. Expanding both sides in coordinates gives Diophantus's identity
$$(a^2+b^2)(c^2+d^2) = (ac-bd)^2 + (ad+bc)^2,$$
which says: **a product of two sums of two squares is itself a sum of two squares.** That is a genuine theorem of number theory, and complex multiplication is where it comes from.
</Aside>
