Since $z = a+bi$ is determined by the pair $(a,b) \in \mathbb{R}^2$ (module 5.2), $\mathbb{C}$ can be drawn as a plane: $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ horizontal, $\operatorname{Im}(z)$ vertical.

    {re: 4, im: 0, label: "4"}
  ]} range={5} />
</Figure>

The **real axis** is $\{z : \operatorname{Im} z = 0\} = \mathbb{R}$; the **imaginary axis** is $\{z : \operatorname{Re} z = 0\}$.

## Addition is vector addition

$(a+bi)+(c+di) = (a+c)+(b+d)i$ is componentwise, so it is exactly vector addition in $\mathbb{R}^2$: the **parallelogram rule**.

<Figure alt="Two arrows drawn from the origin to 3 plus i and to 1 plus 2i, with the sum marked at 4 plus 3i." caption="$(3+i)+(1+2i) = 4+3i$.">
  <ComplexPlane
    vectors={[{re: 3, im: 1}, {re: 1, im: 2}]}
    points={[{re: 4, im: 3, label: "4+3i"}]}
    range={5} />
</Figure>

So as an *additive* structure, $\mathbb{C}$ is nothing but $\mathbb{R}^2$. Everything distinctive comes from multiplication, which $\mathbb{R}^2$ does not have.

That is worth stating carefully, because it is the whole reason the plane picture is useful rather than merely available. The addition facts of module 5.3 — commutativity, associativity, the additive identity, additive inverses — are all inherited from $\mathbb{R}^2$, and their proofs are componentwise and dull. Nothing about $i$ is involved in any of them. The interesting content of Chapter 5 lives entirely in the multiplicative structure, and the plane is the setting where that structure becomes visible.

## Conjugation is reflection

$\bar z = a - bi$ negates the second coordinate: reflection in the real axis.

<Figure alt="A point at 3 plus 2i and its reflection at 3 minus 2i below the horizontal axis." caption="Conjugation reflects in the real axis.">
  <ComplexPlane points={[
    {re: 3, im: 2, label: "z"},
    {re: 3, im: -2, label: "z̄"}
  ]} range={4} />
</Figure>

This makes module 5.4's theorem geometric. A reflection fixes exactly the points of its mirror line; the mirror is the real axis; so $z = \bar z \iff z \in \mathbb{R}$ — *a number equals its own conjugate exactly when it is real*. The two-direction algebraic proof and the one-line geometric argument prove the same fact, and having both is the point.

Note also that $\overline{\bar z} = z$ is now visibly the statement that reflecting twice is the identity.

## Multiplication by $i$ is rotation by $90°$

> **Proposition.** For every $z \in \mathbb{C}$, the point $iz$ is $z$ rotated $90°$ counterclockwise about the origin.

<Proof>
Let $z = a+bi$. Then
$$
iz = i(a+bi) = ai + bi^2 = -b + ai,
$$
so $z$ at $(a,b)$ maps to $(-b, a)$.

Rotation by $90°$ counterclockwise sends $(a,b)$ to $(-b,a)$ — check the basis vectors: $(1,0)$ goes to $(0,1)$, and $(0,1)$ goes to $(-1,0)$, which is exactly this map. So multiplication by $i$ is that rotation.
</Proof>

<Figure alt="Four points marked at the compass positions one, i, minus one, and minus i around the origin." caption="Repeated multiplication by $i$ cycles the compass points.">
  <ComplexPlane points={[
    {re: 1, im: 0, label: "1"},
    {re: 0, im: 1, label: "i"},
    {re: -1, im: 0, label: "-1"},
    {re: 0, im: -1, label: "-i"}
  ]} range={2} />
</Figure>

So $i^2 = -1$ says: *two quarter turns equal a half turn*. The defining algebraic property of $i$ is a geometric identity, and module 5.9 shows this is the special case of a general rule — every complex multiplication rotates and scales.

Later, when you meet the number grids called *matrices*, multiplying by $i$ turns out to be exactly the grid that rotates the plane a quarter turn.

Two consequences follow at once. Since four quarter turns is the identity, $i^4 = 1$ for the same reason, and therefore $i^n$ depends only on the remainder of $n$ on division by $4$ — the powers of $i$ cycle with period four, and the geometric statement and the arithmetic one are the same fact. And since a rotation preserves distance from the origin, $|iz| = |z|$ for every $z$, which module 5.6 will prove algebraically and which the picture gives for free.

<Warning title="Not a function graph">
The complex plane plots the *domain*, not a graph. Each point is one complex number.

Graphing $f : \mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ would require four real dimensions, so complex functions are visualized instead by domain coloring, by drawing source and target planes side by side, or by plotting $|f|$ as a surface. Expecting a curve is a category error.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The plane representation is due to Wessel (1799), Argand (1806), and Gauss, and it is what ended the controversy over whether complex numbers exist. That question dissolves once you can point at them — which is a reasonable general lesson about mathematical objects: legitimacy comes from a construction, and a picture is a construction you can see.
</Aside>
