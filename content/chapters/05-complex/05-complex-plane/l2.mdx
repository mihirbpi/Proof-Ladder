To **plot** a complex number, start at the middle. Go across by the first part. Go up by the second part. Mark the spot.

## Adding is joining the walks

Remember module 5.3: to add, you do one walk and then the other.

On the grid, that means putting the second arrow's tail at the first arrow's head. Where you finish is the answer.

<Figure alt="A grid with two arrows drawn from the centre, one to 3 across 1 up and one to 1 across 2 up, and a dot at 4 across 3 up showing the sum.">
  <ComplexPlane
    vectors={[{re: 3, im: 1}, {re: 1, im: 2}]}
    points={[{re: 4, im: 3, label: "sum"}]}
    range={5} />
</Figure>

$(3 + 1i) + (1 + 2i) = 4 + 3i$. And on the grid, the two arrows joined nose-to-tail land you exactly there.

## The flat line

Look at $4 + 0i$ in the first picture. It has no up part, so it sits right on the horizontal line.

Every ordinary number sits on that line. We call it **the flat line**, and it is the old number line you have always used — now living inside a bigger picture.

## The mirror is the flat line

In module 5.4 you learned the flip: keep the across part, turn the up part upside down.

Now look at where that happens on the grid. $3 + 2i$ is two squares above the flat line. Its flip, $3 - 2i$, is two squares *below*.

The flat line is the mirror!

And that explains something. Numbers sitting *on* the flat line do not move when you flip them — because they are already on the mirror. Those are exactly the ordinary numbers, which is exactly what module 5.4 worked out with numbers.

<Aside>
This is one of the nicest things about the complex numbers. The counting and the picture always agree. If a proof feels fiddly, draw it — and if a drawing feels vague, work it out with the numbers. Each one keeps the other honest.
</Aside>
