The bijection $z \mapsto (\operatorname{Re} z, \operatorname{Im} z)$ identifies $\mathbb{C}$ with $\mathbb{R}^2$ as sets and as real vector spaces (§5.2). Drawing that identification is the **complex plane** or Argand diagram.

## The three operations, geometrically

**Addition** is componentwise, hence vector addition — the parallelogram rule. Nothing about $\mathbb{C}$ beyond its additive group structure is involved.

**Conjugation** negates the second coordinate: reflection in the real axis. Its fixed set is the mirror line, which recovers §5.4's theorem $z = \bar z \iff z \in \mathbb{R}$ without computation, and $\overline{\bar z} = z$ becomes "reflecting twice is the identity."

**Multiplication** is where $\mathbb{C}$ departs from $\mathbb{R}^2$.

> **Proposition.** Multiplication by $z = a+bi$ is the $\mathbb{R}$-linear map with matrix
> $$M_z = \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a\end{pmatrix},$$
> a rotation by $\arg z$ composed with a scaling by $|z|$.

<Proof>
For $w = x+yi$, $zw = (ax - by) + (bx + ay)i$, so in coordinates $(x,y) \mapsto (ax-by,\ bx+ay)$, which is $M_z$ applied to $(x,y)^T$.

Writing $r = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ and, for $z \neq 0$, $\cos\theta = a/r$, $\sin\theta = b/r$,
$$
M_z = r\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix},
$$
the product of a scaling by $r$ and the standard rotation matrix through $\theta$.
</Proof>

Taking $z = i$ gives $M_i = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, rotation by $\pi/2$, and $M_i^2 = -I$ is the matrix form of $i^2 = -1$. Note also $\det M_z = a^2+b^2 = |z|^2$, so multiplication by $z$ scales areas by $|z|^2$ — a fact that reappears in the change-of-variables formula for complex integrals.

## Loci in one variable

Complex notation compresses plane geometry substantially, and the compression is not cosmetic.

$$|z| = r \quad\text{circle, radius } r, \text{ centre } 0$$
$$|z - c| = r \quad\text{circle, radius } r, \text{ centre } c$$
$$|z - c| < r \quad\text{open disc}$$
$$|z - w| = |z - v| \quad\text{perpendicular bisector of the segment } wv$$
$$\operatorname{Im}(z) = 0 \quad\text{the real axis}$$
$$\left|\frac{z-a}{z-b}\right| = k \quad\text{a circle (or a line when } k=1)$$

Each needs one complex variable where rectangular coordinates need two, and each condition is a single equation where the real version needs expanding and collecting.

The last is worth noting: the set of points whose distances to two fixed points have a constant ratio is a circle — the **Apollonius circle** — and proving it in rectangular coordinates takes half a page. In complex notation it is a short manipulation of moduli.

That economy is why complex notation is standard in electrical engineering and fluid dynamics, where plane geometry is done constantly and the objects are naturally rotational.

## What the plane makes visible

Sets defined by algebraic conditions become curves and regions:

| Condition | Locus |
|---|---|
| $\operatorname{Im} z = 0$ | real axis |
| $\lvert z\rvert = r$ | circle of radius $r$ about $0$ |
| $\lvert z - w\rvert = r$ | circle of radius $r$ about $w$ |
| $\lvert z - p\rvert = \lvert z - q\rvert$ | perpendicular bisector of $pq$ |
| $\lvert z\rvert < 1$ | open unit disc |
| $z\bar z = 1$ | unit circle (§5.6) |

Conversely, geometric theorems become algebraic identities. The parallelogram law
$$
|z+w|^2 + |z-w|^2 = 2|z|^2 + 2|w|^2
$$
is a two-line computation with conjugates and a genuine theorem of Euclidean geometry. This translation in both directions is what makes complex numbers a standard tool in plane geometry.

<Warning title="The plane is the domain, not a graph">
A graph of $f : \mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}$ lives in $\mathbb{R}^4$. Standard substitutes are domain colouring (hue = argument, brightness = modulus), plotting $|f|$ as a surface, and drawing source and target planes side by side with the images of a grid.

Relatedly: nearness in $\mathbb{C}$ means nearness in the *plane*, not along a line. Convergence $z_n \to z$ means $|z_n - z| \to 0$, and approach may occur along any path — which is exactly why complex differentiability is so restrictive.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Adjoining a single point at infinity gives the **Riemann sphere** $\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}$, homeomorphic to $S^2$ via stereographic projection. On it, Möbius transformations $z \mapsto \frac{az+b}{cz+d}$ act as the full group of angle-preserving transformations, circles and lines become one family (a line is a circle through $\infty$), and division by zero acquires a consistent meaning. It is the natural home for rational functions.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
The plane picture is what makes complex arithmetic usable rather than merely formal.

**Complex addition is vector addition**, so everything you know about the parallelogram rule transfers unchanged. That identification is exact: as a real vector space, $\mathbb{C}$ *is* $\mathbb{R}^2$, and the two only differ once multiplication is introduced.

**Multiplication is where the plane earns its keep.** $\mathbb{R}^2$ has no useful multiplication; $\mathbb{C}$ has one, and §5.9 shows it is rotation combined with scaling. That single fact makes complex numbers the natural language for anything rotating.

**In calculus, that is why phasors work.** A sinusoidal quantity $A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ is the real part of $Ae^{i\phi}e^{i\omega t}$ — a fixed complex number times a rotating one. Differentiating multiplies by $i\omega$, which is a $90°$ rotation and a scaling, so differential equations in $t$ become algebraic equations in $\mathbb{C}$.

The technique is standard in every treatment of oscillation, from AC circuits to wave mechanics, and it works because differentiation of $e^{i\omega t}$ is multiplication by a constant — the defining property of the exponential, now with a geometric reading.

**Loci become one-variable equations.** $|z - c| = r$ is a circle, $|z-w| = |z-v|$ a perpendicular bisector, $\operatorname{Im} z = 0$ the real axis. Each needs two real variables and two real equations otherwise, and the compression is why complex notation is standard in plane geometry problems.

**In linear algebra, the same plane carries the eigenvector picture.** Complex eigenvalues of a real $2\times2$ matrix always come in conjugate pairs $\alpha\pm\beta i$, and the transformation acts on a plane as a rotation by $\arg(\alpha+\beta i)$ scaled by $|\alpha+\beta i|$ — the classification of $2\times2$ real matrices with no real eigenvalues, read straight off this module.

*You have matrices, determinants and systems; linear algebra builds its vocabulary on top of them. Above: **eigenvalue** — a factor by which a matrix stretches a direction without turning it; **eigenvector** — a direction a matrix stretches without turning; **vector space** — any system where you can add and scale.*
</WhereThisGoes>
