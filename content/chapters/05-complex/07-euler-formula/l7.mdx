$$
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \qquad (\theta \in \mathbb{R}).
$$

## The definition being used

$e^z$ for complex $z$ is **defined** by the series
$$
e^z := \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{n!},
$$
which converges absolutely for every $z \in \mathbb{C}$ by comparison with $\sum |z|^n/n!$, itself convergent by the ratio test. This is not a theorem about a previously known function — for complex arguments there is nothing else $e^z$ could mean, and the definition is justified by the fact that it agrees with the real exponential on $\mathbb{R}$ and satisfies $e^{z+w} = e^ze^w$.

Similarly $\cos$ and $\sin$ are taken as their series, which converge absolutely everywhere.

## Proof

<Proof>
The powers of $i$ satisfy $i^{2k} = (-1)^k$ and $i^{2k+1} = (-1)^k i$, immediately from $i^2 = -1$ and induction.

Substituting $z = i\theta$:
$$
e^{i\theta} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(i\theta)^n}{n!}.
$$

The series converges absolutely, since $\sum |i\theta|^n/n! = \sum |\theta|^n/n! = e^{|\theta|} < \infty$. Absolute convergence permits arbitrary rearrangement, so we may split into even and odd $n$:
$$
e^{i\theta} = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(i\theta)^{2k}}{(2k)!} + \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(i\theta)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}
= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k}}{(2k)!} + i\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}.
$$

The two series are precisely those defining $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$. Hence $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$.
</Proof>

<Warning title="The rearrangement needs justification">
Splitting an infinite series into two subseries and summing separately is **not** valid in general. Riemann's rearrangement theorem: a conditionally convergent real series can be rearranged to converge to any prescribed value, or to diverge. The standard example is the alternating harmonic series.

Absolute convergence is exactly the hypothesis that makes rearrangement safe, and it is why the estimate $\sum|\theta|^n/n! < \infty$ appears in the proof rather than being taken as read. A derivation that omits it is a formal manipulation, not a proof.
</Warning>

## An alternative proof

<Proof title="Proof via a differential equation">
Fix $\theta$ and define $f(\theta) = e^{-i\theta}(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$. Differentiating (term-by-term differentiation of a power series being valid inside its disc of convergence):
$$
f'(\theta) = -ie^{-i\theta}(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) + e^{-i\theta}(-\sin\theta + i\cos\theta) = e^{-i\theta}\big(-i\cos\theta + \sin\theta - \sin\theta + i\cos\theta\big) = 0.
$$
So $f$ is constant, and $f(0) = 1\cdot 1 = 1$. Hence $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta = e^{i\theta}$.
</Proof>

The two proofs make different points. The series proof shows *why* the identity holds — the cycle of $i$ sorts the terms. The ODE proof shows that $e^{i\theta}$ is the unique solution of $y' = iy$, $y(0)=1$, which is the conceptual reason: multiplication by $i$ is rotation by a right angle (§5.5), so a point whose velocity is always $i$ times its position moves perpendicular to its radius — i.e. in a circle at unit speed.

## Reading the formula geometrically

$$|e^{i\theta}| = \sqrt{\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta} = 1$$

by the Pythagorean identity, so $e^{i\theta}$ lies on the unit circle for every real $\theta$, with coordinates $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ — the standard parametrisation of the circle.

**$e^{i\theta}$ is the point reached by travelling $\theta$ radians counterclockwise from $1$.** The formula is a dictionary between arc length and a complex number, and the radian requirement is exactly what makes it exact: an arc of $\theta$ radians on the unit circle *has length* $\theta$.

The special values then read as geometry rather than algebra:

$$e^{i\pi/2} = i \quad\text{— a quarter turn}$$
$$e^{i\pi} = -1 \quad\text{— a half turn}$$
$$e^{2\pi i} = 1 \quad\text{— a full turn}$$

And the exponent law becomes a statement about composing rotations. $e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta} = e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}$ says turning by $\alpha$ then by $\beta$ is turning by $\alpha+\beta$ — which is §5.9, and which makes $i^2 = -1$ the statement that two quarter turns make a half turn.

**Trigonometry and exponentials looked unrelated because you met them over $\mathbb{R}$**, where one oscillates and the other grows. Over $\mathbb{C}$ they are one function seen along two different lines through the origin, and the real line was simply too small a stage to see the connection on.

## Consequences

$$
|e^{i\theta}|^2 = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1,
$$
so $e^{i\theta}$ lies on the unit circle, and $\theta \mapsto e^{i\theta}$ is a surjective operation-preserving map $(\mathbb{R},+) \to (S^1,\times)$ with kernel $2\pi\mathbb{Z}$. Hence $\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z} \cong S^1$, which is the structural statement behind the non-uniqueness of polar form (§5.8).

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i},
$$
which are §5.4's $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$ formulas applied to $e^{i\theta}$, and which convert every trigonometric identity into an exponential one. Most trigonometric manipulations are easier this way, and §5.9 exploits it.

Note also $\overline{e^{i\theta}} = e^{-i\theta}$ and $e^{a+bi} = e^a e^{ib} = e^a(\cos b + i\sin b)$, so the complex exponential is $2\pi i$-periodic — a property the real exponential does not have, and the source of the multivaluedness of complex $\log$.

<Aside>
$e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$ is Euler's identity. Its aesthetic reputation is deserved but the mathematical content is the periodicity: $e^{i\pi} = -1$ says half a turn is a reflection, and $e^{2\pi i}=1$ says a full turn is the identity. The identity is the statement that the exponential wraps the line around the circle.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Euler's formula is the identity that unifies the three families of functions you have studied separately.

**It explains why one technique handles both growth and oscillation.** Exponentials grow; sines and cosines wobble. They look like different animals, and every method you learn for one seems to need a rebuild for the other. Euler's formula says they are the same animal seen from two angles — and once you know that, a single method covers both, with real numbers giving growth and complex ones giving the wobble.

That is not a curiosity. Problems about springs, circuits and populations all reduce to the same equation, and the reason one technique solves all of them is the identity in this module.

**One caution worth carrying.** The derivation rearranged an endless sum, and rearranging an endless sum is not always allowed — some can be rearranged into any answer you like. There is a condition that licenses it, this one satisfies it, and learning to notice when that condition is needed is a large part of what a later analysis course trains.

</WhereThisGoes>
