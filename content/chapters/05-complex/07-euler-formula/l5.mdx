This module states a result you cannot yet prove, because the proof needs two tools you have not met. It is here because the *statement* is reachable, and because it explains why module 5.8's polar form is written the way it is.

## The question

Algebra I gave you exponential functions: $2^x$ and $10^x$. There is one more, with a base you have not met yet — a particular number written $e$, worth about $2.718$, which you will meet properly in Algebra II. For now treat $e^x$ as one more exponential function of the same kind.

Every one of them was defined for **real** $x$. So here is a fair question:

$$
\text{what is } e^{i} \ ?
$$

Nothing you have been taught says. Raising a number to an imaginary power is not repeated multiplication, and no rule from Algebra I covers it.

It turns out there is exactly one sensible answer, and it is startling.

## The answer

> **Raising $e$ to an imaginary power lands you on the unit circle.**

Specifically, $e^{i\theta}$ is the point at distance $1$ from the origin, reached by traveling $\theta$ units **along the circle**, counterclockwise from $1$.

The exponent is an **arc length**, not an angle in degrees. That is all it takes to compute the famous case.

## The most famous equation

The unit circle has circumference $2\pi$. So traveling $\pi$ units around it — half the way — starts at $1$ and finishes at $-1$, directly opposite.

$$
\boxed{\ e^{i\pi} = -1\ }
$$

Often written $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$, which puts five of the most important constants in mathematics into one equation with nothing else in it: $e$, $i$, $\pi$, $1$, and $0$.

Two more, by the same reading:

- Travel $0$ units: you have not moved. $e^{0} = 1$ ✓, agreeing with ordinary exponents.
- Travel $2\pi$ units: all the way round, back to the start. $e^{2\pi i} = 1$.

<Warning title="This is not a coincidence to be admired and forgotten">
It is tempting to file $e^{i\pi} = -1$ under *surprising facts*. It is better understood as a **definition doing its job**.

Module 5.8 named a point by its distance and its direction, and module 5.9 will prove what multiplication does to those two pieces: the distances multiply, and the angles **add**. Exponents also add: $e^{x}e^{y} = e^{x+y}$. Two operations with the same behavior, and Euler's formula is the statement that they are the same operation.

That is why the notation is worth having. Writing a rotation as $e^{i\theta}$ makes *"rotate by $\theta_1$, then by $\theta_2$"* into *"multiply $e^{i\theta_1}$ by $e^{i\theta_2}$"*, which the exponent rule turns into $e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}$ — the angles adding, for free.
</Warning>

## What the proof needs

The general statement gives the across-and-up coordinates of the landing point in terms of how far you traveled — the conversion of module 5.8 with $r = 1$, written as an equation.

Proving it needs two things this course does not assume:

- **A way of naming the coordinates of a point on a circle** from the angle alone. Two such rules exist and are met in a later course.
- **Endless sums** — the fact that $e^x$ and those two rules can each be written as a sum of powers that never stops.

Given those, the proof is short and lovely. Substitute $i\theta$ into the endless sum for $e^x$, and use the four-step cycle $i, -1, -i, 1$ from module 5.3 to sort the terms. The terms with real coefficients assemble into one endless sum and the terms carrying an $i$ assemble into another — and those two sums turn out to be exactly the across and up amounts of the landing point.

**One series splits into two**, and the cycle of powers of $i$ is what does the splitting.

<Aside>
Polar form is normally written $re^{i\theta}$ rather than as a distance and an angle written side by side, and now you can see why.

In that notation the multiplication rule module 5.9 is heading for is just the exponent law:
$$
\big(r_1 e^{i\theta_1}\big)\big(r_2 e^{i\theta_2}\big) = r_1r_2\,e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)},
$$
and De Moivre's formula is $\big(re^{i\theta}\big)^n = r^n e^{in\theta}$ — a rule you already know, applied to a new kind of exponent.

**Good notation makes a theorem look like arithmetic.** The content is identical to what module 5.9 proves; what changes is that the statement takes one line and is impossible to misremember.
</Aside>
