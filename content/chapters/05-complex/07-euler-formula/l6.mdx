Module 5.6 proved that multiplying complex numbers **multiplies their distances** from the origin:

$$|zw| = |z|\,|w|.$$

That is half of what multiplication does to a point. This module settles the other half — the **direction** — with a triangle argument from your geometry course.

## The claim

Write $\angle z$ for the direction of $z$: the angle you turn counterclockwise from the positive real axis to face it. Degrees or fractions of a turn — nothing below depends on which.

> **Theorem.** For non-zero $z$ and $w$, multiplication multiplies the distances and **adds the directions**:
> $$|zw| = |z|\,|w|, \qquad \angle(zw) = \angle z + \angle w.$$

The first half is module 5.6. The second is what the pictures have suggested since module 5.5, proved so far for nothing but quarter turns.

## The proof

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Distances multiply (module 5.6), and two triangles whose three pairs of sides are in the same ratio are similar — side-side-side, from geometry.</div>
  <div slot="want">That multiplying by $w$ turns every point by $\angle w$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Compare two triangles: the one with corners $0$, $1$, $z$ and the one with corners $0$, $w$, $wz$. Show all three sides scale by the same factor.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Take $z$ and $w$ non-zero, with $z \neq 1$ so the first triangle is a genuine one. Compare triangle $0,\,1,\,z$ with triangle $0,\,w,\,wz$ side by side:

| side of the first | side of the second | ratio |
|---|---|---|
| $0$ to $1$, length $1$ | $0$ to $w$, length $\lvert w\rvert$ | $\lvert w\rvert$ |
| $0$ to $z$, length $\lvert z\rvert$ | $0$ to $wz$, length $\lvert wz\rvert = \lvert w\rvert\lvert z\rvert$ | $\lvert w\rvert$ |
| $1$ to $z$, length $\lvert z-1\rvert$ | $w$ to $wz$, length $\lvert wz-w\rvert$ | $\lvert w\rvert$ |

Only the third row needs algebra. Since $wz - w = w(z-1)$, module 5.6 gives $|wz-w| = |w|\,|z-1|$.

So every side of the second triangle is $|w|$ times the matching side of the first, and the two are **similar**.

Similar triangles have equal matching angles. At corner $0$, the first triangle's angle is the turn from $1$ to $z$ — that is $\angle z$, since $1$ points along the positive real axis. The second's is the turn from $w$ to $wz$, which is $\angle(wz) - \angle w$. Setting them equal:
$$\angle(wz) - \angle w = \angle z, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \angle(wz) = \angle w + \angle z. \qquad\blacksquare$$
</Proof>

<Warning title="One gap in that argument, and it is worth seeing">
Similar triangles have equal angles — but an angle in that theorem carries no *direction*. If the second triangle were a mirror image of the first, the turn would come out as $-\angle z$ and the conclusion would be backwards.

One example settles it. Take $w = z = i$, so $wz = -1$: going from $i$ to $-1$ turns counterclockwise, the same way as going from $1$ to $i$. Not reflected.

One example is enough only because multiplying by $w$ cannot flip the plane for some points and not others without tearing it. That is an appeal to something about continuous motion this course has not proved, and the argument does lean on it.
</Warning>

## Reading the theorem

**Multiplying multiplies the distances and adds the turns.** Everything left in this chapter is that sentence in some disguise. Check it where you can already work the answer out:

| product | distances | turns | result |
|---|---|---|---|
| $i \cdot i$ | $1\times 1 = 1$ | quarter $+$ quarter $=$ half | $-1$ ✓ |
| $2i \cdot 2i$ | $2\times2 = 4$ | quarter $+$ quarter $=$ half | $-4$ ✓ |
| $(-1)(-1)$ | $1 \times 1 = 1$ | half $+$ half $=$ full | $1$ ✓ |

And $i^2 = -1$ now reads *two quarter turns make a half turn* — exactly what the Kindergarten version of this chapter says, acted out on a classroom floor.

## Why this deserves exponent notation

Strip any exponential down to its one essential habit:

$$
2^{m}\cdot 2^{n} = 2^{m+n}.
$$

**Adding on the inside, multiplying on the outside.** That is what exponents do.

Now write $C(\theta)$ for the point at distance $1$ in the direction $\theta$, and the theorem reads

$$
C(\alpha)\cdot C(\beta) = C(\alpha+\beta).
$$

The same law twice. $C$ trades addition for multiplication exactly as an exponential does — so $C$ **is** an exponential, not by analogy but by the only test that counts.

That is why later courses write $C(\theta)$ as a power. Which power, of what base, this module leaves alone: raising a number to an *imaginary* exponent needs a tool you have not met. What is settled is that the notation has to be exponent notation, because the law is the exponent law.

<Warning title="What has and has not been proved">
Proved: multiplying multiplies distances and adds turns.

Not proved, and not claimed: that some number raised to an imaginary power equals the point in direction $\theta$. That needs a definition of imaginary exponents, and none has been given.

A great many false proofs are honest ones that quietly used the thing they set out to prove. The habit that catches it is asking, at every line, *which of these am I entitled to?*
</Warning>

## Powers, from the same law

Apply the theorem with $z = w$: squaring doubles the turn and squares the distance. Again: cubing triples the turn. The pattern does not stop, so an $n$th power multiplies the turn by $n$.

**Turning $n$ times through $\theta$ is turning once through $n\theta$** — obvious about turning, not at all obvious about multiplying something out $n$ times.

Module 5.9 makes that a formula and uses it for powers and roots; chapter 6 proves the "does not stop" step by induction, which is exactly what a claim indexed by the whole numbers calls for.

<Aside>
Every point has more than one direction, since a full turn leaves you facing the way you started:

$$C(\theta) = C(\theta + \text{a full turn}) = C(\theta - \text{a full turn}) = \cdots$$

The point is unique; the number naming its direction is not. Module 5.8 takes that seriously — it is a fact about circles rather than a defect in the notation.
</Aside>

## Where this leads

**Growth and turning are the same kind of thing.** On the number line, exponentials grow and nothing turns; in the plane, the law governing $2^m\cdot2^n$ is the law governing rotation, and the direction you travel decides which behavior you see. A later course writes that down as one famous equation.

Getting there means making sense of an imaginary exponent, which needs a way of building $2^x$ out of pieces a complex number can be fed into. Calculus supplies one, and feeding an imaginary quantity in produces exactly the two measurements that locate a point on a circle — sorted out by the cycle $i, -1, -i, 1$ of module 5.3. That was Euler's route, in the 1740s.
