## The cycle

$$
i^0 = 1, \quad i^1 = i, \quad i^2 = -1, \quad i^3 = -i, \quad i^4 = 1, \ldots
$$

Each step multiplies by $i$ one more time. After four steps you are back at $1$, and then the whole list repeats.

**So $i^n$ depends only on the remainder $n$ leaves when divided by $4$.**

## Using it

> **Example.** What is $i^{23}$?
>
> Divide: $23 = 4\cdot 5 + 3$, remainder $3$. Twenty of those steps are five complete laps that change nothing, so $i^{23} = i^3 = -i$.

> **Example.** What is $i^{100}$?
>
> $100 = 4\cdot 25$, remainder $0$. So $i^{100} = i^0 = 1$.

This is module 4.4's division with remainder, applied to an exponent. The four possible remainders — $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ — give exactly the four possible values, and there is never a fifth.

<Warning title="The remainder is the whole answer, but only for whole exponents">
The argument counts complete laps, so it needs $n$ to be a whole number. $i^{1/2}$ is not covered by it, and is a real question with a genuinely surprising answer — one that a later course settles.

Watch this carefully: a proof that works by counting whole laps is entitled to conclusions about whole numbers of laps and nothing else. Noticing exactly how far a proof reaches is a skill this course is training, and it is more valuable than the result itself.
</Warning>

## What the cycle is really saying

Module 5.5 read multiplying by $i$ as a **quarter turn** counterclockwise about the origin.

Read the cycle that way and it stops being arithmetic:

| power | turn from $1$ | lands on |
|---|---|---|
| $i^1$ | quarter turn | $i$ |
| $i^2$ | half turn | $-1$ |
| $i^3$ | three-quarter turn | $-i$ |
| $i^4$ | full turn | $1$ |

**The list repeats after four steps because a full turn brings you back.** That is the entire explanation, and it is a fact about turning rather than a fact about arithmetic — which is the sort of trade this chapter keeps making.

And $i^2 = -1$, the equation the whole chapter started from, is now the statement that **two quarter turns make a half turn**. Nothing else was ever going on.

## What quarter turns cannot yet reach

The cycle handles quarter turns. It says nothing about turning by, say, a third of a turn — for that you would need to name the point you land on, and naming points at any angle you like takes a tool this course does not assume.

That is where this module stops. Module 5.8 picks it up once the tools exist.

## Working with the cycle

The cycle is short enough to use directly, and the exercises below are the ones worth being able to do without thinking.

> **What is $i^{35}$?**
>
> Divide: $35 = 4\cdot 8 + 3$. Eight complete laps change nothing, so $i^{35} = i^3 = -i$.

> **What is $i^{100} + i^{101} + i^{102} + i^{103}$?**
>
> Four consecutive powers always run once around the whole cycle, so they are $1$, $i$, $-1$ and $-i$ in some order — and those four add to $0$. The answer is $0$ without computing a single power.

That second one is worth noticing. **Any four consecutive powers of $i$ add to zero**, because going once around the circle and back to the start means the four steps cancel in pairs: $1$ against $-1$, and $i$ against $-i$.

<Aside>
Here is the destination, purely as something to look at.

There is a way of writing "the point you reach by turning through an angle" as a *power* — the same kind of expression as $2^n$, though with a strange-looking exponent. When the angle is half a turn, that expression works out to $-1$, and moving the $-1$ across gives a line with a considerable reputation:

$$e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0.$$

Three numbers with nothing to do with each other — one from compound interest, one from circles, one from unsolvable equations — held together by a single equation. It is regularly voted the most beautiful formula in mathematics.

None of it is needed here, and the version of this module you would read in twelfth grade is where it gets explained. It is worth seeing early that the chapter has somewhere to go.
</Aside>
