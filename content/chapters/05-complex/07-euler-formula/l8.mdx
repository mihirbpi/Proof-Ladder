$$
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta \qquad (\theta \text{ in radians}).
$$

An exponential on the left, trigonometry on the right, and nothing in either subject suggests the two should ever meet.

## What the left-hand side even means

You know what $e^x$ means for a real exponent. Nothing you have met says what $e$ raised to an **imaginary** power should mean — there is no "multiply $e$ by itself $i\theta$ times".

So the formula is not a fact waiting to be checked. The right-hand side is the definition, and the real question is the one behind it:

> **Why is $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ entitled to be called an exponential at all?**

That question has a sharp answer, and it is the content of this module.

## What makes something an exponential

Strip $e^x$ down to its one essential habit and this is what is left:

$$
e^{x}e^{y} = e^{x+y}.
$$

**An exponential turns adding into multiplying.** Everything else about $e^x$ — its graph, its rate of change, its decimal expansion — is downstream of that one law.

So the claim to test is whether the circle points obey it. Write $C(\theta) = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ for the point at angle $\theta$ on the unit circle, and ask whether $C(\alpha)C(\beta) = C(\alpha+\beta)$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The angle-sum identities from trigonometry, and the fact that two complex numbers are equal exactly when their real parts and imaginary parts agree (§5.2).</div>
  <div slot="want">$C(\alpha)C(\beta) = C(\alpha+\beta)$ — multiplying circle points adds their angles.</div>
  <div slot="how">Multiply the two out, use $i^2 = -1$ to collect the real and imaginary parts, and recognize what appears.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Expand the product:
$$
C(\alpha)C(\beta) = (\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta+i\sin\beta)
$$
$$
= \cos\alpha\cos\beta + i\cos\alpha\sin\beta + i\sin\alpha\cos\beta + i^2\sin\alpha\sin\beta.
$$

The last term carries $i^2 = -1$, so it joins the real part with a minus sign:
$$
= \big(\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta\big) + i\big(\sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta\big).
$$

The two brackets are exactly the angle-sum identities: the first is $\cos(\alpha+\beta)$, the second is $\sin(\alpha+\beta)$. So
$$
C(\alpha)C(\beta) = \cos(\alpha+\beta) + i\sin(\alpha+\beta) = C(\alpha+\beta). \qquad\blacksquare
$$
</Proof>

**That is the whole justification.** $C$ converts addition into multiplication, which is what an exponential does and what nothing else does. Writing it $e^{i\theta}$ is not a coincidence being celebrated; it is a law being recorded.

Notice also where the minus sign in the cosine identity went. It came out of $i^2 = -1$ — the one place a minus sign can come from here. The identity everybody misremembers is bookkeeping for a single fact about $i$.

<Warning title="This is a definition doing work, not a theorem out of nowhere">
Nothing above proves that $e$ — the number $2.718\ldots$ — is somehow secretly involved. What is proved is that the circle points obey the exponential law, and that fact earns them exponential *notation*.

The section below closes the loop: taking the series for $e^x$ as the *definition* of $e^z$ for complex $z$ — which agrees with the old one on the reals — turns $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta+i\sin\theta$ into a theorem rather than a naming decision.

Keeping straight which lines are definitions and which are theorems is not fussiness. It is the difference between a derivation and a circle.
</Warning>

## Reading the formula geometrically

$$|e^{i\theta}| = \sqrt{\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta} = 1$$

by the Pythagorean identity, so $e^{i\theta}$ lies on the unit circle for every real $\theta$, with coordinates $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$.

**$e^{i\theta}$ is the point reached by traveling $\theta$ radians counterclockwise from $1$.** The formula is a dictionary between arc length and a complex number, and the radian requirement is what makes it exact: an arc of $\theta$ radians on the unit circle *has length* $\theta$, with no conversion factor anywhere.

The special values then read as geometry rather than algebra:

$$e^{i\pi/2} = i \quad\text{— a quarter turn}$$
$$e^{i\pi} = -1 \quad\text{— a half turn}$$
$$e^{2\pi i} = 1 \quad\text{— a full turn}$$

And the law just proved becomes a statement about composing rotations: $e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta} = e^{i(\alpha+\beta)}$ says turning by $\alpha$ and then by $\beta$ is turning by $\alpha+\beta$. That is §5.9, and it makes $i^2 = -1$ the statement that two quarter turns make a half turn.

**Trigonometry and exponentials looked unrelated because you met them on the real line**, where one oscillates and the other grows. In the plane they are one function seen along two different directions, and the line was simply too small a stage to see the connection on.

## Consequences

Since $\cos$ is even and $\sin$ is odd, $e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta$, which is the conjugate of $e^{i\theta}$. Adding and subtracting the two:

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}.
$$

These are §5.4's formulas for the real and imaginary parts, applied to $e^{i\theta}$. They turn every trigonometric identity into a statement about products of circle points, which is usually the easier form — §5.9 exploits it.

One more consequence worth having: a full turn changes nothing, so $e^{i(\theta+2\pi)} = e^{i\theta}$. The complex exponential **repeats**, which the real one never does. That single fact is behind everything awkward in §5.8.

<Aside>
$e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$ is Euler's identity, regularly voted the most beautiful formula in mathematics — five constants from five different corners of the subject, in one equation with no room to spare.

The tidiness reputation is deserved, but the mathematical content is the repetition: $e^{i\pi} = -1$ says half a turn is a reflection through the origin, and $e^{2\pi i} = 1$ says a full turn is doing nothing. The identity is the statement that the exponential wraps the line around the circle.
</Aside>

## The proof from the series

Calculus supplies three expansions, each valid for every real $x$:

$$
e^{x} = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{x^n}{n!}, \qquad
\cos x = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n}}{(2n)!}, \qquad
\sin x = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n x^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\sum_{n\ge0}$ adds a term for each whole number $n$ from $0$ upward, and $n!$ is the product of the whole numbers up to $n$. So the line reads: each of the three is an endless sum of powers, with a fixed rule for the coefficients.*

Substitute $i\theta$ for $x$ in the first. Each term carries a power of $i$, and §5.3's four-step cycle collapses every one of them to $1$, $i$, $-1$ or $-i$:

$$
e^{i\theta} = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(i\theta)^n}{n!} = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{i^n\theta^n}{n!}.
$$

Now sort by whether $n$ is even or odd. For even $n = 2k$, $i^{n} = (i^2)^k = (-1)^k$, so those terms carry no $i$ at all. For odd $n = 2k+1$, $i^{n} = (-1)^k i$, so each carries exactly one $i$. Collecting the two families:

$$
e^{i\theta} = \underbrace{\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k}}{(2k)!}}_{\cos\theta} \;+\; i\underbrace{\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k\theta^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}}_{\sin\theta},
$$

*So the line reads: the terms with no $i$ assemble into exactly the cosine series, and the terms carrying one $i$ assemble into the sine series — giving $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$.*

**The cycle is what does the sorting.** Without $i^4 = 1$ the powers would not fall into two families, and there would be nothing to collect.

<Warning title="The rearrangement needs justifying">
Splitting one endless sum into two and collecting terms out of order is not free. There are sums whose terms can be reordered to give any answer you like, so a step like this needs a licence.

The licence is **absolute convergence**: when the sum of the sizes of the terms converges, any reordering gives the same total. Here the sizes are $|\theta|^n/n!$, whose sum is $e^{|\theta|}$ — finite for every $\theta$. So the rearrangement is legitimate, and noticing that it needed checking at all is the habit worth taking from this.
</Warning>

## A second proof, with no series at all

The derivation above rearranges an endless sum, and §5.7 flagged that rearranging infinitely many terms needs justification. Here is an argument avoiding the issue entirely, using only the derivative rules of a first calculus course.

Fix $\theta$ and consider

$$g(\theta) = \frac{\cos\theta + i\sin\theta}{e^{i\theta}}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $g$ is a rule taking a real number $\theta$ to a complex number. So the line reads: compare the point at angle $\theta$ on the circle against the exponential, by dividing one by the other.*

Differentiate the top and bottom by the usual rules, treating $i$ as the constant it is. The top has derivative $-\sin\theta + i\cos\theta$, which is $i$ times the top itself; the bottom has derivative $i e^{i\theta}$, which is $i$ times the bottom. Feed both into the quotient rule and the numerator cancels to zero.

So $g' = 0$ everywhere, meaning $g$ is constant. Evaluate at $\theta = 0$: the top is $1$, the bottom is $1$, so the constant is $1$. Therefore the two agree for every $\theta$, which is the formula.

**What this costs and what it buys.** It assumes the derivative rules apply to a complex-valued rule of a real variable — true, and provable by treating the two real parts separately. In exchange it never rearranges an infinite sum, and it explains *why* the two sides agree: both change at exactly $i$ times their current value, and two quantities that grow at the same proportional rate from the same start stay equal.

<WhereThisGoes level="l8">

Euler's formula is the identity that unifies the families of functions you have studied separately.

**It explains why one technique handles both growth and oscillation.** Exponentials grow; sines and cosines wobble. They look like different animals, and every method you learn for one seems to need a rebuild for the other. Euler's formula says they are the same animal seen from two angles — and once you know that, a single method covers both, with the real direction giving growth and the imaginary one giving the wobble.

That is not a curiosity. Problems about springs, circuits and populations turn out to reduce to the same handful of equations, and the reason one technique solves all of them is the identity in this module.

**Both proofs above are worth keeping.** The series route is how Euler came at it in the 1740s, and it shows *why* the two trigonometric functions appear at all — they are what the even and odd halves of one exponential sum happen to be. The derivative route shows something the series hides: both sides change at $i$ times their current value, and that alone forces them to agree.

</WhereThisGoes>
