## The cycle of powers of $i$

*A reminder from module 5.3: a dash in front means the backwards side of zero, so $-1$ is one step from $0$ going the other way. And $i^2$ is short for $i \times i$.*

Start with $i$ and keep multiplying:

$$
i^1 = i, \qquad
i^2 = -1, \qquad
i^3 = i^2 \times i = -i, \qquad
i^4 = i^3 \times i = -i \times i = 1.
$$

And then it starts over: $i^5 = i$, $i^6 = -1$, and so on.

So the powers of $i$ take only **four** values, cycling forever. To find $i^{23}$, divide $23$ by $4$: it goes $5$ times with remainder $3$. The five full loops change nothing, so $i^{23} = i^3 = -i$.

(That is module 4.4's remainder idea, used on turns instead of counters.)

## Why the cycle matters

There is a famous formula that ties the powers of $i$ to the position of a point on a circle. It is called **Euler's formula**, and it is often called the most beautiful equation in mathematics.

Writing it down properly needs two pieces of notation you have not met, so it is not written out here. What it *says* can be said in words, and the saying is the interesting part.

### Where the formula comes from

There is a way of writing $e^x$ as an endless sum of powers of $x$. Put $i\theta$ in place of $x$ and you get an endless sum of powers of $i\theta$. Each power of $i$ is one of the four cycle values.

- The terms where $i$ came out as $+1$ or $-1$ have no $i$ left. They are the **real** terms.
- The terms where $i$ came out as $+i$ or $-i$ each carry one $i$. They are the **imaginary** terms.

Sort them into those two piles. Each pile turns out to be an endless sum that measures something about a point on a circle: the first pile gives how far **across** the point is, and the second gives how far **up**.

**The cycle is what does the sorting.** Without it, the terms would not separate into two clean halves, and the formula would not exist.

## What the formula says

In a picture: the formula describes the point you reach by walking around a circle of radius $1$, starting from $1$ and going counterclockwise. Feed in how far around you walked, and it gives back the across and up amounts of where you stopped.

<Figure alt="A dashed unit circle centerd at the origin with a point marked on it at the top." caption="Walking around the circle of radius 1: a quarter of the way lands on i.">
  <ComplexPlane circle={1} points={[{re: 0, im: 1, label: "quarter way"}]} range={2} />
</Figure>

Check it against the cycle. A quarter of the way around, the point is $0$ across and $1$ up — which is $i$. ✓

Half way around, it is $1$ to the left and nothing up — which is $-1$. ✓

All the way round is $1$ again. The four-step cycle is Euler's formula, checked at four points.

Euler's formula needs infinite sums and the calculus that handles them safely. It is true, it has several complete proofs, and none of them can be given honestly at this stage. Take it as a promissory note rather than as something to accept on authority — the proof exists, and you will be able to read it.

<Aside>
Put $\theta$ = half a turn into the formula. You get $e^{i\pi} = -1$, usually written
$$e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0.$$
Five of the most important constants in mathematics — $e$, $i$, $\pi$, $1$, and $0$ — in one short line, and none of them was invented with the others in mind.
</Aside>
