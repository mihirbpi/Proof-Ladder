## The cycle of powers of $i$

*A reminder from module 5.3: a dash in front means the backwards side of zero, so $-1$ is one step from $0$ going the other way.*

Start with $i$ and keep multiplying:

$$
i = i, \qquad
i \times i = -1, \qquad
i \times i \times i = -i, \qquad
i \times i \times i \times i = 1.
$$

And then it starts over: a fifth $i$ brings you back to $i$, a sixth to $-1$, and so on forever.

<Figure alt="Four points marked at the compass positions one, i, minus one, and minus i around the origin." caption="Multiplying by $i$ again and again walks these four points in order — $1$, $i$, $-1$, $-i$ — and comes back to the start.">

    {re: 1, im: 0, label: "1"},
    {re: 0, im: 1, label: "i"},
    {re: -1, im: 0, label: "-1"},
    {re: 0, im: -1, label: "-i"}
  ]} range={2} />
</Figure>

So multiplying $i$ by itself, however many times, lands on only **four** possible answers, cycling forever. To find where $23$ $i$s land, divide $23$ by $4$: it goes $5$ times with remainder $3$. The five full loops change nothing, so the answer is wherever $3$ $i$s land, which is $-i$.

(That is module 4.4's remainder idea, used on turns instead of counters.)

## Why the cycle matters

There is a famous formula that ties the powers of $i$ to the position of a point on a circle. It is called **Euler's formula**, and it is often called the most beautiful equation in mathematics.

Writing it down properly needs two pieces of notation you have not met, so it is not written out here. What it *says* can be said in words, and the saying is the interesting part.

### Where the formula comes from

Getting to it needs a tool from a much later course, and this page will not pretend to hand you one. But the part the cycle does can be described.

That later tool turns the two sides of the formula into two very long strings of additions — one term, then another, then another, going on forever. Every one of those terms carries some number of $i$s, and by the cycle each of them collapses to one of just four things: $1$, $i$, $-1$, $-i$.

So every term lands in one of two piles: the ones with no $i$ left, and the ones carrying a single $i$. The first pile turns out to measure how far **across** the point is, and the second how far **up**.

**The cycle is what does the sorting.** Without it the terms would not fall into two clean piles, and the formula would not exist.

## What the formula says

In a picture: the formula describes the point you reach by walking around a circle of radius $1$, starting from $1$ and going counterclockwise. Feed in how far around you walked, and it gives back the across and up amounts of where you stopped.

<Figure alt="A dashed unit circle centerd at the origin with a point marked on it at the top." caption="Walking around the circle of radius 1: a quarter of the way lands on i.">
  <ComplexPlane circle={1} points={[{re: 0, im: 1, label: "quarter way"}]} range={2} />
</Figure>

Check it against the cycle. A quarter of the way around, the point is $0$ across and $1$ up — which is $i$. ✓

Half way around, it is $1$ to the left and nothing up — which is $-1$. ✓

All the way round is $1$ again. The four-step cycle is Euler's formula, checked at four points.

<Warning title="This one is stated, not proved">
Everything else in this course has been proved from things you already had. This is the first genuine exception, and it is worth flagging.

Euler's formula needs infinite sums and the calculus that handles them safely. It is true, it has several complete proofs, and none of them can be given honestly at this stage. Take it as a promise note rather than as something to accept on authority — the proof exists, and you will be able to read it.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Feed half a turn into the formula and it says: walking halfway around the circle from $1$ lands you exactly on $-1$. You already knew that from the picture — but the formula gets there from the other side, out of the cycle and an endless string of additions, with no circle drawn anywhere.

Written out in the notation of a later course, that single sentence turns into a one-line equation tying together five of the most important numbers in mathematics, none of which was invented with the others in mind. It is regularly voted the most beautiful equation there is, and what it says is the sentence above.
</Aside>
