Module 5.6 ended on a complaint: the modulus tells you how far $z$ is from the origin and nothing about which direction. This module supplies the missing half.

## Two numbers, a different pair

So far a complex number has been located by **across and up** — the pair $(a, b)$ of module 5.5.

There is a second way to reach the same point. Give its **distance from the origin** and its **direction**.

$$
z \ \leftrightarrow\ (\underbrace{r}_{\text{how far}},\ \underbrace{\theta}_{\text{which way}})
$$

This is **polar form**. The distance $r$ is the modulus $|z|$ from module 5.6, and $\theta$ — the angle measured counterclockwise from the positive real axis — is called the **argument** of $z$.

Reading off that picture, with angles in degrees:

| $z$ | $r$ | $\theta$ |
|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $1$ | $0°$ |
| $i$ | $1$ | $90°$ |
| $-1$ | $1$ | $180°$ |
| $-i$ | $1$ | $270°$ |
| $2i$ | $2$ | $90°$ |

The last two rows make the point of the pair: $i$ and $2i$ share a direction and differ in distance, while $i$ and $-i$ share a distance and differ in direction. **Neither number alone locates the point.**

## The angle is not quite unique

<Warning title="Adding a full turn changes nothing">
$90°$ and $450°$ describe the same direction, because $450° = 90° + 360°$ — one extra lap around.

So the argument of a complex number is only determined **up to whole turns.** Every nonzero $z$ has infinitely many valid arguments, differing by multiples of $360°$.

Books usually pick one, most often the value in $[0°, 360°)$ or in $(-180°, 180°]$, and call it *the* argument. That is a choice made for tidiness, and different books choose differently.

And $z = 0$ has **no** argument at all: it sits at the origin, so no direction points to it. Its modulus is $0$, which is all there is to say.
</Warning>

## Converting between the two forms

Going one way is the Pythagorean theorem, which you have: $r = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.

Going the other way — recovering $a$ and $b$ from $r$ and $\theta$ — needs a tool this course does not assume: a pair of rules that turn an angle into the across and up amounts of a point on a circle of size $1$.

Those rules exist and are met in a later course. Until then, the picture does the job: drop a line from the point to the horizontal axis and you have a right triangle whose slanted side is $r$, whose short sides are $a$ and $b$, and whose corner sits at the angle $\theta$.

**You are not expected to convert in that direction yet.** Every example below uses angles you can read straight off a drawing — quarter turns, half turns, and even divisions of a circle.

<Aside>
Why bother with a second coordinate system at all? Because the two are good at different things.

Addition is easy in **across-and-up** form: add the parts separately (module 5.3), and the picture is arrows joining nose to tail.

Multiplication is a mess in that form — the formula $(ac-bd) + (ad+bc)i$ has four products and a stray minus sign, and nothing about it is memorable.

In polar form multiplication becomes simple, and module 5.9 shows how. **Choosing the right coordinates can turn a hard operation into an easy one**, and that is a general lesson worth more than either formula.
</Aside>
