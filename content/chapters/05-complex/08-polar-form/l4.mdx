## Radians

Angles in this chapter are measured in **radians**: the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc of length equal to the radius. A full turn is $2\pi$ radians.

| Turn | Degrees | Radians |
|---|---|---|
| full | $360°$ | $2\pi$ |
| half | $180°$ | $\pi$ |
| quarter | $90°$ | $\pi/2$ |
| eighth | $45°$ | $\pi/4$ |

Radians are not a convention adopted for elegance: Euler's formula, the derivative $\tfrac{d}{d\theta}\sin\theta = \cos\theta$, and the series for $\sin$ and $\cos$ are all *false* in degrees. Degrees are the arbitrary unit; radians are the natural one.

## Polar form

Every $z \neq 0$ can be written
$$
z = re^{i\theta}, \qquad r = |z| > 0,
$$
where $\theta$ is an **argument** of $z$ — the counterclockwise angle from the positive real axis.

By Euler's formula (module 5.7),
$$
re^{i\theta} = r\cos\theta + i\,r\sin\theta,
$$
so the conversions are
$$
a = r\cos\theta, \quad b = r\sin\theta; \qquad r = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}, \quad \tan\theta = \frac{b}{a}.
$$

<Warning title="tan θ = b/a does not determine θ">
$\tan$ has period $\pi$, so it cannot distinguish $z$ from $-z$. For $1+i$ and $-1-i$, both give $\tan\theta = 1$, yet the arguments are $\pi/4$ and $5\pi/4$.

Always check which **quadrant** $z$ is in before accepting the output of $\arctan$. Calculators and programming languages provide `atan2(b, a)` for exactly this reason.

Also: $z = 0$ has modulus $0$ and **no** argument. Every angle points at it, so none is distinguished. The origin is a genuine exception to polar form.
</Warning>

## Non-uniqueness

$$
re^{i\theta} = re^{i(\theta + 2\pi k)} \quad\text{for every } k \in \mathbb{Z},
$$
since $e^{2\pi i k} = 1$. So the argument is determined only **up to whole turns**, and $\arg(z)$ denotes a set of values rather than a single number.

The **principal argument** $\operatorname{Arg}(z) \in (-\pi, \pi]$ is the standard choice, and choosing it is what makes $\arg$ a function (module 3.1) — but the choice creates a discontinuity along the negative real axis, where the argument jumps from near $\pi$ to near $-\pi$. That jump is not an artefact of a bad choice; no continuous choice exists on all of $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$.

Compare with Cartesian form $a + bi$, which is unique. The two representations trade uniqueness for convenience under multiplication.

## Worked conversions

**$1 + i$ to polar.** $r = \sqrt{1+1} = \sqrt2$. The point is in the first quadrant with equal coordinates, so $\theta = \pi/4$. Hence $1+i = \sqrt2 \,e^{i\pi/4}$.

**$3e^{i\pi/3}$ to Cartesian.** $\cos(\pi/3) = \tfrac12$ and $\sin(\pi/3) = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, so
$$
3e^{i\pi/3} = 3\left(\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i\right) = \tfrac32 + \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}i.
$$

**$-2$ to polar.** $r = 2$, and $-2$ points along the negative real axis, so $\theta = \pi$. Hence $-2 = 2e^{i\pi}$.

## Special values

$$
1 = e^{0}, \qquad i = e^{i\pi/2}, \qquad -1 = e^{i\pi}, \qquad -i = e^{i3\pi/2} = e^{-i\pi/2}.
$$

The second of these, rearranged, is **Euler's identity**:
$$
e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0.
$$

Five constants — $e$, $i$, $\pi$, $1$, $0$ — and three operations, in one line. Its content is that half a turn is multiplication by $-1$; its fame is that $e$ (from growth), $\pi$ (from circles), and $i$ (from algebra) had no business meeting.

## Why polar form exists

Cartesian form makes addition trivial and multiplication messy. Polar form does the reverse:
$$
r_1e^{i\theta_1} \cdot r_2e^{i\theta_2} = r_1r_2\,e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}.
$$
Multiply the moduli, add the arguments. Module 5.9 proves this and extracts the consequences, which include most of trigonometry.

<Aside>
Because arguments only add up to whole turns, the complex logarithm $\log z = \ln|z| + i\arg z$ is multivalued — it has infinitely many values differing by $2\pi i$. Choosing one requires a *branch cut*, a curve from $0$ to $\infty$ that the function is not allowed to cross. This is not a defect of notation: it is the reason $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a\sqrt b$ fails on $\mathbb{C}$ (module 5.3), and the honest resolution is Riemann surfaces.
</Aside>
