Every $z \in \mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ can be written
$$
z = re^{i\theta}, \qquad r = |z| > 0, \quad \theta \in \mathbb{R},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in".*

and by Euler's formula $re^{i\theta} = r\cos\theta + ir\sin\theta$, so $a = r\cos\theta$, $b = r\sin\theta$.

Angles are in **radians** throughout, necessarily: the series for $\sin$ and $\cos$, hence Euler's formula, hence $\sin' = \cos$, all fail in degrees.

## Existence and non-uniqueness

> **Proposition.** For $z \neq 0$ there exists $\theta \in \mathbb{R}$ with $z = |z|e^{i\theta}$, and $\theta$ is unique modulo $2\pi$.

<Proof>
*Existence.* Put $w = z/|z|$, so $|w| = 1$ and $w = u + iv$ with $u^2+v^2 = 1$. The map $\theta \mapsto (\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ is a surjection from $[0,2\pi)$ onto the unit circle, so some $\theta$ has $\cos\theta = u$, $\sin\theta = v$; then $w = e^{i\theta}$ and $z = |z|e^{i\theta}$.

*Uniqueness mod $2\pi$.* If $e^{i\theta_1} = e^{i\theta_2}$ then $e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)} = 1$, so $\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) = 1$ and $\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) = 0$, which forces $\theta_1 - \theta_2 \in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}$.
</Proof>

Structurally: $\theta \mapsto e^{i\theta}$ is a surjective operation-preserving map $(\mathbb{R},+)\to(S^1,\cdot)$ with kernel $2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, so
$$
\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z} \cong S^1, \qquad \mathbb{C}^\times \cong \mathbb{R}_{>0}\times S^1.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\mathbb{C}$ is the complex numbers.*

Polar form is that a renaming that preserves all the structure written out. The non-uniqueness of $\theta$ is not sloppiness — it is the kernel.

## Principal value and the impossibility of a global choice

$\arg(z)$ is properly a *set*, $\{\theta + 2\pi k : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$. The **principal argument** is $\operatorname{Arg}(z) \in (-\pi,\pi]$.

> **Proposition.** There is no continuous function $\alpha : \mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\} \to \mathbb{R}$ with $e^{i\alpha(z)} = z/|z|$ for all $z$.

<Proof title="Proof sketch">
Suppose such $\alpha$ existed. Restrict to the unit circle and traverse it once: $\gamma(t) = e^{it}$, $t \in [0,2\pi]$. Then $\alpha(\gamma(t)) - t$ is continuous and takes values in $2\pi\mathbb{Z}$, hence is constant. So $\alpha(\gamma(2\pi)) - 2\pi = \alpha(\gamma(0))$. But $\gamma(2\pi) = \gamma(0)$, so $\alpha$ takes two different values at the same point — a contradiction.
</Proof>

So the discontinuity of $\operatorname{Arg}$ across the negative real axis is unavoidable, not an artefact of a poor choice. The obstruction is geometric: $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ has a hole at the origin that a loop can encircle, and $\pi_1(\mathbb{C}^\times) \cong \mathbb{Z}$ counts exactly the winding that defeats any continuous choice.

<Warning title="Practical hazards">
**$\arctan(b/a)$ is not $\operatorname{Arg}(z)$.** $\tan$ has period $\pi$, so it cannot separate $z$ from $-z$; the quadrant must be checked. Use `atan2(b, a)`.

**$z = 0$ has no argument.** Polar form covers $\mathbb{C}^\times$, not $\mathbb{C}$.

**$\operatorname{Arg}(zw) \neq \operatorname{Arg}(z)+\operatorname{Arg}(w)$ in general.** The identity holds modulo $2\pi$; the principal values may differ by $2\pi$. Take $z = w = -1$: $\operatorname{Arg}(zw) = 0$ while the sum of the principal arguments is $2\pi$.
</Warning>

## The complex logarithm

$$
\log z = \ln|z| + i\arg z,
$$

*In words: the angle is fixed only up to whole turns, so this rule returns many answers and one must be chosen as official.*
multivalued, with values differing by $2\pi i k$. The **principal branch** $\operatorname{Log} z = \ln|z| + i\operatorname{Arg}(z)$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb{C}\setminus(-\infty,0]$, the removed ray being the **branch cut**.

This is the precise source of the trap in §5.3: $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a\sqrt b$ fails on $\mathbb{C}$ because $\sqrt z = e^{\frac12\log z}$ inherits the branch ambiguity, and the two sides may pick different branches. Likewise $\log(zw) = \log z + \log w$ holds only as an identity of sets.

The honest resolution is not a cleverer branch but a different domain: on the Riemann surface of $\log$ — an infinite spiral covering $\mathbb{C}^\times$ — the logarithm is single-valued and holomorphic everywhere.

## Converting, and the quadrant trap

$$r = |z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac ba.$$

The first is the distance formula. The second follows from dividing $b = r\sin\theta$ by $a = r\cos\theta$.

<Warning title="The tangent does not determine the argument">
$\tan\theta = b/a$ has two solutions per full turn, half a revolution apart, and $\arctan$ returns only the one in $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$.

For $z = -1-i$: $b/a = 1$ and $\arctan 1 = \pi/4$ — first quadrant. But $z$ is in the **third**, so $\operatorname{Arg} z = \pi/4 - \pi = -3\pi/4$.

The correction is $\pm\pi$ whenever $a < 0$, and it is required, not optional. Programming languages provide a two-argument `atan2(b, a)` precisely to handle it, and its existence is an admission that the one-argument version gets this wrong half the time.

The underlying cause is §3.5: $\tan$ is not injective, and $\arctan$ exists only because the domain was restricted — which threw away the other solution.
</Warning>

> **Example.** Convert $-1+i\sqrt3$ to polar form.
>
> $r = \sqrt{1+3} = 2$. And $b/a = -\sqrt3$, so $\arctan$ gives $-\pi/3$ — fourth quadrant. The point is in the second, so add $\pi$:
> $$\operatorname{Arg} z = -\tfrac\pi3 + \pi = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}, \qquad z = 2e^{2\pi i/3}.$$
>
> Check: $2\cos(2\pi/3) = -1$ ✓ and $2\sin(2\pi/3) = \sqrt3$ ✓.

## Special values

$$
1 = e^{0},\quad i = e^{i\pi/2},\quad -1 = e^{i\pi},\quad -i = e^{-i\pi/2},\quad 1+i = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}.
$$

$$
e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0.
$$

<Aside>
$i^i$ is real. Using $i = e^{i(\pi/2 + 2\pi k)}$,
$$i^i = e^{i\log i} = e^{i \cdot i(\pi/2 + 2\pi k)} = e^{-\pi/2 - 2\pi k},$$
a real number for each $k$ — with principal value $e^{-\pi/2}\approx 0.2079$. Euler found this in 1746 and thought it worth writing to a friend about. By Gelfond–Schneider, every value is transcendental (§4.7).
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Polar form is the representation that makes multiplication easy, and choosing a representation to suit the operation is a habit both courses reward.

**The non-uniqueness of the argument is the same phenomenon as the restricted range of $\arctan$.** $\operatorname{Arg}$ is made single-valued by confining it to $(-\pi,\pi]$, which introduces a jump across the negative real axis; $\arctan$ is made single-valued by restricting $\tan$ to $(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$, which is why $\arctan(y/x)$ gives the wrong quadrant half the time.

Both are the §3.9 repair — shrink the domain to manufacture a bijection — and both leave the same residue: a formula that is correct only after a quadrant check.

**In calculus, polar coordinates are the same idea for the plane.** $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ is exactly the polar-to-rectangular conversion here, and integrating in polar coordinates carries a Jacobian factor of $r$ that comes from the same geometry. Curves that are messy in $x$ and $y$ — circles, cardioids, lemniscates — become short in $r$ and $\theta$, and the reason is the reason of this module: the representation should match the symmetry of the problem.

The multivaluedness returns there too. A point has polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ and $(r, \theta+2\pi)$, which is why polar curves can trace the same points more than once and why the limits of integration in a polar area computation require care.

</WhereThisGoes>
