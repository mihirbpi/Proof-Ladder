There are two ways to say where a point is: **across and up**, or **how far and which way**.

$$a+bi \qquad\text{versus}\qquad re^{i\theta}$$

Both name the same point. Every complex number except $0$ can be written the second way, with $r = |z| > 0$ the distance from the origin and $\theta$ a real angle in radians, and by §5.7 the second unpacks as

$$re^{i\theta} = r\cos\theta + ir\sin\theta, \qquad\text{so}\qquad a = r\cos\theta,\quad b = r\sin\theta.$$

$r$ is the modulus. $\theta$ is the **argument**, written $\arg z$.

Angles are in **radians** throughout, and not as a matter of taste: an arc of $\theta$ radians on the unit circle has length $\theta$ exactly, which is what makes every formula below free of conversion factors.

## Every non-zero point has an angle

> **Proposition.** For $z \neq 0$ there is a real $\theta$ with $z = |z|e^{i\theta}$, and any two such angles differ by a whole number of full turns.

<Proof>
*There is one.* Put $w = z/|z|$. Then $|w| = 1$, so writing $w = u+iv$ gives $u^2+v^2 = 1$ — the point $(u,v)$ is on the unit circle. Going once round the circle, $\theta \mapsto (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ passes through every point of it, so some $\theta$ in $[0,2\pi)$ has $\cos\theta = u$ and $\sin\theta = v$. For that $\theta$, $w = e^{i\theta}$ and so $z = |z|e^{i\theta}$.

*Any two differ by full turns.* Suppose $e^{i\theta_1} = e^{i\theta_2}$. Dividing, $e^{i(\theta_1-\theta_2)} = 1$, so $\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) = 1$ and $\sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) = 0$. The only angles with cosine $1$ are the whole multiples of $2\pi$, so $\theta_1 - \theta_2$ is a whole number of full turns. $\blacksquare$
</Proof>

Read the second half again, because it is the point of the module. **The angle is not quite determined by the point.** Turning all the way around returns you to where you started, so

$$
re^{i\theta} = re^{i(\theta+2\pi k)} \quad\text{for every integer } k,
$$

and $1$ has angle $0$, and $2\pi$, and $-2\pi$, and infinitely many others.

So $\arg$ is not a function in the sense of §3.1: one input, many outputs. This is not sloppiness in the notation. It is a fact about circles, and no cleverer notation removes it.

## Picking one angle, and what it costs

The usual repair is to name one of them official: take the angle in $(-\pi, \pi]$ — half a turn each way from the positive real axis — and call that *the* argument.

That makes $\arg$ a genuine function, and it costs exactly one thing: a jump. Points just above the negative real axis have angles near $\pi$; points just below have angles near $-\pi$. Cross that ray and the answer leaps by a full turn, even though the points barely moved.

**The jump cannot be designed away.** Put it somewhere else — measure from $[0,2\pi)$ instead — and the jump moves to the positive real axis; it does not vanish. Any single choice has to break somewhere, because walking once around the origin brings you back to the same point having gained a full turn, and no single-valued angle can both track the walk and return to its starting value.

This is the same repair, with the same side effect, as restricting sine's domain to define $\sin^{-1}$ (§3.9): shrink things until the answer is unique, and accept the seam that leaves.

<Warning title="Three practical hazards">
**$\tan^{-1}(b/a)$ is not the argument.** Tangent repeats every half turn, so it cannot tell $z$ from $-z$; the quadrant has to be checked separately. Programming languages provide a two-argument `atan2(b, a)` exactly because of this, and its existence is an admission that the one-argument version is wrong half the time.

**$z = 0$ has no argument.** Polar form covers every complex number *except* zero. There is no direction to a point sitting at the origin.

**Angles add only up to full turns.** Multiplying $-1$ by $-1$ gives $1$, whose official angle is $0$ — but the two official angles being added are $\pi$ and $\pi$, totalling $2\pi$. The two answers describe the same point and differ by a full turn, which is all the rule ever promised.
</Warning>

## Converting, and the quadrant trap

$$r = |z| = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac ba.$$

The first is the distance formula. The second follows from dividing $b = r\sin\theta$ by $a = r\cos\theta$.

<Warning title="The tangent does not determine the argument">
$\tan\theta = b/a$ has two solutions in a full turn, half a revolution apart, and $\tan^{-1}$ returns only the one between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$.

For $z = -1-i$: $b/a = 1$ and $\tan^{-1}(1) = \pi/4$ — first quadrant. But $z$ is in the **third**, so the argument is $\pi/4 - \pi = -3\pi/4$.

The correction is $\pm\pi$ whenever $a < 0$, and it is required, not optional.

The underlying cause is §3.5: $\tan$ is not injective, and $\tan^{-1}$ exists only because the domain was cut down — which threw the other solution away.
</Warning>

> **Example.** Convert $-1+i\sqrt3$ to polar form.
>
> $r = \sqrt{1+3} = 2$. And $b/a = -\sqrt3$, so $\tan^{-1}$ gives $-\pi/3$ — fourth quadrant. The point is in the second, so add $\pi$:
> $$\theta = -\tfrac\pi3 + \pi = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}, \qquad z = 2e^{2\pi i/3}.$$
>
> Check: $2\cos(2\pi/3) = -1$ ✓ and $2\sin(2\pi/3) = \sqrt3$ ✓.

## Which form for which job

| Task | Better form |
|---|---|
| add, subtract | rectangular |
| multiply, divide | polar |
| raise to a power | polar |
| take roots | polar |
| read off real and imaginary parts | rectangular |
| read off distance and direction | polar |

Adding in polar form is miserable: there is no usable formula relating $\arg(z+w)$ to $\arg z$ and $\arg w$. Multiplying in rectangular form works, and is fiddly.

Choosing the representation to suit the operation is a habit worth building — the same instinct that puts a figure on the axes for one geometry problem and leaves it off for another.

## Special values

$$
1 = e^{0},\quad i = e^{i\pi/2},\quad -1 = e^{i\pi},\quad -i = e^{-i\pi/2},\quad 1+i = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}.
$$

$$
e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0.
$$

<Aside>
The first four are quarter turns, evenly spaced round the unit circle, and multiplying by $i$ steps once around — §5.5's rotation, now read off the angle.

More generally, the solutions of $z^n = 1$ are the $n$ points $e^{2\pi ik/n}$ for $k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$: the vertices of a **regular $n$-gon** inscribed in the unit circle, one vertex sitting at $1$. So the regular polygons of your geometry course are the solution sets of the simplest equations there are.

Gauss proved at nineteen that a regular $17$-gon can be built with compass and straightedge, by working out which of these vertices can be reached using square roots alone — a result that ties construction, algebra and number theory together in one theorem, and which he asked to have carved on his gravestone.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Polar form is the representation that makes multiplication easy, and choosing a representation to suit the operation is a habit both of your remaining courses reward.

**The non-uniqueness of the angle is the same phenomenon as the restricted range of $\tan^{-1}$.** The argument is made single-valued by confining it to half a turn each way, which introduces a jump across the negative real axis; $\tan^{-1}$ is made single-valued by cutting tangent's domain down, which is why $\tan^{-1}(y/x)$ gives the wrong quadrant half the time. Both are the §3.9 repair, and both leave the same residue: a formula that is correct only after a quadrant check.

**In calculus, polar coordinates are this same idea for the plane.** $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ is exactly the conversion above. Curves that are ugly in $x$ and $y$ — circles, spirals, the cardioid — become short in $r$ and $\theta$, for the reason this module is about: the description should match the symmetry of the thing described.

The many-angles problem follows you there. A point has coordinates $(r,\theta)$ and $(r,\theta+2\pi)$, which is why a polar curve can trace the same points more than once, and why the endpoints in a polar area calculation need checking rather than guessing.

</WhereThisGoes>
