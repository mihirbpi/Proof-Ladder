So far you have named complex numbers by *across* and *up*. There is a second way, and for some jobs it is much better.

## Distance and angle

Every point in the plane is fixed by two facts:

- how far it is from the origin — that is the absolute value $|z|$, from module 5.6;
- what angle you turn through to face it, measuring counterclockwise from the positive real axis.

Call the distance $r$ and the angle $\theta$. Then $z$ is written
$$
z = (r, \theta),
$$
read **distance $r$, turn $\theta$** — and this is called **polar form**. It is a pair, like the across-and-up pair you plot points with, but the two slots mean different things: the first says how far out, the second says how far round. Later courses write the same pair a shorter way, using the notation module 5.7 pointed at.

## Reading off the easy ones

Using degrees for now:

| Number | $r$ | $\theta$ |
|---|---|---|
| $1$ | $1$ | $0°$ |
| $i$ | $1$ | $90°$ |
| $-1$ | $1$ | $180°$ |
| $-i$ | $1$ | $270°$ |
| $3$ | $3$ | $0°$ |
| $-2$ | $2$ | $180°$ |

Notice the top four all have $r = 1$. They are the four compass points on the unit circle, and only the angle distinguishes them.

## Converting

**From polar to grid.** Draw the point. Drop a line straight down to the horizontal axis and you have a right triangle: the slanted side is $r$, and the two short sides are the across part and the up part.

So the two pieces are the short sides of a right triangle whose slanted side has length $r$ and whose corner sits at the angle you turned. Working them out from $r$ and the angle needs a tool that comes later; reading them off a careful drawing does not.

**From grid to polar.** Given the across part and the turn part, the distance comes from the right-triangle rule of module 5.6, and the angle is whatever turn points you at that spot.

Example: $3 + 4i$ has $r = 5$, since $3$, $4$ and $5$ make a right triangle. Its angle is whatever turn points you at that spot — a little over half a right angle, as the picture shows.

## The same point, many names

Here is what makes polar form different from grid form.

$$
(r, \theta) = (r, \theta + 360°) = (r, \theta + 720°) = \cdots
$$

Turning an extra full circle leaves you facing the same way. So $i$ can be described as $90°$, or $450°$, or $-270°$ — all correct.

Grid form has no such freedom: $3+4i$ is $3+4i$, full stop.

<Warning title="Non-uniqueness is not sloppiness">
It is tempting to feel that many names for one point is a defect. It is not — it is a fact about turning, and every honest description of angles has it.

What it does mean is that if you need *one* angle, you have to say which range you are picking from (usually $0°$ up to but not including $360°$). Whichever range you pick, say so out loud — do not leave the reader to guess which of the many correct angles you meant.
</Warning>

## Special values

$$
i = (1, 90°), \qquad -1 = (1, 180°), \qquad -i = (1, 270°), \qquad 1 = (1, 0°).
$$

The second one is worth staring at. Go one step out from the center and turn half a circle, and you land exactly on $-1$. That one sentence, written in the shorter notation and in the angle units mathematicians actually use, becomes an equation often called the most beautiful in mathematics — but it says no more than the sentence does.

<Aside>
Why bother with a second notation? Because multiplication becomes easy. In grid form, multiplying means expanding brackets and collecting terms. In polar form, you multiply the distances and add the angles — and that is the whole rule. Module 5.9 does exactly this.
</Aside>
