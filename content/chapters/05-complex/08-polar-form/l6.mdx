There are two ways to say where a point is: **across and up**, or **how far and which way**.

$$a+bi \qquad\text{versus}\qquad \big(r,\ \theta\big)$$

Both name the same point. The first suits addition; the second suits multiplication, for the reason module 5.7 proved. This module makes the second precise.

## The two pieces

**How far** is the modulus $r = |z|$ of module 5.6 — the distance from the origin, computed by the right-triangle rule.

**Which way** is the **argument** $\theta$, written $\arg z$: the angle you turn counterclockwise, starting from the positive real axis, to face the point. Angles are in degrees here, so a full turn is $360°$.

A point named this way is in **polar form**.

Some points can be read straight off the picture:

| $z$ | $r$ | $\theta$ |
|---|---|---|
| $3$ | $3$ | $0°$ |
| $3i$ | $3$ | $90°$ |
| $-3$ | $3$ | $180°$ |
| $-3i$ | $3$ | $270°$ |
| $2+2i$ | $2\sqrt2$ | $45°$ |

The distances come from module 5.6. The last angle is $45°$ because that point sits on the diagonal, equally far across and up.

## Why this form exists

Module 5.7 proved the theorem the whole chapter runs on:

> Multiplying multiplies the distances and adds the turns.

In across-and-up form that fact is invisible: $(a+bi)(c+di)$ is four products and a sign change, and nothing in it looks like turning. In polar form it is one multiplication and one addition.

$$\text{if } z = (r_1, \theta_1) \text{ and } w = (r_2, \theta_2), \qquad\text{then}\qquad zw = (r_1r_2,\ \theta_1+\theta_2).$$

Addition runs the other way. There is no usable rule relating $\arg(z+w)$ to $\arg z$ and $\arg w$ — try to find one and you will see quickly why nobody adds in polar form.

**Neither form is the right one. The operation decides.**

| Task | Better form |
|---|---|
| add, subtract | across and up |
| multiply, divide | polar |
| raise to a power | polar |
| take roots | polar |
| read off real and imaginary parts | across and up |
| read off distance and direction | polar |

Choosing the description to suit the operation is a habit worth building — the same instinct that puts a figure on the axes for one geometry problem and leaves it off for another.

## Converting between them

One direction you already have: $r = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, the right-triangle rule of module 5.6.

The other direction — recovering $a$ and $b$ from $r$ and $\theta$ — needs a tool this course does not assume: a pair of rules turning an angle into the across and up amounts of a point at distance $1$ from the origin. Those rules exist, and a later course spends a good deal of time on them.

Until then the picture does the work. Drop a line from the point to the horizontal axis and you have a right triangle: slanted side $r$, short sides $a$ and $b$, and the angle $\theta$ at the corner sitting on the origin. Everything this chapter needs can be read off that triangle, and every angle used below — quarter turns, half turns, even divisions of the circle — can be read straight off a drawing.

## The angle is not quite pinned down

Turn a full turn and you are facing the way you started. So a point does not have *one* direction; it has infinitely many, each differing from the next by a whole turn:

$$\theta, \quad \theta + 360°, \quad \theta - 360°, \quad \theta + 720°, \quad \ldots$$

The point $1$ sits at $0°$, and at $360°$, and at $-360°$. The distance has no such problem: $|1|$ is $1$, and that is the end of it.

So $\arg$ is **not a function** in the sense of module 3.1 — one input, many outputs. This is not sloppy notation. It is a fact about circles, and no cleverer notation removes it.

## Picking one angle, and what it costs

The usual repair is to name one of them official: take the angle strictly between $-180°$ and $180°$, and call that *the* argument.

That makes $\arg$ a genuine function, and it costs exactly one thing: a jump. Points just above the negative real axis have angles near $180°$; points just below have angles near $-180°$. Two points almost touching, handed directions almost a full turn apart.

## The jump cannot be designed away

Put the break somewhere else — measure from $0°$ round to $360°$ instead — and the jump moves to the positive real axis. It does not vanish.

The reason is the walk. Going once around the origin returns you to the point you started from, having gained a whole turn on the way, and no single choice of angle can both follow the walk and end where it began.

This is the same repair, with the same side effect, as cutting a function's domain down in order to invert it (module 3.9): shrink things until the answer is unique, and accept the seam that leaves behind.

<Warning title="Two things that catch people out">
**The origin has no direction.** $z = 0$ has distance $0$ and no angle at all — there is no way to point at the place you are already standing. Polar form covers every complex number *except* zero.

**Angles add only up to whole turns.** Multiply $-1$ by $-1$ and you get $1$, whose official angle is $0°$ — but the two official angles being added are $180°$ and $180°$, totalling $360°$. Both answers describe the same point, and they differ by a full turn, which is all the rule ever promised.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The four points $1$, $i$, $-1$, $-i$ sit at $0°$, $90°$, $180°$ and $270°$: quarter turns, evenly spaced round the circle of radius $1$. Multiplying by $i$ steps once around, which is module 5.5's rotation read off the angle.

There is nothing special about quarters. Divide the circle into $n$ equal steps and you get $n$ evenly spaced points, at the corners of a regular $n$-gon — and module 5.9 shows those are exactly the solutions of $z^n = 1$.

So the regular polygons of your geometry course are the solution sets of the simplest equations there are. Gauss proved at nineteen that a regular $17$-gon can be built with compass and straightedge, by working out which of those corners can be reached using square roots alone — and asked to have the figure carved on his gravestone.
</Aside>
