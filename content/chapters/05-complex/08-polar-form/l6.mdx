There are two ways to say where a point is: **across and up**, or **how far and which way**.

$$a+bi \qquad\text{versus}\qquad r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$$

Both name the same point. The second is better suited to multiplication, and this module is about converting between them.

## Two tools borrowed from this year's course

Module 5.7 measured directions in degrees, or in fractions of a turn, and got everything it needed that way. Converting between the two descriptions needs two tools that belong to the Algebra II you are taking right now. Both are stated here, in the form this chapter uses, and neither is assumed beyond that.

**Radians.** An angle in radians is the arc length it cuts on a circle of radius $1$. A full turn is $2\pi$ radians, since that circle has circumference $2\pi$.

| degrees | radians |
|---|---|
| $30°$ | $\pi/6$ |
| $45°$ | $\pi/4$ |
| $60°$ | $\pi/3$ |
| $90°$ | $\pi/2$ |
| $180°$ | $\pi$ |
| $360°$ | $2\pi$ |

$$\text{radians} = \text{degrees}\times\frac{\pi}{180}.$$

Radians are not a convenience. Their whole point is that an arc of $\theta$ radians on that circle *has length* $\theta$ — the angle and the distance travelled are the same number, with no conversion factor anywhere. Every formula in the next two courses is stated this way, so it is worth getting used to now.

**Sine and cosine of any angle.** Your geometry course defined $\cos$ and $\sin$ as ratios of sides in a right triangle, which only makes sense for angles under a quarter turn. Algebra II extends them: $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ are the across and up coordinates of the point you reach by turning $\theta$ from the positive real axis on the circle of radius $1$. For angles under a quarter turn the two definitions agree, since that point sits at the corner of exactly the right triangle.

## Polar form

With those in hand, the point at distance $1$ in the direction $\theta$ — module 5.7's $C(\theta)$ — has coordinates $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, so it *is* the complex number $\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$. Scaling by $r>0$:

$$
r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = r\cos\theta + ir\sin\theta,
$$

the point at **distance $r$** from the origin at **angle $\theta$** counterclockwise from the positive real axis.

$r = |z|$ is the modulus. $\theta$ is the **argument**, written $\arg z$.

## Polar to rectangular

$$
a = r\cos\theta, \qquad b = r\sin\theta.
$$

**Right-triangle trigonometry**, exactly as in your geometry course: the horizontal leg is the hypotenuse times the cosine, the vertical leg is the hypotenuse times the sine.

> **Example.** Convert the point at distance $3$ and angle $\pi/3$.
>
> $\pi/3$ is $60°$, so $\cos(\pi/3) = \frac12$ and $\sin(\pi/3)=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$.
>
> $$a = 3\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac32, \qquad b = 3\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}.$$
>
> So the point is $\frac32+\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}i \approx 1.5+2.598i$.

## Rectangular to polar

$$
r = \sqrt{a^2+b^2}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac ba.
$$

The first is the distance formula. The second comes from dividing $b = r\sin\theta$ by $a = r\cos\theta$.

<Warning title="The tangent does not determine the angle">
$\tan\theta = b/a$ has **two** solutions in a full turn, half a revolution apart, and $\tan^{-1}$ returns only one of them — always in the first or fourth quadrant.

$$z = -1-i:\quad \frac ba = \frac{-1}{-1}=1, \quad \tan^{-1}(1) = \frac\pi4.$$

But $\pi/4$ points into the **first** quadrant, and $-1-i$ is in the third. The correct argument is $\pi/4+\pi = \frac{5\pi}{4}$.

**Always plot the point and check the quadrant.** This is the single most common error in polar conversion, and it happens because $\tan$ is not injective (module 3.5) — restricting its domain to make $\tan^{-1}$ a function is precisely what threw away the other solution.
</Warning>

> **Example.** Convert $-1+i\sqrt3$.
>
> $r = \sqrt{1+3}=2$. And $\tan\theta = \frac{\sqrt3}{-1} = -\sqrt3$, so $\tan^{-1}$ gives $-\pi/3$ — fourth quadrant. The point is in the **second** quadrant, so add $\pi$:
> $$\theta = -\frac\pi3+\pi = \frac{2\pi}{3}.$$
>
> Hence $-1+i\sqrt3 = 2\left(\cos\frac{2\pi}{3}+i\sin\frac{2\pi}{3}\right)$. Check: $2\cos(2\pi/3) = -1$ ✓ and $2\sin(2\pi/3)=\sqrt3$ ✓.

## Non-uniqueness

$$
r\big(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\big) = r\big(\cos(\theta+2\pi k)+i\sin(\theta+2\pi k)\big) \quad\text{for every integer } k,
$$

since turning all the way around returns you to the same point.

So the argument is **not a function** in the sense of module 3.1 — it is not single-valued. $1$ has argument $0$, $2\pi$, $4\pi$, $-2\pi$, and infinitely many others.

The standard repair is to name one of them official: take the angle between $-\pi$ and $\pi$ — up to half a turn each way from the positive real axis. That choice makes $\arg$ a genuine function, at the cost of a jump. Points just below the negative real axis get angles near $-\pi$, points just above get angles near $\pi$, and crossing that ray makes the answer leap by a full turn even though the point barely moved.

## The jump cannot be designed away

Put the break somewhere else — measure from $0$ to $2\pi$ instead — and it moves to the positive real axis rather than disappearing. Any single choice has to break somewhere, because walking once around the origin brings you back to the same point having gained a whole turn.

This is the same repair as restricting sine's domain to define $\sin^{-1}$ (module 3.9), and it has the same side effect: a discontinuity you must remember is there.

**$\theta = 0$ is a special case**: the modulus is $0$, the argument is undefined, and there is no direction to a point sitting at the origin.

## Which form for which job

| Task | Better form |
|---|---|
| add, subtract | rectangular |
| multiply, divide | polar |
| raise to a power | polar |
| take roots | polar |
| identify real/imaginary parts | rectangular |
| identify distance/direction | polar |

Adding in polar form is awful: there is no formula relating $\arg(z+w)$ to $\arg z$ and $\arg w$. Multiplying in rectangular form works and is fiddly.

Choosing the representation to suit the operation is a habit worth building — the same reason your geometry course sometimes places a figure on the axes and sometimes argues synthetically.

<Aside>
The special values in polar form:

$$1: \theta = 0,\qquad i: \theta = \frac\pi2,\qquad -1: \theta = \pi,\qquad -i: \theta = \frac{3\pi}{2}.$$

Four points, evenly spaced round the unit circle at quarter turns. Multiplying by $i$ steps once round, which is module 5.5's rotation seen in polar coordinates.

More generally, the solutions of $z^n = 1$ are the $n$ points at angles $\frac{2\pi k}{n}$ for $k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$ — the vertices of a **regular $n$-gon** inscribed in the unit circle, with one vertex at $1$.

So the regular polygons of your geometry course are the solution sets of the simplest possible polynomial equations. Gauss proved at nineteen that a regular $17$-gon is constructible with compass and straightedge, by studying which of these roots can be built from square roots — a result that connects construction, algebra, and number theory in one theorem.
</Aside>
