> **Proposition.** $x^2 \geq 0$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}$; hence $x^2 + 1$ has no real root.

<Proof>
Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Every real is positive, zero, or negative, so $x>0$, $x=0$, or $x<0$. In the first case $x^2 > 0$ as a product of positives; in the second $x^2 = 0$; in the third $-x > 0$ and $x^2 = (-x)(-x) > 0$. The cases are exhaustive, so $x^2 \geq 0$ and $x^2 + 1 \geq 1 > 0$.
</Proof>

The proof used only the arithmetic and ordering rules, and that is the substantive point: the obstruction is not about $\mathbb{R}$ specifically but about order. In any such system, squares are non-negative, so $x^2+1$ has no root. Consequently **$\mathbb{C}$ cannot be ordered compatibly with its arithmetic** — the price of adjoining $i$ is the loss of $<$, and it is unavoidable rather than an oversight.

## The extension sequence

| System | Defect | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{N}$ | no additive inverses | $\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | cannot divide | $\mathbb{Q}$ |
| $\mathbb{Q}$ | not order-complete | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $\mathbb{R}$ | not algebraically closed | $\mathbb{C}$ |

Each step adjoins what is missing and verifies the axioms survive. Note the last two failures are of different kinds — one analytic, one algebraic — and correspondingly $\mathbb{R}$ is built by filling gaps and $\mathbb{C}$ by adjoining a root.

## The construction, done properly

Declaring "let $i$ be a number with $i^2 = -1$" is a *definition by fiat*, and by itself it guarantees nothing: one may equally declare a number $j$ with $j \cdot 0 = 1$, and that declaration is inconsistent. What legitimises $i$ is a construction inside existing mathematics.

Two standard ones:

**As polynomials, with $x^2+1$ counted as zero.** Take real polynomials and agree to treat two of them as the same whenever they differ by a multiple of $x^2+1$. Under that agreement $x^2$ *is* $-1$, so the polynomial $x$ plays the part of $i$, and every polynomial reduces to the form $a + bx$. All four operations work, and it is the construction that generalises: the same trick adjoins a root of any polynomial you like.

**As pairs.** $\mathbb{C} := \mathbb{R}^2$ with
$$(a,b)+(c,d) = (a+c, b+d), \qquad (a,b)(c,d) = (ac-bd,\ ad+bc).$$
Verifying the arithmetic rules is a finite computation; $i := (0,1)$ satisfies $i^2 = (-1,0)$.

Either way $\mathbb{C}$ exists as an object built from $\mathbb{R}$, so no new consistency assumption is made. **If $\mathbb{R}$ is consistent, so is $\mathbb{C}$** — and that, not the fiat, is what makes $i$ legitimate.

<Warning title="Two persistent misstatements">
**"Imaginary numbers aren't real."** A pun on terminology Descartes coined dismissively. Every extension in the table above was resisted in its time; negative numbers were called "absurd" into the eighteenth century.

**"You can't take the square root of a negative number."** The correct statement is that no element of a system whose ordering fits its arithmetic has negative square. In $\mathbb{C}$, every element has exactly two square roots, including $-1$.

**A real caution, though:** $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a \sqrt b$ **fails** on $\mathbb{C}$. The classic trap is
$$-1 = i^2 = \sqrt{-1}\sqrt{-1} = \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt1 = 1.$$
The identity holds for non-negative reals and not in general, because $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ on $\mathbb{C}$ requires a branch choice (§5.8). Write $i$, never $\sqrt{-1}$.
</Warning>

## What the extension costs

Every extension in Chapter 4 was a pure gain: $\mathbb{Z}$ kept everything $\mathbb{N}$ had, $\mathbb{Q}$ kept everything $\mathbb{Z}$ had. This one is not.

The reals carry an ordering that fits their arithmetic. The complex numbers support all four operations and carry no such ordering whatsoever. The proof is the three-case argument above: in a system whose ordering fits its arithmetic every square is non-negative, and $i^2 = -1$ makes that impossible.

So the following are not false but **meaningless**:
$$i > 0, \qquad 3+4i < 5+2i, \qquad \sup\{z : |z|<1\}.$$

The loss is real. Every theorem that mentions increasing functions, least upper bounds, or the guarantee that a continuous function passing from negative to positive must cross zero somewhere in between — that last one is the Intermediate Value Theorem, which your calculus course states early — refers to the order, and none of them transfers. There is no complex version of "increasing on an interval."

What replaces the order is the **modulus** (§5.6): a real-valued measure of size, so that $|z|<|w|$ is a comparison of reals and therefore legitimate. Every convergence statement in complex analysis is phrased that way, for exactly this reason.

**This is the general pattern of extensions.** Each new system buys a property and usually sells one, and knowing which was sold tells you which theorems will not survive the move.

## Algebraic closure

A number system is **algebraically closed** when every polynomial equation with coefficients from it has a solution inside it.

> **Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.** $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed.

The striking content is not that $x^2+1$ acquires a root but that adjoining *one* root of *one* polynomial suffices for all of them, including polynomials with complex coefficients. Compare $\mathbb{Q}$, where adjoining $\sqrt2$ leaves $\sqrt3$ missing and no finite extension ever closes: $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ is infinite-dimensional over $\mathbb{Q}$, while $\mathbb{C}$ is two-dimensional over $\mathbb{R}$.

No purely algebraic proof exists, and this is not a gap in knowledge — it cannot exist. The reals are pinned down by their no-gaps property, so any proof of this has to appeal to that somewhere. Standard proofs use Liouville's theorem, the argument principle, or an argument that $|p(z)|$ attains a minimum.

<Aside>
Historically, quadratics were not the motivation. Cardano's formula for $x^3 = 15x + 4$ passes through $\sqrt{-121}$ and emerges at $x = 4$ — an integer answer reachable only via a detour outside $\mathbb{R}$. Bombelli's 1572 analysis of that computation is where complex numbers were first taken seriously, precisely because they could not be dismissed as answers to unanswerable questions: here they were intermediate steps toward answers everyone accepted.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

The extension to $\mathbb{C}$ is what makes the algebra of the next two courses uniform.

**In calculus, complex roots explain the shape of partial fractions.** Every real polynomial factors over $\mathbb{R}$ into linear and *irreducible quadratic* factors — and the quadratics are irreducible precisely because their roots are complex. That is why partial-fraction decomposition has two cases, one producing $\frac{A}{x-a}$ and one producing $\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+px+q}$, and why the second integrates to a logarithm plus an arctangent rather than to a logarithm alone.

The same fact governs second-order linear differential equations. The characteristic equation $ar^2+br+c=0$ has real roots when the discriminant is positive, giving exponential solutions, and complex roots when it is negative, giving oscillation. **The discriminant's sign is the same $b^2-4ac$ from your quadratic formula**, and the switch from growth to oscillation is Euler's formula (§5.7) doing its work.

</WhereThisGoes>
