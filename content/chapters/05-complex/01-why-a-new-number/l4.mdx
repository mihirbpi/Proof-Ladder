> **Proposition.** For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$, $x^2 \geq 0$. Consequently $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no real solution.

<Proof>
Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary. By trichotomy — every real is positive, zero, or negative — exactly one of $x > 0$, $x = 0$, $x < 0$ holds.

If $x > 0$: the product of two positives is positive, so $x^2 > 0$.
If $x = 0$: $x^2 = 0$.
If $x < 0$: then $-x > 0$, and $x^2 = (-x)(-x) > 0$.

The cases are exhaustive, so $x^2 \geq 0$ for all real $x$. Hence $x^2 + 1 \geq 1 > 0$, and $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no real solution.
</Proof>

Restated: the polynomial $x^2 + 1 \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ has **no root in $\mathbb{R}$**. And it is not an exotic polynomial — degree two, integer coefficients, the simplest possible example of the failure.

## The extension pattern

This is the fourth time in the course that a system has failed to answer a legitimate question.

| System | Failure | Extension |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{N}$ | $3 - 5$ has no value | $\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | $3x = 1$ has no solution | $\mathbb{Q}$ |
| $\mathbb{Q}$ | $x^2 = 2$ has no solution; sequences have no limits | $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $\mathbb{R}$ | $x^2 = -1$ has no solution | $\mathbb{C}$ |

The move is always the same: adjoin what is missing, then verify the arithmetic still works. The verification is the hard part — Chapter 5 is largely that verification — and the reason it is worth doing is that the payoff is bigger each time.

## Where this shows up in ordinary algebra

The gap has a familiar face. A square with side $s$ has area $s \times s$, and an area is never negative — so no square you can draw has area $-1$. Turned into a question about numbers, that says: no number multiplied by itself gives $-1$.

Every time you take a square root you are running that fact in reverse, and every time the thing under the root turns out negative, the answer is "there is no such number" and you stop.

That is accurate but incomplete. There *is* an answer; it is not on the number line. Once $i$ exists, "no solution" becomes "a solution that is not real" — and Algebra I's rule that some equations simply have no answer turns out to be a statement about where you were looking, not about whether anything is there.

## The definition

$$
i \text{ is a number with } i^2 = -1.
$$

That single line is the entire content of the extension. Everything in modules 5.2–5.9 is a consequence.

<Warning title="Two claims to avoid">
**"$i$ isn't a real number, so it isn't real."** A pun on a badly chosen name. $\mathbb{R}$ is a technical term. Negative numbers, zero, and irrationals were each considered dubious in their time; $i$ is the most recent addition to a list of things that turned out to be fine.

**"You can't take the square root of a negative number."** The accurate statement is that no *real* number squares to $-1$ — which the proposition above establishes. Extending the system removes the obstruction, exactly as extending $\mathbb{Z}$ to $\mathbb{Q}$ removed the obstruction to $3x=1$.
</Warning>

## The payoff

$\mathbb{C}$ is the last extension needed. This is the **Fundamental Theorem of Algebra**:

> Every non-constant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root.

So adjoining a root of the single polynomial $x^2 + 1$ supplies roots for *every* polynomial at once — including polynomials whose coefficients are themselves complex. The sequence $\mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Q} \to \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}$ terminates, and nothing further needs inventing.

Note that the theorem is not proved by algebra alone; every known proof reaches outside algebra for tools. It is stated here as motivation, not derived.

<Aside>
Historically the motivation came from cubics, not quadratics. Cardano's formula for $x^3 = 15x + 4$ requires square roots of negative numbers *en route*, even though the answer $x = 4$ is a perfectly ordinary integer. Sixteenth-century algebraists found they had to pass through $\sqrt{-121}$ to arrive somewhere real, and that unavoidable detour — not the unsolvable quadratic — is what forced the issue.
</Aside>
