To **square** a number, build a square of counters with that many along each side — then count every counter. Let's make a table.

| Side of the square | Squares inside |
|---|---|
| $1$ | $1$ |
| $2$ | $4$ |
| $3$ | $9$ |
| $4$ | $16$ |
| $5$ | $25$ |

Look down the right-hand column. What do you notice?

**Every single answer is a real, countable amount.** You can never build a square that holds *less than nothing*.

## Turning instead of counting

There is another way to square something, and it is about **turning**.

Face the window. Now make a quarter turn. Now make the *same* quarter turn again.

You are facing away from the window — the **opposite** way from where you started.

Doing a turn twice is a lot like squaring. And here, doing it twice got you to *backwards*.

## The gap

So here is a question with no answer:

> Is there a **number** that, squared, turns you backwards?

Try everything. Big numbers make bigger squares. Small numbers make smaller squares. Every one of them holds counters you can point at, and none of them ever points you the other way.

There is a **gap**. A question that looks perfectly reasonable, and none of our numbers can answer it.

## What people did about it

They could have shrugged and said "that question has no answer." Some did, for a long time.

But you have seen this pattern before! In module 4.1, whole numbers could not handle sharing $7$ between $2$ — so people invented pieces. In module 4.7, fractions could not handle the diagonal of a square — so people accepted new numbers again.

Every time a question had no answer, people built a new number to answer it. And every time, they checked very carefully that the new number did not break anything old.

That is exactly what happened here, and it is what this chapter is about.

<Aside>
The new number is called $i$, and it has exactly one job: **joining $i$ on turns you a quarter turn.** Do it twice and you are facing backwards. Everything else in this chapter follows from that single line.
</Aside>
