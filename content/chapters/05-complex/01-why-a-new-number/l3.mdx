Chapter 4 ended with a pattern. Each new kind of number was built because the ones before it could not answer a reasonable question.

| Number system | Question it could not answer | What got built |
|---|---|---|
| counting numbers | what is $7 \div 2$? | fractions |
| fractions | how long is the diagonal of a square? | the not-fractions |

This chapter is the next step in that story, and it starts with a question about **turning**.

## Turning a card

Put a card on the table with an arrow drawn on it, pointing right.

Turn the card a **quarter turn** counterclockwise. The arrow now points up.

Turn it another quarter turn. The arrow points left — the *opposite* of where it started.

Two more quarter turns and you are back to the beginning. Four quarter turns, all the way round.

## The question

Now here is the question that needed a new number.

Doing a turn twice is a bit like multiplying a number by itself. Turning a quarter turn twice gets you to *pointing backwards*.

So: **is there a number which, multiplied by itself, reverses you?**

Among the numbers you know, no.

<Proof>
Take any number on the number line and multiply it by itself. Where does the answer land?

If your number is bigger than zero, then multiplying two numbers bigger than zero gives an answer bigger than zero.

If your number is zero, the answer is zero.

And there is no third possibility among the numbers you have met.

So multiplying a number by itself always lands at zero or above. It never reverses you.
</Proof>

There is a whole direction — *backwards* — that squaring can never reach.

## What people did about it

Here is what mathematicians did **not** do: declare the question meaningless.

Here is what they did do: **invent a number that answers it, and then check very carefully that nothing broke.**

They called it $i$, and it has exactly one job:

> Multiplying by $i$ turns you a quarter turn.

Do it twice and you have turned a half turn, which points you backwards. That is the whole definition, and everything else in this chapter is squeezed out of it.

<Warning title="Two things people say that are wrong">
People sometimes say $i$ is "imaginary, so it isn't real." That is a pun on a badly chosen name, not a fact. $i$ is exactly as real as $\tfrac12$ or the diagonal of a square — both of which were once considered suspicious, and both of which are now ordinary.

People also say "you can't multiply a number by itself and get a reversal." What is true is that no number *on the number line* does it. Once you step off the line and start turning, you can.
</Warning>

## The check that mattered

Inventing a number is easy. Inventing one that does not break anything is hard.

If $i$ had caused a contradiction — if it let you prove that $1 = 2$ — it would have been thrown away. It does not. You can add, subtract, multiply, and divide with $i$ in the mix, and every rule you already know keeps working.

That check took mathematicians about two hundred years to feel comfortable with. The rest of this chapter walks through it.

## Why it was worth the trouble

Because the payoff turned out to be enormous.

Once you have $i$, **every** equation of the kind you meet in algebra has an answer. Not just this one — all of them, forever. Nothing more ever needs inventing.

That is why the story that started with counting numbers and ran through fractions finally stops here.

<Aside>
The names are unfortunate. Calling $i$ *imaginary* and the number line *real* was originally an insult, coined by people who did not believe in $i$. The names stuck. Try not to read anything into them.
</Aside>
