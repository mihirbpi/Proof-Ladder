Chapter 4 ended with $\mathbb{R}$: the rationals with their gaps filled. It is a field, it is dense, it is complete. And it still cannot do something.

## The gap

> **Proposition.** There is no real number $x$ with $x^2 + 1 = 0$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Every real is negative, zero, or positive — exactly one, with no fourth option.</div>
  <div slot="want">$x^2 + 1 \neq 0$ for every real $x$; equivalently $x^2 \geq 0$ always.</div>
  <div slot="how">No single algebraic step covers all three sign cases, since the rule for the sign of a product differs between them. <strong>Split into cases</strong> (module 4.4), and check the split is exhaustive.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $x \in \mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary. Exactly one of the following holds.

*Case 1: $x > 0$.* A product of two positives is positive, so $x^2 > 0$.

*Case 2: $x = 0$.* Then $x^2 = 0$.

*Case 3: $x < 0$.* A product of two negatives is positive, so $x^2 > 0$.

In every case $x^2 \geq 0$, so $x^2 + 1 \geq 1 > 0$. The cases exhaust $\mathbb{R}$, and $x$ was arbitrary, so no real satisfies $x^2 + 1 = 0$.
</Proof>

Note that the proof used the **ordering** of $\mathbb{R}$, not just its arithmetic. That will matter in module 5.2, where the new number system turns out to have no ordering at all — and this proof is the reason why it cannot.

## Where you have already met this

The gap is not exotic. You met it in Algebra I, in the quadratic formula:

$$
x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}.
$$

The **discriminant** $b^2 - 4ac$ decides everything:

| Discriminant | Real solutions |
|---|---|
| positive | two |
| zero | one |
| negative | **none** |

That last row is the same gap. $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has discriminant $-4$, and the formula asks for $\sqrt{-4}$, which module 3.2 said does not exist in $\mathbb{R}$.

You were taught to write "no real solutions" and stop. **The question this chapter asks is what happens if you do not stop.**

## The pattern, one more time

Module 4.1 laid out the chain, and each link was forged for exactly this reason:

| System | Cannot do | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{N}$ | $3 - 5$ | negatives → $\mathbb{Z}$ |
| $\mathbb{Z}$ | $1 \div 3$ | fractions → $\mathbb{Q}$ |
| $\mathbb{Q}$ | $\sqrt2$ | fill the gaps → $\mathbb{R}$ |
| $\mathbb{R}$ | $\sqrt{-1}$ | ? |

Every previous row was resolved by building a bigger system, and every resolution was resisted at the time. Negative numbers were called *absurd* into the 17th century. Irrationals caused a crisis (module 4.7). Each was accepted once it proved useful and once someone showed how to construct it.

**Row four is the same story**, and the construction is module 5.2's job.

<Warning title="Inventing a solution is not obviously allowed">
You cannot fix every failure by declaring a solution into existence.

Try it on division by zero. Suppose we invent $\omega$ with $0 \cdot \omega = 1$. Then
$$
1 = 0 \cdot \omega = (0 + 0)\cdot \omega = 0\cdot\omega + 0\cdot\omega = 1 + 1 = 2,
$$
so $1 = 2$, and from there every number equals every other. The system collapses.

**So the question is never "may I invent a number?" but "does the result still work?"** For $\sqrt{-1}$ the answer turns out to be yes — the resulting system keeps every field property of $\mathbb{R}$ — and module 5.3 verifies it rather than assuming it.

That verification is what separates $i$ from $\omega$, and it is the only thing that does.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Here is the payoff, and it is far larger than plugging one hole.

Once $\sqrt{-1}$ exists, **every** polynomial equation becomes solvable. Not just $x^2+1 = 0$, but $x^5 - 3x + 1 = 0$ and every other — any polynomial of degree $n$ with complex coefficients has exactly $n$ complex roots, counted properly.

That is the *Fundamental Theorem of Algebra*, and it is a remarkable piece of luck. Adding one number to fix one equation happened to fix all of them at once, and there was no reason to expect that.

Compare the earlier rows. Adding negatives fixed subtraction and left division broken; adding fractions fixed division and left $\sqrt2$ missing. Only the last extension finishes the job — and after $\mathbb{C}$, no further extension is needed.
</Aside>
