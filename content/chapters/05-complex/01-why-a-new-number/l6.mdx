Chapter 4 built each number system by fixing a closure failure. $\mathbb{N}$ could not subtract, so $\mathbb{Z}$ was built. $\mathbb{Z}$ could not divide, so $\mathbb{Q}$ was built. $\mathbb{Q}$ had gaps, so $\mathbb{R}$ was built.

$\mathbb{R}$ still cannot do one thing, and this chapter fixes it.

## The failure

> **Proposition.** The equation $x^2 + 1 = 0$ has no solution in $\mathbb{R}$.

<Proof>
Suppose not: suppose $x\in\mathbb{R}$ satisfies $x^2+1=0$, so $x^2 = -1$.

Every real number is positive, negative, or zero. If $x>0$ then $x^2 = x\cdot x>0$. If $x<0$ then $x^2>0$ as well, since the product of two negatives is positive. If $x = 0$ then $x^2=0$.

The cases are exhaustive, so $x^2\geq0$ for every real $x$. But $-1<0$, a contradiction.

Hence no real $x$ satisfies $x^2+1=0$.
</Proof>

Three cases and a contradiction — modules 1.12 and 2.5, on the simplest possible polynomial. **The failure is not exotic.** It happens at $x^2+1$, and it is why the quadratic formula sometimes stalls.

## Where you have already met it

The discriminant. For $ax^2+bx+c=0$,

$$x = \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a},$$

and when $b^2-4ac<0$ your course says "no real solutions."

Read that phrasing again. It does not say *no solutions* — it says *no real solutions*, and the qualifier has been there all along, waiting for this chapter. The parabola misses the $x$-axis, and the algebra says the roots live somewhere the real line does not reach.

## The move

The response is the same one Chapter 4 made three times: **build a bigger system in which the operation succeeds.**

Declare a new object $i$ with the property
$$i^2 = -1,$$
and see what must be true of a system containing both $i$ and all the reals.

If the arithmetic rules (module 4.5) are to hold, that system must contain $a + bi$ for all real $a, b$ — closure under multiplication and addition demands it. And a short computation shows that set is already closed under all four operations, so nothing more is needed.

The result is written $\mathbb{C}$ — the standard name for the **complex numbers**, joining $\mathbb{N}, \mathbb{Z}, \mathbb{Q}, \mathbb{R}$ from module 2.1 — and module 5.3 verifies the axioms.

<Warning title="This is not cheating">
The objection is natural: you cannot just *invent* a number with the property you wanted.

But the same objection applies to $-1$ and to $\frac12$ and to $\sqrt2$, and every one of them was resisted when introduced. Negative numbers were called *fictitious* into the seventeenth century; Chapter 4 showed $\sqrt2$ destroyed a school of Greek mathematics.

What makes an extension legitimate is not familiarity but **consistency**: the new system must contain the old one, must satisfy the same laws, and must not prove anything false. All three can be checked, and for $\mathbb{C}$ all three hold — $\mathbb{C}$ can be built concretely from pairs of real numbers, with no new objects assumed at all (module 5.2).

The word *imaginary* is a seventeenth-century insult that stuck. It describes the history, not the mathematics.
</Warning>

## The payoff

Extending to $\mathbb{C}$ solves the problem completely, and that is genuinely surprising.

Adding $i$ was meant to solve one equation. It solves **all** of them:

> **Fundamental Theorem of Algebra.** Every non-constant polynomial with complex coefficients has a complex root.

No further extension is ever needed. Compare with the earlier steps: adding negatives did not give you fractions, and adding fractions did not give you $\sqrt2$. Each of those repairs left work undone. This one does not.

A system with this property is called **algebraically closed**, and the complex numbers are the smallest extension of the reals having it.

## What it costs

Extensions are not free, and this one gives up the order.

$\mathbb{R}$ is a system whose ordering fits its arithmetic (module 4.5): any two reals compare, and the order respects the arithmetic. **$\mathbb{C}$ has no such order.**

The reason is the one from the proposition above. In a system whose ordering fits its arithmetic, every square is non-negative — that was the three-case argument. But $i^2 = -1$, so $i$ would have a negative square, and no ordering can survive it.

So "$i > 0$" and "$i<0$" are both meaningless. Asking which of two complex numbers is larger is asking a malformed question, and module 5.6 supplies the replacement: you may compare their distances from the origin, and that is a comparison of *real* numbers.

## What it buys, for geometry

Here is the part your course will find useful.

Module 3.1 wrote the $90°$ rotation about the origin as $(x,y)\mapsto(-y,x)$. Now identify the point $(x,y)$ with the complex number $x+yi$ and multiply by $i$:

$$i(x+yi) = ix + i^2y = -y + xi.$$

**That is the same rotation.** Multiplying by $i$ rotates the plane a quarter turn, and $i^2 = -1$ says two quarter turns is a half turn — which is exactly the transformation $(x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)$.

The defining property of $i$, read geometrically, is a fact about rotations you already know. Module 5.9 develops this into a general rule: multiplication by any complex number is a rotation combined with a dilation, and the two transformations your geometry course treats separately turn out to be a single operation.

<Aside>
Complex numbers were discovered in the sixteenth century, and not for quadratics.

Cardano and Bombelli were solving *cubics*. The formula for a cubic sometimes requires square roots of negatives **in the intermediate steps** even when all three roots are real and obvious. Bombelli found in 1572 that $x^3 = 15x + 4$ has the plain root $x=4$, and the formula produces it only by way of $\sqrt{-121}$.

That is a much harder case to dismiss than a quadratic with no real roots. Here the answer is real, verifiable, and unreachable without passing through the complex numbers.

The imaginary quantities were doing real work two centuries before anyone could say what they were, and the geometric interpretation — the plane of module 5.5 — arrived only around 1800.
</Aside>
