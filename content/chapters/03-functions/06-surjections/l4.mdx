## Definition

$f : S \to T$ is **surjective** (or **onto**) when
$$
\forall t \in T,\ \exists s \in S,\ f(s) = t,
$$
equivalently $\operatorname{Im}(f) = T$, equivalently $|f^{-1}(\{t\})| \geq 1$ for every $t \in T$.

Compare with injectivity, which said *at most* one pre-image. Injectivity and surjectivity are the two halves of "exactly one", and having both is module 3.8.

## The shape of the proof

The definition is $\forall \exists$, so by module 1.12 the proof is: take an arbitrary $t \in T$, then **exhibit a witness** $s$. The witness is almost always built out of $t$, and finding the formula for it is where the work lies.

> Let $t \in T$ be arbitrary. Set $s = \ldots$ [built from $t$]. Then $s \in S$, and $f(s) = t$. Hence $f$ is surjective.

Note the small obligation that is easy to skip: you must check the witness actually lies in $S$.

> **Proposition.** $g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$, $g(x) = e^x$, is surjective.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$t > 0$. Available: $\ln$ is defined on $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, takes real values, and satisfies $e^{\ln t} = t$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Some real $x$ with $e^x = t$.</div>
  <div slot="how">The witness is $x = \ln t$. The hypothesis $t > 0$ is exactly what makes $\ln t$ defined, so it is doing real work — this is where the choice of codomain enters.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $t \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ be arbitrary. Since $t > 0$, the number $x = \ln t$ is a well-defined real number, so $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

Then $g(x) = e^{\ln t} = t$.

As $t$ was an arbitrary element of $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, every element of the codomain is attained, so $g$ is surjective.
</Proof>

## The codomain decides

> **The same rule with a different codomain.** $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = e^x$, is **not** surjective.

<Proof>
Take $t = -1 \in \mathbb{R}$. For every $x \in \mathbb{R}$ we have $e^x > 0 > -1$, so no $x$ satisfies $f(x) = -1$. Hence $\operatorname{Im}(f) \neq \mathbb{R}$.
</Proof>

$f$ and $g$ have the same rule and the same domain, and differ only in the declared codomain — and one is surjective while the other is not. Module 3.1 insisted that domain and codomain are part of the function; this is the sharpest illustration of why.

<Warning title="Injectivity and surjectivity are not symmetric in this respect">
Injectivity depends only on the rule and the domain: enlarging the codomain cannot create a collision. Surjectivity depends on the codomain essentially.

Consequently "is $f$ injective?" is a well-posed question once you know the rule and domain, while "is $f$ surjective?" is not well-posed until the codomain is stated.
</Warning>

## Disproving surjectivity

The negation of $\forall t\, \exists s,\ f(s) = t$ is, by module 1.11,
$$
\exists t \in T,\ \forall s \in S,\ f(s) \neq t.
$$
So you exhibit **one** unhit target — and then prove *nothing* hits it, which is a universal claim requiring an argument, not a search. That second half is what people forget.

In the $e^x$ example above, the unhit target was $-1$ and the universal argument was "$e^x > 0$ for all $x$."

## More examples

| Function | Surjective? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z}$ | Yes | given $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, take $x = n$ |
| $m : \mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$, $m(a,b)=ab$ | Yes | given $n$, take $(n,1)$ |
| $f : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $f(x)=x^2$ | No | $-1$ is unhit, since $x^2 \geq 0$ |
| $f : \mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)$, $f(x)=x^2$ | Yes | given $t \geq 0$, take $x = \sqrt t$ |
| $r : \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\} \to \mathbb{R}$, $r(x)=1/x$ | No | $0$ is unhit |
| $r : \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\} \to \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ | Yes | given $t \neq 0$, take $x = 1/t$ |

Three of these pairs are the same rule twice, with the codomain adjusted. That is the module in one table.

<Aside>
Any function becomes surjective if you replace its codomain by its image: $f : S \to \operatorname{Im}(f)$ is surjective by construction. So surjectivity measures whether the declared codomain was honest, and the standard factorisation $S \twoheadrightarrow \operatorname{Im}(f) \hookrightarrow T$ writes any function as a surjection followed by an injection.
</Aside>
