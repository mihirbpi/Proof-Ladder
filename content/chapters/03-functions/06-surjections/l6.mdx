Injectivity ruled out merging. This module rules out the other defect: **missing**.

## Definition

$f : S\to T$ is **surjective** (or **onto**) when every element of the codomain is hit:

$$
\forall t \in T,\ \exists s \in S:\ f(s)=t.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some".*

Equivalently, $f(S) = T$ — the image is the whole codomain.

## The proof template

The definition is $\forall t\, \exists s$, and modules 1.10 and 1.12 give the shape directly:

> Let $t \in T$ be **arbitrary**. Let $s = $ [something built from $t$]. Then $s \in S$ and $f(s)=t$.

Take any one and follow it — for the $\forall$ — and produce a witness for the $\exists$. Note the order: the witness $s$ is allowed to depend on $t$, which is why the quantifiers are in that order and not the other.

> **Proposition.** $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $f(x)=3x-2$ is surjective.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">We need, for each target $y$, an input landing on it.</div>
  <div slot="want">A formula for $x$ in terms of $y$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Solve $3x - 2 = y$ backwards as scratch work — that is where $x = (y+2)/3$ comes from — then present the proof forwards, with the verification.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $y \in \mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary. Set $x = \dfrac{y+2}{3}$, which is a real number.

Then
$$f(x) = 3\cdot\frac{y+2}{3} - 2 = (y+2)-2 = y.$$

So $y$ has a pre-image. Since $y$ was arbitrary, $f$ is surjective.
</Proof>

**The backwards algebra belongs in the Discussion, and the verification belongs in the proof.** This is module 1.6's warning about working backwards: solving for $x$ is how you *find* the witness, and checking $f(x)=y$ is what *proves* it works.

## Failing to be surjective

> **Proposition.** $g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $g(x)=x^2$ is not surjective.

<Proof>
Consider $y = -1$. For every real $x$, $x^2 \geq 0 > -1$, so $g(x)\neq -1$.

So $-1$ has no pre-image, and $g$ is not surjective.
</Proof>

Refuting a $\forall$ needs one counterexample — but note that the counterexample here required its own universal argument, since showing $-1$ is missed means showing *no* $x$ works. Refuting surjectivity is genuinely harder than refuting injectivity, and the quantifier structure predicts that.

<Warning title="Surjectivity is a fact about the codomain, not the formula">
$$f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\ f(x)=x^2 \quad\text{is not surjective.}$$
$$g:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty),\ g(x)=x^2 \quad\text{is surjective.}$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers.*

Same formula. Different functions, because the codomain is part of the function (module 3.1).

**Every function is surjective onto its own image.** Replacing $T$ by $f(S)$ makes surjectivity automatic, which means the property is really a statement about how you *declared* the function, not about what it does.

Injectivity has no such escape. Merging is intrinsic; missing is bookkeeping.
</Warning>

## Rigid motions are surjective

> **Proposition.** Every rotation of the plane about the origin is surjective.

<Proof>
Let $\rho$ be the rotation through $\theta$ about the origin, and let $Q$ be an arbitrary point of the plane.

Let $P$ be the image of $Q$ under the rotation through $-\theta$ about the origin. Then rotating $P$ through $\theta$ returns it to $Q$, so $\rho(P)=Q$.

Since $Q$ was arbitrary, $\rho$ is surjective.
</Proof>

The witness was produced by rotating backwards — the general strategy for surjectivity is to build a candidate source from the target, and for a transformation that means undoing it.

The same works for every rigid motion. A reflection is its own undoing; a translation by $(a,b)$ is undone by translating by $(-a,-b)$; a composition is undone by undoing the pieces in reverse order.

**That is why congruence works in both directions in your course.** If a rigid motion carries $F$ onto $G$, surjectivity supplies the motion carrying $G$ back to $F$, so *$F$ congruent to $G$* and *$G$ congruent to $F$* are the same claim.

## Not every function has this

The projection $\pi : \mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$, $\pi(x,y)=(x,0)$, is surjective onto the $x$-axis and not onto the plane. It also merges: $\pi(1,5)=\pi(1,-3)$.

Projections lose information in both ways, which is why shadows are ambiguous and why a two-dimensional drawing of a solid needs conventions to be readable.

## Surjectivity and the round trip

Module 3.3 showed $f(f^{-1}(B))\subseteq B$, with strictness possible. Equality for **every** $B\subseteq T$ holds exactly when $f$ is surjective — a missed element cannot be recovered, because the pre-image that would have produced it is empty.

So the two round-trip identities from module 3.3 correspond exactly:

| Identity | Holds for all subsets exactly when |
|---|---|
| $f^{-1}(f(A)) = A$ | $f$ is injective |
| $f\big(f^{-1}(B)\big) = B$ | $f$ is surjective |

Both at once is a bijection, and that is the next module but one.

<Aside>
Surjections compare sizes in the opposite direction from injections. If a surjection $S\to T$ exists, then $S$ is at least as large as $T$ — each element of $T$ needs its own source, and sources may be reused but never invented.

Combine with the previous module: an injection $S\to T$ says $|S|\leq|T|$; a surjection $S\to T$ says $|S|\geq|T|$. Having both should say the sizes are equal, and for finite sets it plainly does.

For infinite sets, that both-conditions-give-equality claim is the **Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem**, and it is genuinely difficult — the obvious proof does not work, because you cannot simply pair the two functions up. It took twenty years and three mathematicians to settle in the 1890s.
</Aside>
