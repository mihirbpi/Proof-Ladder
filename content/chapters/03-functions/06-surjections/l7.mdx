$f : S \to T$ is **surjective** when
$$
\forall t \in T,\ \exists s \in S,\ f(s) = t,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some".*

equivalently $\operatorname{Im}(f) = T$, equivalently $f^{-1}(\{t\})$ is nonempty for every output $t$.

Injectivity capped each of those sets at one element; surjectivity puts a floor under them. §3.8 imposes both.

## Proof shape: arbitrary target, exhibited witness

The definition is $\forall\exists$, so §1.12 dictates: fix arbitrary $t \in T$, construct $s \in S$ depending on $t$, verify $f(s) = t$. Two things to check are routinely skipped — that the construction is *defined* for every $t \in T$, and that the result *lies in $S$*.

> **Proposition.** $g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$, $g(x) = e^x$, is surjective.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$t > 0$; $\ln : \mathbb{R}_{>0} \to \mathbb{R}$ exists and satisfies $e^{\ln t} = t$.</div>
  <div slot="want">A real $x$ with $e^x = t$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Take $x = \ln t$. The hypothesis $t > 0$ is exactly the condition under which $\ln t$ is defined — so the choice of codomain is not incidental to the proof, it is what makes the proof possible.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $t \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ be arbitrary. Since $t > 0$, $x := \ln t$ is well defined — the logarithm returns exactly one value, and that value is real — so $x \in \mathbb{R}$. Then $g(x) = e^{\ln t} = t$. Since $t$ could have been any of them, $g$ is surjective.
</Proof>

A construction-free alternative, worth knowing because it generalizes: $\exp$ is continuous on $\mathbb{R}$ with $\lim_{x\to-\infty} e^x = 0$ and $\lim_{x\to+\infty} e^x = +\infty$, so by the intermediate value theorem its image contains every $t > 0$. This is non-constructive in flavour — it shows a pre-image exists without producing one — but often the only available route.

## Codomain dependence

> $f : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = e^x$, is **not** surjective: $e^x > 0$ for all $x$, so $-1$ is unattained.

Same rule, same domain as $g$; only the codomain differs, and the answer flips. Since the domain and codomain are part of what a function *is* (§3.1), $f$ and $g$ are different functions, and this is the cleanest demonstration of why that convention is not fussiness.

<Warning title="An asymmetry between the two properties">
Injectivity depends on the rule and the domain only — enlarging the codomain cannot create collisions. Surjectivity depends essentially on the codomain.

So "is $f$ injective?" is well-posed given rule and domain; "is $f$ surjective?" is not well-posed until the codomain is declared. Students who report that surjectivity is the harder notion are usually reporting this asymmetry.
</Warning>

## Refutation

By §1.11 the negation is
$$
\exists t \in T,\ \forall s \in S,\ f(s) \neq t.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some".*

Exhibiting the unhit $t$ is only half; the other half is a *universal* argument that nothing reaches it. In the example, $t = -1$ with the universal fact $e^x > 0$.

## Equivalent formulations

> **Proposition.** For $f : S \to T$, the following are equivalent:
> 1. $f$ is surjective;
> 2. $f$ has a **right inverse**: some $h : T \to S$ with $f \circ h = \mathrm{id}_T$;
> 3. $f$ can be canceled from the right: for all $u, v : T \to R$, $u \circ f = v \circ f \Rightarrow u = v$;
> 4. $f(f^{-1}(U)) = U$ for all $U \subseteq T$.

<Proof title="Proof that (1) and (2) say the same thing">
(forward) Suppose $f$ is surjective. For each $t \in T$ the set $f^{-1}(\{t\})$ is nonempty; **choose** an element $h(t)$ from each. Then $f(h(t)) = t$ for all $t$, so $f \circ h = \mathrm{id}_T$.

(backward) Suppose $f \circ h = \mathrm{id}_T$ and let $t \in T$. Then $f(h(t)) = t$ with $h(t) \in S$, so $t \in \operatorname{Im}(f)$.
</Proof>

<Aside>
The word **choose** in that proof is doing more work than it appears to. Selecting one element from each of possibly infinitely many non-empty sets, with no rule specifying which, is a genuine extra assumption — and in fact "every surjection has a right inverse" is *equivalent* to AC over ZF. Contrast the corresponding fact for injections (§3.5), whose left inverse was constructed explicitly and needs no choice at all. The asymmetry is real: injectivity is constructive, surjectivity is not.
</Aside>

## Surjectivity by adjusting the codomain

Every $f : S \to T$ restricts to a surjection $S \to \operatorname{Im}(f)$, giving the standard factorisation
$$
S \to \operatorname{Im}(f) \to T
$$
of any function as a surjection followed by an injection. This split — hitting everything first, then no collisions — is the standard way to take a function apart into its two possible failures, and it comes back repeatedly in the courses ahead.

| $\sin : \mathbb{R}\to[-1,1]$ | yes | an unbroken curve hits every value in between |

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

**Surjectivity is the existence half of solving equations.** "$f$ is surjective" says $f(x)=t$ is solvable for every $t$; injectivity says the solution is unique. Nearly every existence-and-uniqueness theorem ahead is one of these two claims wearing different clothes.

**One of the first theorems in calculus is an onto statement.** It says an unbroken curve takes *every* value between its starting height and its finishing height — in this module's language, it maps onto the stretch joining them. Every root-existence argument you will run is an application: to show $x^3 - x - 1$ has a root, note the polynomial is negative at $1$ and positive at $2$, and surjectivity onto $[f(1),f(2)]$ supplies a $c$ with $f(c)=0$.

**The existence is non-constructive**, which §1.12 flagged. The theorem promises a root and produces none, and that gap is why bisection and Newton's method exist as separate topics.

**And surjectivity remains a fact about the declared codomain.** $e^x$ is surjective onto $(0,\infty)$ and not onto $\mathbb{R}$ — which is exactly why $\ln$ has domain $(0,\infty)$, and why $\ln$ of a negative number is undefined rather than merely awkward.

</WhereThisGoes>
