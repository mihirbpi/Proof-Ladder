Module 3.3 observed that $\operatorname{Im}(f) \subseteq T$ and that the containment can be strict. **Surjectivity is the condition that closes the gap.**

> $f : S \to T$ is **surjective** (or **onto**) when $\operatorname{Im}(f) = T$: every $t \in T$ has at least one $s \in S$ with $f(s) = t$.

<Figure alt="Three elements on the left with arrows landing on all three elements of the right, nothing missed." caption="Surjective: every target is hit.">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="S" labelRight="T"
    left={["a", "b", "c"]} right={["1", "2", "3"]}
    edges={[[0,2],[1,0],[2,1]]} />
</Figure>

In the language of module 1.10 the definition is
$$
\forall t \in T,\ \exists s \in S,\ f(s) = t,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\exists$ is "there is some".*

and reading the quantifiers (the *for every* and *there is* words, §1.10) tells you the proof's shape before you know anything else: **take an arbitrary $t$, then produce an $s$ that works.** The $s$ you produce is called a *witness* — an actual value exhibited, not merely argued to exist.

## A worked proof

> **Proposition.** $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = 3x + 2$ is surjective.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">An arbitrary target $y \in \mathbb{R}$, and nothing else about it.</div>
  <div slot="want">Some real $x$ with $3x + 2 = y$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Solve for $x$ in terms of $y$ on scratch paper. That computation is the <em>search</em>; the proof then presents the answer and verifies it, which is the only part that needs writing down.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $y \in \mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary. Take
$$
x = \frac{y-2}{3},
$$
which is a real number since $3 \neq 0$. Then
$$
f(x) = 3\cdot\frac{y-2}{3} + 2 = (y-2) + 2 = y.
$$

So every real is hit, and $f$ is surjective.
</Proof>

Notice the structure. **The witness depended on $y$** — as it must, since $y$ was quantified first (module 1.10). And the scratch work that found $(y-2)/3$ does not appear: a witness must be verified, not motivated.

## Failure, and the role of the codomain

> **Proposition.** $g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $g(x) = x^2$ is not surjective.

<Proof>
Take $y = -1$. For every real $x$, $x^2 \geq 0 > -1$, so no $x$ satisfies $g(x) = -1$.

So $-1 \in \mathbb{R}$ is not in the image, and $g$ is not surjective.
</Proof>

Disproving surjectivity is disproving a universal claim, so **one counterexample target** is enough (module 1.11) — but proving *that* target is missed requires an argument covering every input, which is why this proof has a "for every real $x$" in it and the injectivity disproof of module 3.5 did not.

Now change one thing:

> **Proposition.** $h : \mathbb{R} \to [0,\infty)$, $h(x) = x^2$ **is** surjective.

<Proof>
Let $y \in [0,\infty)$ be arbitrary, so $y \geq 0$. Then $\sqrt y$ is a real number, and $h(\sqrt y) = (\sqrt y)^2 = y$.
</Proof>

**Same rule. Different codomain. Different answer.**

<Warning title="Surjectivity is a property of the pair, not of the formula">
Asking "is $x^2$ onto?" is not a well-posed question. Onto *what*?

This is the sharpest illustration of module 3.1's insistence that the codomain is part of the function, and it is why that insistence was not fussiness. A property that some books state as though it were about a formula is in fact about a formula together with a declared target.

Note the general trick the last proof used: **restricting the codomain to the image makes any function surjective.** $f : S \to \operatorname{Im}(f)$ is always onto, by the definition of image. So surjectivity can always be arranged, and the interesting question is whether it holds for the codomain you actually wanted.
</Warning>

## The mirror image of module 3.5

Put the two definitions side by side and the symmetry is exact.

| | Condition | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Injective | every $t$ has **at most one** pre-image | the domain |
| Surjective | every $t$ has **at least one** pre-image | the codomain |

Both are statements about $f^{-1}(\{t\})$, the pre-image of a single element from module 3.3 — one bounds its size above, the other below.

Neither implies the other, and all four combinations occur. $3x + 2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is both. $x^2$ on $\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is neither. $x^2$ on $[0,\infty) \to \mathbb{R}$ is injective and not surjective. $x^2$ on $\mathbb{R} \to [0,\infty)$ is surjective and not injective.

Module 3.8 studies the case where both hold at once, and the table above says what that will mean: **every $t$ has exactly one pre-image.**

<Aside>
The multiplication function $m : \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$, $m(a,b) = ab$, is a good test case.

It is **not injective** — module 3.5 found $m(6,4) = m(2,12)$.

It **is surjective**: given any $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, take the pair $(n, 1)$, and $m(n,1) = n$.

One witness, chosen well, and the proof is a line. Finding the *easy* witness is a skill worth practicing — $(n,1)$ works for every $n$ at once, where hunting for factorizations of each $n$ separately would get nowhere.
</Aside>
