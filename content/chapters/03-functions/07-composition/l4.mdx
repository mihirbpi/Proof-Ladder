## Definition

Given $f : S \to T$ and $g : T \to R$, the **composition** $g \circ f : S \to R$ is defined by
$$
(g \circ f)(s) = g\big(f(s)\big).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\circ$ is "after". So the line reads: to apply *$g$ after $f$* to $s$: run $f$ first, then run $g$ on whatever comes out.*

(The notation $f(s)$ for the output on input $s$ is §3.1's.)

It is defined **only** when the codomain of $f$ equals the domain of $g$:
$$
S \;\to\; T \;\to\; R
$$

The first arrow is $f$ and the second is $g$.

That $g \circ f$ is itself a function needs no separate check: it is total because $f$ and $g$ are, single-valued for the same reason, and lands in $R$ because $g$ does.

<Warning title="Read right to left">
$g \circ f$ means "$f$ first, then $g$" — read it as "$g$ after $f$". The order matches $g(f(s))$, where the inner function is evaluated first.

Composition is not commutative. With $f(x) = x+1$ and $g(x) = 2x$ on $\mathbb{R}$:
$$(g\circ f)(x) = 2(x+1) = 2x+2, \qquad (f \circ g)(x) = 2x + 1.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\circ$ is "after". So the line reads: doing $f$ then $g$ adds first and doubles second; doing $g$ then $f$ doubles first and adds second — and the answers differ.*

Different functions. And frequently one of the two does not exist at all, because the codomains do not line up.
</Warning>

## Associativity

For $f : S \to T$, $g : T \to R$, $h : R \to Q$:
$$
h \circ (g \circ f) = (h \circ g) \circ f,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\circ$ is "after". So the line reads: grouping three functions either way gives the same function.*

since both send $s$ to $h(g(f(s)))$. So $h \circ g \circ f$ needs no parentheses. Together with the identity functions $\mathrm{id}_S(s) = s$, which satisfy $f \circ \mathrm{id}_S = f = \mathrm{id}_T \circ f$, composition gives functions the structure of an algebraic system in their own right.

## Composition preserves injectivity

> **Proposition.** If $f : S \to T$ and $g : T \to R$ are both injective, so is $g \circ f$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Injectivity in the usable form (module 3.5): $f(s_1) = f(s_2) \Rightarrow s_1 = s_2$, and likewise for $g$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$(g\circ f)(s_1) = (g\circ f)(s_2) \Rightarrow s_1 = s_2$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Unfold the composition. The hypothesis becomes $g(f(s_1)) = g(f(s_2))$. Apply injectivity of $g$ to the arguments $f(s_1)$ and $f(s_2)$, which gives $f(s_1) = f(s_2)$; then apply injectivity of $f$. The two hypotheses get used in turn, outermost first.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $s_1, s_2 \in S$ with $(g \circ f)(s_1) = (g \circ f)(s_2)$, i.e. $g(f(s_1)) = g(f(s_2))$.

Since $f(s_1), f(s_2) \in T$ and $g$ is injective, $f(s_1) = f(s_2)$.

Since $f$ is injective, $s_1 = s_2$.

So $g \circ f$ is injective.
</Proof>

## Composition preserves surjectivity

> **Proposition.** If $f : S \to T$ and $g : T \to R$ are both surjective, so is $g \circ f$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Every $r \in R$ has a $g$-pre-image in $T$; every $t \in T$ has an $f$-pre-image in $S$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Every $r \in R$ has a $(g\circ f)$-pre-image in $S$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Start from a single $r$, assuming nothing about it, and pull back twice. This time the hypotheses are used innermost last: $g$ first to reach $T$, then $f$ to reach $S$ — the reverse of the injectivity proof, which is worth noticing.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Take any $r \in R$. Since $g$ is surjective, there is $t \in T$ with $g(t) = r$. Since $f$ is surjective, there is $s \in S$ with $f(s) = t$.

Then $(g \circ f)(s) = g(f(s)) = g(t) = r$.

Nothing was assumed about $r$, so $g \circ f$ is surjective.
</Proof>

Combining the two: **a composition of bijections is a bijection** (module 3.8).

## The partial converses

The converses fail, but something weaker is true and worth sitting with.

Suppose $g \circ f$ is injective. Must $f$ be? Must $g$?

Try $S = \{a\}$, $T = \{1,2\}$, $R = \{x\}$ with $f(a) = 1$ and $g(1) = g(2) = x$. Then $g \circ f$ is injective (its domain has one element!) but $g$ is not.

What is true: **if $g \circ f$ is injective then $f$ is injective**. (If $f(s_1) = f(s_2)$, apply $g$ to both sides to get $(g\circ f)(s_1) = (g \circ f)(s_2)$, so $s_1 = s_2$.)

Dually: **if $g \circ f$ is surjective then $g$ is surjective**, and $f$ need not be.

The pattern is worth remembering: the composite's injectivity constrains the *first* function; its surjectivity constrains the *second*.

<Aside>
Composition is the reason functions form a *structure* rather than a collection. Bijections from a set to itself, under composition, form a **group** — the symmetric group — with the identity function as the identity element and inverses supplied by module 3.9. The associativity proved above is one of the group axioms, free of charge.
</Aside>
