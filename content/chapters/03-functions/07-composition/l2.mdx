Run one machine. Take its answer. Feed it straight into a second machine. That is a **machine chain**.

## Trace it through

<Figure alt="Three children on the left with arrows to three cubbies in the middle." caption="Machine one: child to cubby.">

<Figure alt="Three cubbies on the left with arrows to colors on the right; cubby 1 goes to red, cubby 2 to blue, cubby 3 to green." caption="Machine two: cubby to color.">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="cubbies" labelRight="colors"
    left={["1", "2", "3"]} right={["red", "blue", "green"]}
    edges={[[0,0],[1,1],[2,2]]} />
</Figure>

Now chain them.

- Sam → cubby 3 → **green**.
- Pip → cubby 1 → **red**.
- Ada → cubby 2 → **blue**.

The chain is one new machine: child in, colour out. You never have to mention cubbies at all once you have traced it through.

## The middle has to match

Here is the one rule you must obey.

The second machine takes **cubbies**. So the first machine had better hand it cubbies.

If machine one gave out colors and machine two only takes cubbies, the chain breaks. The second machine would not know what to do with a color.

So: **what comes out of the first must be what goes into the second.** Check the middle before you chain.

## Order matters

Chaining is not like adding. Swapping the machines gives a completely different chain — and usually it does not work at all.

Try it: cubby → color → ...? Now we would need a machine that takes colors. We do not have one. Chain broken!

<Warning title="Which one goes first?">
When you write down a chain, mathematicians write the *last* machine on the left. It looks backwards, and it is: you read it right to left, the way you read the inside of a bracket first.

You will meet that notation properly in module 3.7 at higher levels. For now, just remember: the machine that runs first is the one nearest the thing going in.
</Warning>
