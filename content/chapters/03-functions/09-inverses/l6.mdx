Reflect a figure twice in the same line and it comes back. Rotate $90°$ then $-90°$ and nothing has moved. Every rigid motion can be undone, and this module says exactly which functions have that property and why.

## Definition

$g : T\to S$ is an **inverse** of $f : S\to T$ when

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\circ$ reads "after".*

$$
g\circ f = \operatorname{id}_S \qquad\text{and}\qquad f\circ g = \operatorname{id}_T.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\circ$ is "after".*

In elements: $g(f(s))=s$ for every $s\in S$, and $f(g(t))=t$ for every $t\in T$.

**Both equations are required.** One alone gives a one-sided inverse, which is a weaker and genuinely different thing.

## The theorem

> **Theorem.** $f : S\to T$ has an inverse if and only if $f$ is a bijection.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Bijection means $\forall t\,\exists!\,s$ with $f(s)=t$ (module 3.8).</div>
  <div slot="want">Both directions — this is an if-and-only-if statement, so module 1.9 applies.</div>
  <div slot="how">Forward: the $\exists!$ is exactly what is needed to *define* $g$, since a definition requires existence and uniqueness of the output. Backward: assume $g$ exists and use each equation to get one property.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**(backward)** Suppose $f$ is a bijection. For each $t\in T$ there is exactly one $s\in S$ with $f(s)=t$; define $g(t)$ to be that $s$.

This is a legitimate definition: surjectivity gives at least one such $s$, so $g$ is total, and injectivity gives at most one, so $g$ is single-valued.

Now $g(f(s))$ is the unique element mapping to $f(s)$, which is $s$; and $f(g(t))=t$ by construction. So $g$ is an inverse.

**(forward)** Suppose $g$ is an inverse of $f$.

*Injective:* if $f(s_1)=f(s_2)$, apply $g$ to both sides: $g(f(s_1))=g(f(s_2))$, so $s_1 = s_2$.

*Surjective:* let $t\in T$. Then $f\big(g(t)\big) = t$, so $g(t)$ is a pre-image of $t$.

So $f$ is a bijection.
</Proof>

**The forward direction is where the definition of bijection earns its keep.** Existence and uniqueness are not two nice properties that happen to travel together — they are exactly the two conditions a rule must satisfy to be a function at all (module 3.1). Surjectivity makes $g$ total; injectivity makes it single-valued.

## The inverse is unique

> **Proposition.** A function has at most one inverse.

<Proof>
Suppose $g_1$ and $g_2$ are both inverses of $f$. Let $t \in T$ be arbitrary.

Since $f\circ g_1 = \operatorname{id}_T$, we have $f\big(g_1(t)\big) = t$, and likewise $f\big(g_2(t)\big)=t$.

So $f\big(g_1(t)\big) = f\big(g_2(t)\big)$. By the theorem $f$ is injective, so $g_1(t)=g_2(t)$.

Since $t$ was arbitrary, $g_1 = g_2$.
</Proof>

That is module 1.12's uniqueness template — suppose two, show they are one — and it is what licenses the notation **$f^{-1}$**, with a definite article. Without uniqueness, "the inverse" would be an unjustified phrase.

<Warning title="Two meanings of f⁻¹">
$f^{-1}(B)$ for a **set** $B$ is the pre-image (module 3.3). Defined for every function, always.

$f^{-1}(t)$ for an **element** $t$ is the inverse function. Defined only when $f$ is a bijection.

For $f(x)=x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$: the pre-image $f^{-1}(\{4\})=\{2,-2\}$ is fine, and $f^{-1}(4)$ as an inverse value does not exist, because $f$ is not injective.

The collision is unfortunate and permanent. Read the argument: a set means pre-image, an element means inverse.
</Warning>

## Rigid motions

Every rigid motion is a bijection (module 3.8), so every rigid motion has an inverse — and the inverses are the ones you would guess:

| Motion | Inverse |
|---|---|
| Reflection in $\ell$ | itself |
| Rotation through $\theta$ about $C$ | rotation through $-\theta$ about $C$ |
| Translation by $(a,b)$ | translation by $(-a,-b)$ |
| Dilation by $k \neq 0$ about $C$ | dilation by $1/k$ about $C$ |

A reflection is **its own inverse**, which is why reflecting twice in the same line returns every point. Compare $\overline{\overline S}=S$ for complements (module 2.5) — the same phenomenon.

The general rule for compositions reverses the order:

$$
(g\circ f)^{-1} = f^{-1}\circ g^{-1}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\circ$ is "after".*

Socks then shoes; to undo, shoes off then socks off. Applied to a congruence proved by "reflect, then translate," the reverse congruence is "translate back, then reflect back" — in that order, and getting it backwards produces a different motion.

## Why congruence is symmetric

Your book says that $F\cong G$ implies $G \cong F$. Here is the proof.

<Proof>
Suppose $F\cong G$, so there is a rigid motion $f$ with $f(F)=G$.

By module 3.8, $f$ is a bijection, so $f^{-1}$ exists. It is again a rigid motion: for any $P, Q$,
$$d\big(f^{-1}(P), f^{-1}(Q)\big) = d\Big(f\big(f^{-1}(P)\big), f\big(f^{-1}(Q)\big)\Big) = d(P,Q),$$
using that $f$ preserves distance and $f\circ f^{-1} = \operatorname{id}$.

And $f^{-1}(G) = f^{-1}\big(f(F)\big) = F$. So $G\cong F$.
</Proof>

Three chapters of machinery, and a property your textbook takes for granted is now a theorem.

## Restricting to gain an inverse

Squaring is not injective on the reals, so it has no inverse: $3$ and $-3$ both square to $9$. Restrict its domain to $[0,\infty)$ and its codomain to $[0,\infty)$, and the restricted function is a bijection — its inverse is the square root.

That is why a calculator returns $3$ and not $-3$ for $\sqrt9$, even though both square to $9$, and why solving $x^2 = 9$ requires you to put the second solution back by hand. The restriction that created the function is also what removed them.

<Aside>
The two equations $g\circ f = \operatorname{id}_S$ and $f\circ g = \operatorname{id}_T$ can hold separately.

A **left inverse** ($g\circ f = \operatorname{id}_S$) exists exactly when $f$ is injective. A **right inverse** ($f\circ g = \operatorname{id}_T$) exists exactly when $f$ is surjective. Both together give the bijection.

The projection $\pi(x,y)=(x,0)$ has a right inverse — the inclusion of the $x$-axis into the plane — and no left inverse, since it merges. That matches the intuition: you can always lift a shadow back to *some* solid, but never to the one it came from.
</Aside>
