Some machines can be run backwards. That backwards version is called an **undo-machine**.

## When it works

<Figure alt="Three children on the left, each with one arrow, landing on three different cubbies so every cubby is used exactly once." caption="A perfect pairing. This one can be undone.">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="children" labelRight="cubbies"
    left={["Sam", "Pip", "Ada"]} right={["1", "2", "3"]}
    edges={[[0,1],[1,2],[2,0]]} />
</Figure>

Turn every arrow around. Now put in a cubby and get out a child.

Cubby 2 → Sam. Cubby 3 → Pip. Cubby 1 → Ada.

It works! And notice: turning the arrows around gave us another perfectly good machine.

## Two ways it can fail

**Failure one: sharing.**

<Figure alt="Sam and Pip both have arrows landing on cubby 2; cubby 3 is unused." caption="Sam and Pip share cubby 2. Turn the arrows around and cubby 2 has two answers.">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="children" labelRight="cubbies"
    left={["Sam", "Pip", "Ada"]} right={["1", "2", "3"]}
    edges={[[0,1],[1,1],[2,0]]} />
</Figure>

Turn the arrows around and cubby 2 now points at *two* children. But a machine must give exactly **one** answer. Broken.

**Failure two: something left out.**

Look at that same picture. Cubby 3 has no arrow at all. Turn it around and cubby 3 points at nobody. But a machine must work for *everything* on its list. Broken again.

## The rule

> An **undo-machine** exists only when the first machine never shared and never left anything out.

Both. Not one or the other. And that is exactly the perfect pairing from module 3.8.

<Warning title="Undo twice and you are back">
Take a machine you can undo. Undo it. Now undo *that*.

You are back where you started! Undoing the undo-machine gives you the original machine. Try it on the picture above and see.
</Warning>

## Undo with numbers

The doubling machine, going from whole numbers to even numbers, is a perfect pairing. So it has an undo-machine.

What is it? **Halving.** Put in $10$, get $5$. Put in $6$, get $3$.

And check: double $5$ is $10$, then halve $10$ and you are back to $5$. Undone.
