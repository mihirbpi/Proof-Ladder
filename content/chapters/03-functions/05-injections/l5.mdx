Module 3.1 forbade one input going to two outputs. It said nothing about two inputs going to one output. **Injectivity is the condition that forbids that too.**

> $f : S \to T$ is **injective** (or **one-to-one**) when distinct inputs give distinct outputs:
> $$ s_1 \neq s_2 \Rightarrow f(s_1) \neq f(s_2). $$

## Turn the statement around first

The definition as stated is nearly unusable. Its hypothesis is $s_1 \neq s_2$, and a *difference* gives you nothing to compute with — you cannot subtract two things you only know are unequal.

So use module 1.8 and flip it:

$$
f(s_1) = f(s_2) \Rightarrow s_1 = s_2.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

Same statement, and now the hypothesis is an **equation**, which is something algebra can act on. **This is the form every proof uses**, and if you find yourself stuck on an injectivity proof, check first that you started from the right end.

## A worked proof

> **Proposition.** $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = 3x + 2$ is injective.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Nothing about $x_1$ and $x_2$ except that their outputs agree.</div>
  <div slot="want">$x_1 = x_2$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Set the outputs equal and solve. Each algebraic step must be reversible in principle, but here we only need the forward direction.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Suppose $f(x_1) = f(x_2)$ for some $x_1, x_2 \in \mathbb{R}$. Then
$$
3x_1 + 2 = 3x_2 + 2.
$$
Subtracting $2$: $3x_1 = 3x_2$. Dividing by $3$, which is nonzero: $x_1 = x_2$.

So $f$ is injective.
</Proof>

Note where the hypothesis $3 \neq 0$ was used. A function $f(x) = 0x + 2$ is constant, and constant functions on a domain with more than one element are as far from injective as possible.

## A function that is not injective

> **Proposition.** $g : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $g(x) = x^2$ is not injective.

<Proof>
$g(-2) = 4 = g(2)$, and $-2 \neq 2$.
</Proof>

That is a complete proof. Non-injectivity is an existence claim — *there exist two distinct inputs with equal outputs* — so module 1.12's witness template applies, and one pair settles it.

**Never argue in general to disprove injectivity.** Exhibit the collision.

The same move works on the multiplication function of module 3.1: $m : \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$, $m(a,b) = ab$ has
$$
m(6, 4) = 24 = m(2, 12),
$$
and $(6,4) \neq (2,12)$ since ordered pairs compare coordinate by coordinate. Not injective.

## The domain is part of the answer

$g(x) = x^2$ fails to be injective on $\mathbb{R}$. Restrict the domain to $[0,\infty)$ and it succeeds.

<Proof>
Let $h : [0,\infty) \to \mathbb{R}$, $h(x) = x^2$, and suppose $h(x_1) = h(x_2)$, so $x_1^2 = x_2^2$.

Then $x_1^2 - x_2^2 = 0$, so $(x_1 - x_2)(x_1 + x_2) = 0$, and so $x_1 = x_2$ or $x_1 = -x_2$.

In the second case, both $x_1, x_2 \geq 0$ and $x_1 = -x_2$ force $x_1 = x_2 = 0$, so $x_1 = x_2$ regardless.

So $h$ is injective.
</Proof>

**Same rule, different domain, different answer** — module 3.1's insistence that the domain is part of the function, now with consequences.

<Warning title="Injectivity is about the domain; surjectivity is about the codomain">
Both properties depend on more than the formula, and they depend on *different* parts of it.

- Shrinking the domain can only help injectivity — fewer inputs, fewer chances to collide.
- Changing the codomain cannot affect injectivity at all, since the condition never mentions $T$ beyond where outputs live.

Module 3.6 has the mirror image: surjectivity depends entirely on the codomain and is untouched by shrinking the domain's image.
</Warning>

## The graphical test

For $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, injectivity is the **horizontal line test**: no horizontal line meets the graph twice.

Compare module 3.1. The vertical line test checks that something is a function; the horizontal line test checks that it is injective. Two different tests, two different conditions, and the pairing is worth holding onto — *vertical for function, horizontal for one-to-one*.

$y = x^2$ passes the vertical test and fails the horizontal one at every height above $0$, which is the graph saying exactly what the algebra said.

<Aside>
Injectivity is what makes undoing possible.

If $f$ is injective and you are told $f(x) = 7$, there is at most one $x$ it could have been. If $f$ is not injective, the output has genuinely lost information and no amount of cleverness recovers it.

That is why module 3.9's inverse functions require injectivity, and it is why $\sqrt{\ }$ is defined only on $[0,\infty)$ — squaring is injective there and nowhere larger.
</Aside>
