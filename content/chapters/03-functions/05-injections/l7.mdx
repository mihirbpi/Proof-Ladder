$f : S \to T$ is **injective** when
$$
\forall s_1, s_2 \in S,\ s_1 \neq s_2 \Rightarrow f(s_1) \neq f(s_2),
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

equivalently, by contraposition,
$$
\forall s_1, s_2 \in S,\ f(s_1) = f(s_2) \Rightarrow s_1 = s_2. \tag{I}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\forall$ is "for every", $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

Form (I) is the working definition. The reason is §1.8's decision rule: the first version has a negation as hypothesis and supplies nothing to compute with, while (I) hands you an equation. Every injectivity proof in mathematics opens by assuming $f(s_1) = f(s_2)$.

Equivalently again, said with pre-images (§3.3): $|f^{-1}(\{t\})| \leq 1$ for all $t \in T$.

## Proving injectivity

> **Proposition.** $\exp : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is injective.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$e^a = e^b$. Available: $\exp$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb{R}$, a consequence of $\exp' = \exp > 0$.</div>
  <div slot="want">$a = b$.</div>
  <div slot="how">Strictly increasing means bigger inputs always give bigger outputs, which forbids equal values at distinct points. Proving the general lemma is no harder than the instance, so prove the lemma.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof title="Proof (general lemma)">
Let $f : I \to \mathbb{R}$ be strictly increasing on an interval $I$, and suppose $f(a) = f(b)$ with $a, b \in I$. Any two reals compare in exactly one of three ways, so either $a < b$, $a = b$, or $a > b$. If $a < b$ then $f(a) < f(b)$, contradicting equality; if $a > b$ then $f(a) > f(b)$, likewise. Hence $a = b$, so $f$ is injective.

Applying this to $\exp$, which is strictly increasing since $\exp' = \exp > 0$ everywhere, gives injectivity of $\exp$.
</Proof>

Being strictly increasing — bigger inputs always giving bigger outputs — is the standard route to injectivity for real functions, and the derivative test ($f' > 0$ throughout an interval) is the standard route to being strictly increasing. Note the implication is one-way: $f(x) = x^3$ is injective with $f'(0) = 0$, so $f' > 0$ everywhere is sufficient but not necessary.

## Disproof is cheap

Injectivity is made about every element, so one collision refutes it (§1.11):

| $f$ | Collision |
|---|---|
| $x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ | $f(3) = f(-3)$ |
| $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$ | $\lfloor 3.1\rfloor = \lfloor 3.9\rfloor$ |
| $m(a,b) = ab$ on $\mathbb{Z}^2$ | $m(6,4) = m(2,12)$ |
| $\sin$ on $\mathbb{R}$ | $\sin 0 = \sin \pi$ |

Always spend a moment hunting for a collision before attempting a proof.

<Warning title="Injectivity is a property of the function, hence of its domain">
$x \mapsto x^2$ is not injective on $\mathbb{R}$ but is injective on $[0,\infty)$: if $a,b \geq 0$ and $a^2 = b^2$ then $(a-b)(a+b) = 0$, and $a+b = 0$ with $a,b \geq 0$ forces $a = b = 0$; otherwise $a = b$.

Since domain and codomain are part of the function (§3.1), this is not a change of answer but a change of question. It is also exactly why $\sqrt{\phantom{x}}$ is defined as the inverse of squaring restricted to $[0,\infty)$.
</Warning>

## Equivalent formulations

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\circ$ reads "after".*

> **Proposition.** For $f : S \to T$ with $S \neq \emptyset$, the following are equivalent:
> 1. $f$ is injective;
> 2. $f$ has a **left inverse**: some $g : T \to S$ with $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_S$;
> 3. $f$ can be canceled from the left: for all $h, k : R \to S$, $f \circ h = f \circ k \Rightarrow h = k$;
> 4. $f(A \cap B) = f(A) \cap f(B)$ for all $A, B \subseteq S$;
> 5. $f^{-1}(f(A)) = A$ for all $A \subseteq S$.

<Proof title="Proof that (1) and (2) say the same thing">
(forward) Suppose $f$ is injective. Fix any $s_0 \in S$ (possible since $S \neq \emptyset$) and define
$$
g(t) = \begin{cases} \text{the unique } s \text{ with } f(s) = t, & t \in \operatorname{Im}(f),\\ s_0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in".*

Uniqueness — hence well-definedness of $g$ — is exactly injectivity. For $s \in S$ we have $g(f(s)) = s$, so $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_S$.

(backward) Suppose $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}_S$ and $f(s_1) = f(s_2)$. Applying $g$: $s_1 = g(f(s_1)) = g(f(s_2)) = s_2$.
</Proof>

Items (4) and (5) were flagged in §3.4: all the misbehavior of the image operator is exactly the failure of injectivity. Item (3) is the formulation that survives when elements are not available to chase, and it is the version that survives when "elements" are unavailable.

The hypothesis $S \neq \emptyset$ in (2) is needed only for the trivial reason that a left inverse must be defined somewhere; the empty function $\emptyset \to T$ is injective but has no left inverse when $T \neq \emptyset$.

<Aside>
Injectivity is what "no information is lost" means precisely. An injective $f$ can be undone on its image, so $s$ is recoverable from $f(s)$. This is why injectivity is the condition for an encoding to be decodable, for a hash function to be collision-free, and for a change of variables to be legitimate.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Injectivity is the condition under which an equation has at most one solution, and both courses ahead have a test for it.

**One vector's worth of checking replaces a quantified statement about all pairs.** That is a genuine simplification, and it is available only because linearity lets you subtract — which is why the same trick works for no other kind of function.

**In calculus, being strictly increasing gives injectivity.** If $f' > 0$ throughout an interval then $f$ is strictly increasing there, hence injective, hence invertible on that interval — which is exactly the hypothesis of the inverse-function results and the reason $\sin$ must be restricted to $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$ before $\sin^{-1}$ exists.

The horizontal line test is the graphical form, and the derivative test is the computable one. Note the direction: $f'>0$ implies injective, and the converse fails — $f(x)=x^3$ is injective with $f'(0)=0$. A one-way implication, and §1.7's warning applies.

</WhereThisGoes>
