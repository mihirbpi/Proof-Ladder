A **machine** takes each thing on one list and sends it to exactly one thing on another list.

Two rules, and that is the whole idea:

1. Everybody on the first list goes somewhere.
2. Nobody goes to two places.

The best way to draw one is an **arrow diagram**. Put the first list on the left, the second on the right, and draw an arrow from each thing to where it goes.

## A good machine

<Figure alt="Three children Sam, Pip and Ada on the left, three cubbies 1, 2 and 3 on the right, with exactly one arrow leaving each child." caption="Every child leaves. Each one has exactly one arrow.">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="children" labelRight="cubbies"
    left={["Sam", "Pip", "Ada"]} right={["1", "2", "3"]}
    edges={[[0,1],[1,0],[2,2]]} />
</Figure>

Check it: every child on the left has an arrow leaving. And nobody has two arrows. Good machine!

Now you can ask it questions. *Where does Pip go?* Follow Pip's arrow. Cubby $1$. There is always an answer, and there is only ever one — that is what the two rules bought you.

## Broken machine one: somebody got skipped

<Figure alt="Three children on the left but Ada has no arrow leaving her; the other two have one arrow each." caption="Ada has no cubby. Broken!">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="children" labelRight="cubbies"
    left={["Sam", "Pip", "Ada"]} right={["1", "2", "3"]}
    edges={[[0,1],[1,0]]} />
</Figure>

Ada has nowhere to put her coat. A machine has to work for *everybody* on the first list.

## Broken machine two: two arrows

<Figure alt="Sam has two arrows leaving, going to two different cubbies; the second one is drawn dashed to mark it as the problem." caption="Sam has two cubbies. Which one? Broken!">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="children" labelRight="cubbies"
    left={["Sam", "Pip", "Ada"]} right={["1", "2", "3"]}
    edges={[[0,0],[0,1],[1,2],[2,1]]} broken={[1]} />
</Figure>

Now nobody knows where Sam's boots go. A machine has to give **one** answer.

## Not broken: sharing

<Figure alt="Sam and Pip both have arrows landing on cubby 2, while Ada has her own; every child still has exactly one arrow leaving." caption="Two arrows land on the same cubby. That is allowed!">
  <ArrowDiagram labelLeft="children" labelRight="cubbies"
    left={["Sam", "Pip", "Ada"]} right={["1", "2", "3"]}
    edges={[[0,1],[1,1],[2,0]]} />
</Figure>

This one *looks* wrong but it is fine. The rule is about arrows **leaving**, not arrows landing. Every child still has exactly one cubby. They just have to squeeze.

<Warning title="Check the left, not the right">
To test a machine, look only at the left list. Does every thing have an arrow leaving? Is it exactly one? Then you are done. What happens on the right is a different question, and it gets its own module later on.
</Warning>

## Machines are everywhere

Once you know the shape, you start seeing it:

- Every kid → their birthday.
- Every book on the shelf → how many pages it has.
- Every number → that number plus one.

Each one sends everybody somewhere, and each one gives exactly one answer. All three are machines.
