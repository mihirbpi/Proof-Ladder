§3.1's definition mentions neither formulas nor numbers. This module exercises that generality, because the tacit identification of *function* with *expression* is the single most limiting misconception a student brings into a proofs course.

## Floor

$$
\lfloor \cdot \rfloor : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{Z}, \qquad \lfloor x \rfloor = \max\{ n \in \mathbb{Z} \mid n \leq x \}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that", the arrow says what goes in and what comes out. So the line reads: floor takes a real number and returns the largest integer not above it.*

That the maximum exists — that $\{n \in \mathbb{Z} \mid n \leq x\}$ is nonempty and bounded above, and that a nonempty set of integers bounded above has a greatest element — is the fact that the whole numbers climb past any real, together with there always being a smallest one. Floor is not a triviality; it depends on the reals having no gaps.

The working characterisation:
$$
\lfloor x \rfloor = n \iff n \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ and } n \leq x < n+1.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when".*

From it: $\lfloor x + m \rfloor = \lfloor x \rfloor + m$ for $m \in \mathbb{Z}$; $x - 1 < \lfloor x \rfloor \leq x$; and $\lfloor -2.3 \rfloor = -3$, not $-2$. Floor is not truncation, and conflating them is a standard source of off-by-one errors.

Floor is neither injective ($\lfloor 3.1 \rfloor = \lfloor 3.9 \rfloor$) nor continuous, but it is surjective onto $\mathbb{Z}$ and monotone — an illustration that useful functions need not be well behaved.

## Euler's totient

$$
\operatorname{tot}(n) = \big|\{ k \in \mathbb{Z} \mid 1 \leq k \leq n,\ \gcd(k,n)=1 \}\big|.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: count how many of $1$ through $n$ share no factor with $n$.*

Values: $\operatorname{tot}(1)=1$, $\operatorname{tot}(6)=2$, $\operatorname{tot}(8)=4$, $\operatorname{tot}(9)=6$, $\operatorname{tot}(12)=4$.

> **Proposition.** $\operatorname{tot}(p) = p-1$ for $p$ prime.

<Proof>
Let $p$ be prime and let $1 \leq k \leq p$. If $k < p$ then $\gcd(k,p)$ divides $p$, so it is $1$ or $p$; it cannot be $p$ since $p \nmid k$ — read *"$p$ does not divide $k$"* — for $0 < k < p$. Hence $\gcd(k,p) = 1$, and all $p-1$ such $k$ are counted. Finally $\gcd(p,p) = p \neq 1$, so $k = p$ is not counted. Therefore $\operatorname{tot}(p) = p-1$.
</Proof>

$\operatorname{tot}$ is *multiplicative*: $\operatorname{tot}(mn) = \operatorname{tot}(m)\operatorname{tot}(n)$ when $\gcd(m,n)=1$, which with $\operatorname{tot}(p^k) = p^k - p^{k-1}$ yields
$$
\operatorname{tot}(n) = n \prod_{p \mid n}\left(1 - \frac1p\right).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

Note that this "formula" presupposes the factorisation of $n$; it is not an evaluation procedure. The gap between *characterised* and *computable* is exactly where cryptography lives.

## Functions on products, and currying

$m : \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$, $m(a,b) = ab$, is a function whose domain is a product set (§2.9). Treating binary operations as functions is what lets §3.5–3.6 apply to them: $m$ fails injectivity ($m(6,4)=m(2,12)$) and satisfies surjectivity ($m(n,1)=n$).

Equivalently, by currying, $m$ corresponds to $\hat m : \mathbb{Z} \to (\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z})$ sending $a$ to multiplication-by-$a$. The two views are interchangeable, and the second is how one sees at a glance that $\hat m(0)$ is the constant zero map while $\hat m(a)$ for $a \neq 0$ is injective.

## Domain restrictions are theorems

$$
r : \mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\} \to \mathbb{R},\ r(x)=1/x;
\qquad
\ell : \mathbb{R}_{>0} \to \mathbb{R},\ \ell(x) = \ln x.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, the arrow says what goes in and what comes out. So the line reads: each excludes exactly the inputs where its rule would fail.*

The excluded points are not conventions. There is no real $y$ with $0 \cdot y = 1$, so $1/0$ names nothing; and $\exp(\mathbb{R}) = \mathbb{R}_{>0}$, so $\ln x$ has no value for $x \leq 0$. In each case the domain is the largest set on which totality holds, and identifying it is a small theorem.

<Warning title="Well-definedness on quotients">
When the domain has elements with several names, a rule must be checked to be name-independent.

- **Not a function.** $f(p/q) = p+q$ on $\mathbb{Q}$: $\tfrac12 = \tfrac24$ but $3 \neq 6$.
- **A function.** $g : \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, $g([a]) = [a^2]$, because $a \equiv b \pmod n$ implies $a^2 \equiv b^2 \pmod n$.
- **Not a function.** $h : \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{Z}$, $h([a]) = a$: no one name is the official one, so the "value" depends on the name.

Every definition on objects with more than one name — remainder arithmetic, the fractions built as pairs of integers (§4.5), and every later construction that declares two things equal — carries this obligation, and it is the step first-time provers most often skip.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Functions can act on functions. Differentiation $D : C^1(\mathbb{R}) \to C^0(\mathbb{R})$ and definite integration $I : C^0([a,b]) \to \mathbb{R}$ are functions whose domains are sets of functions. Nothing in §3.1 forbids this, and the whole of functional analysis lives there.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">
**A sequence is a function $\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}$.** Writing $a_n$ instead of $a(n)$ is notation, not a different kind of object — which is why sequences have limits, why they can be monotone or bounded, and why the theorems about sequences and about functions look so alike. Recognising the identification early saves relearning the same material twice.

**Piecewise definitions are the norm, not the exception.** $|x|$, the floor function, and every function defined by cases require the two obligations this module names: exhaustive cases and agreement where they overlap. Continuity at a boundary between pieces is then a genuine question, and it is exactly where the standard exam problems live.

**Functions with no formula are common in calculus.** $F(x) = \int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt$ is a perfectly good function, differentiable everywhere, with no expression in terms of elementary functions. So is the solution of a differential equation whose closed form does not exist. Insisting a function be a formula would exclude both, and much of the subject with them.

That last example is where the abstraction pays. **Nothing in §3.1 required inputs to be numbers**, so the whole of Chapters 3's machinery — injective, surjective, invertible, composition — applies to differentiation, and it is genuinely useful there.


</WhereThisGoes>
