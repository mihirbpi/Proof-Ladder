Machines do not all look alike. What makes something a machine is *not* what it does — it is that every thing you put in gets exactly one answer out.

## The first-letter machine

Put in a name, get its first letter.

Sam → S. Pip → P. Ada → A.

Every name has exactly one first letter, so this is a machine. And notice: not a single number in sight.

## The alphabet-place machine

Put in a letter, get its place in the alphabet.

a → 1. b → 2. c → 3. And so on, up to z → 26.

Numbers on the right, letters on the left. Machines are allowed to change what kind of thing they are dealing with.

## The chop-it-down machine

This one is about money, and you have used it your whole life.

You have $\$3$ and $75$ cents. How many whole dollars? **3**. You just **chop it down** to the whole number below.

$\$7$ and $10$ cents → $7$.
$\$12$ and $99$ cents → $12$.
$\$5$ and $0$ cents → $5$.

You never round up. You chop. $\$12$ and $99$ cents is still only $12$ whole dollars, even though it is so close.

Every amount of money gives exactly one whole-dollar answer, so this is a machine too.

<Aside>
Grown-up mathematicians use the chop-it-down machine constantly, and they have a name for it. You will meet the name in a couple of years. The idea is exactly the one you just learned with dollars.
</Aside>

## What they have in common

Nothing! One works on names, one on letters, one on money. They look completely different.

But all three pass the same test: put a thing in, get exactly one thing out, every time.

That is the only thing a machine has to do.

<TryIt>
Try the chop-it-down machine on 4 different amounts of money. Then find one where you *want* to round up and do not. That is the rule!
</TryIt>
