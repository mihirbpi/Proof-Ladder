Pre-images pass through every operation of Chapter 2 unchanged. For $f : S \to T$ and $U, V \subseteq T$:

$$
f^{-1}(U \cup V) = f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V)
$$
$$
f^{-1}(U \cap V) = f^{-1}(U) \cap f^{-1}(V)
$$
$$
f^{-1}\big(\overline{U}\big) = \overline{f^{-1}(U)}
$$

Three theorems, and **one proof technique repeated three times.** The pivot is always the same line:

$$
x \in f^{-1}(U) \iff f(x) \in U.
$$

That is just the definition of pre-image, and it is a **biconditional** — which is why these proofs come out so cleanly. A biconditional runs in both directions at once, so the ($\subseteq$) and ($\supseteq$) halves of each double inclusion are the same computation read forwards and backwards.

## The union law

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The pivot equivalence, and the definition $x \in A \cup B \iff x \in A \vee x \in B$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion (module 2.6).</div>
  <div slot="how">Unfold the left side one step at a time until it reads as a statement about $\vee$, then fold that back up into the right side. Each step is an equivalence, so both directions come out together.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in f^{-1}(U \cup V)$ be arbitrary. By the pivot, $f(x) \in U \cup V$, so $f(x) \in U$ or $f(x) \in V$.

*Case 1: $f(x) \in U$.* Then $x \in f^{-1}(U)$.

*Case 2: $f(x) \in V$.* Then $x \in f^{-1}(V)$.

Either way $x \in f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V)$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V)$ be arbitrary, so $x \in f^{-1}(U)$ or $x \in f^{-1}(V)$.

In the first case $f(x) \in U$; in the second $f(x) \in V$. Either way $f(x) \in U \cup V$, so $x \in f^{-1}(U \cup V)$.

Both inclusions hold.
</Proof>

The case splits appeared because a disjunction sat in each hypothesis — module 1.12's rule, showing up again. And the two directions are mirror images, because every step was reversible.

## The complement law

This is the one that would fail if pre-images were badly behaved, so it is the one worth doing carefully. Complements are taken in $T$ on the left and in $S$ on the right.

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in f^{-1}(\overline{U})$ be arbitrary. By the pivot, $f(x) \in \overline{U}$, so $f(x) \notin U$.

Now suppose $x \in f^{-1}(U)$. That would give $f(x) \in U$, which we just ruled out. So $x \notin f^{-1}(U)$, i.e. $x \in \overline{f^{-1}(U)}$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in \overline{f^{-1}(U)}$ be arbitrary, so $x \notin f^{-1}(U)$. By the pivot, $f(x) \notin U$ — for if $f(x)$ were in $U$, then $x$ would be in $f^{-1}(U)$.

So $f(x) \in \overline{U}$, giving $x \in f^{-1}(\overline{U})$.

Both inclusions hold.
</Proof>

Each direction used **suppose not** in miniature (module 2.5): the goal was a negative statement, so the route was to assume the positive and find it impossible.

Note also that totality is quietly doing work. $f(x)$ exists for *every* $x \in S$, so every element of $S$ is either in $f^{-1}(U)$ or in $f^{-1}(\overline U)$, with no leftovers. A rule that failed to be total would break this law.

## Images do not behave this way

<Warning title="The image only manages union">
The same three laws for the *forward* image are not all true.

**Union — true.** $f(A \cup B) = f(A) \cup f(B)$ always.

**Intersection — false.** Take $f(x) = x^2$, $A = \{-1\}$, $B = \{1\}$:
$$
A \cap B = \emptyset, \text{ so } f(A \cap B) = \emptyset,
$$
but $f(A) = f(B) = \{1\}$, so $f(A) \cap f(B) = \{1\}$.

The general fact is only $f(A \cap B) \subseteq f(A) \cap f(B)$.

**Complement — false**, and it does not even typecheck comfortably: $f(\overline A)$ and $\overline{f(A)}$ can overlap in any way at all.

Why the difference? Collisions. Two inputs sharing an output make the image forget which one it came from, and intersection is the operation that notices. Pre-images never face this, because collecting *everything* that lands somewhere loses nothing.
</Warning>

<Aside>
There is a name for a map that preserves $\cap$, $\cup$ and complement, and the theorem of this module is that $f^{-1}$ is always one while the forward image is usually not.

Notice the direction, because it is the surprising part. The function $f$ runs $S \to T$, but the operator $f^{-1}$ that behaves well runs the other way, from subsets of $T$ to subsets of $S$. **The well-behaved construction goes backwards.**

That is not a quirk of this example. A great deal of mathematics is organised around structure-preserving maps that reverse direction, and this is the first one you meet — which is a reason to notice it now, even though nothing here depends on it.
</Aside>
