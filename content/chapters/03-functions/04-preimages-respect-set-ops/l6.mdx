Pre-images pass straight through every set operation:

$$
f^{-1}(B\cup C) = f^{-1}(B)\cup f^{-1}(C)
$$

*So the line reads: the things landing in $B$ or $C$ are exactly the things landing in $B$, together with the things landing in $C$.*

$$
f^{-1}(B\cap C) = f^{-1}(B)\cap f^{-1}(C)
$$

*So the line reads: the things landing in both $B$ and $C$ are exactly the things that land in $B$ and also land in $C$.*

$$
f^{-1}(\overline{B}) = \overline{f^{-1}(B)}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: tracing back respects pooling, overlap and outside-of, all three exactly.*

Three identities, no hypotheses on $f$. Images do not behave this well, and the contrast is the content of the module.

## The pivot

Everything follows from one line:

$$
s \in f^{-1}(B) \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad f(s)\in B.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when". So the line reads: $s$ is in the pre-image of $B$ exactly when $f$ sends $s$ into $B$ — the one line every proof here unfolds*

Membership in a pre-image is *directly* a condition on $f(s)$ — no quantifier (the *for every* and *there is* words, §1.10), no search. Because of that, whatever $\cup$, $\cap$, and complement do to $B$ transfers unchanged, and the proofs are short.

## Proof of one

> **Proposition.** $f^{-1}(B\cap C) = f^{-1}(B)\cap f^{-1}(C)$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">The pivot equivalence, and the definitions of $\cap$ on both sides.</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion (module 2.6).</div>
  <div slot="how">Each direction is one application of the pivot followed by unpacking $\cap$. In fact the chain is reversible at every step — but write it as two inclusions, so the reversibility is visible rather than assumed.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $s \in f^{-1}(B\cap C)$. Then $f(s)\in B\cap C$, so $f(s)\in B$ and $f(s)\in C$.

From $f(s)\in B$ we get $s \in f^{-1}(B)$; from $f(s)\in C$ we get $s\in f^{-1}(C)$. So $s \in f^{-1}(B)\cap f^{-1}(C)$.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $s \in f^{-1}(B)\cap f^{-1}(C)$. Then $s \in f^{-1}(B)$, so $f(s)\in B$; and $s \in f^{-1}(C)$, so $f(s)\in C$.

Therefore $f(s)\in B\cap C$, giving $s \in f^{-1}(B\cap C)$.
</Proof>

**No property of $f$ was used** — not injectivity, not surjectivity, nothing. The identity holds for every function there is, which is unusual and worth noticing.

The other two identities have the same shape. Try the complement one; it is four lines, and the only step that needs care is that $s \in \overline{f^{-1}(B)}$ means $s \in S$ and $f(s)\notin B$.

## Images do not cooperate

$$
f(A\cup A') = f(A)\cup f(A') \qquad \checkmark
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with". So the line reads: images do respect pooling exactly, with no condition attached*

$$
f(A\cap A') \subseteq f(A)\cap f(A') \qquad \text{— containment only}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: but with overlapping only containment holds: the image of an overlap can be smaller than the overlap of the images*

**Counterexample.** Let $f(x)=x^2$, $A = \{2\}$, $A'=\{-2\}$.

Then $A \cap A' = \emptyset$, so $f(A\cap A') = \emptyset$. But $f(A) = \{4\}$ and $f(A')=\{4\}$, so $f(A)\cap f(A') = \{4\}$.

$$\emptyset \subsetneq \{4\}.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subsetneq$ is "sits strictly inside", $\emptyset$ is the empty collection. So the line reads: the empty set sits strictly inside $\{4\}$ — the two differ, which is what makes this a genuine counterexample to equality*

The right side found $4$ in both images, from *different* sources. The left side needed one source in both sets, and there was none.

<Warning title="Why the asymmetry exists">
Compare the two membership conditions:

$$s \in f^{-1}(B) \iff f(s)\in B \qquad\text{(nothing to search for)}$$

*So the line reads: $s$ traces back from $B$ exactly when its own output lands in $B$ — one thing to check, not a search.*

$$t \in f(A) \iff \exists a\in A,\ f(a)=t \qquad\text{(existential)}$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subsetneq$ is "sits strictly inside", $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\emptyset$ is the empty collection. So the line reads: the difference is the search: tracing back needs none, going forwards needs one, and that is the whole asymmetry.*

The image condition hides an $\exists$, and $\exists$ distributes over $\vee$ but **not** over $\wedge$ — "something is in $A$ or $A'$" splits cleanly; "something is in both" does not follow from "something is in each."

That is why union works for images and intersection does not. The failure is a quantifier fact from Chapter 1, not a fact about functions.
</Warning>

Complements are worse. In general $f(\overline A)$ and $\overline{f(A)}$ are unrelated — neither contains the other. For $f(x)=x^2$ and $A = [0,\infty)$: $f(\overline A) = (0,\infty)$ while $\overline{f(A)} = (-\infty, 0)$, and these are disjoint.

## When images do cooperate

If $f$ is **injective** (§3.5 — no two inputs share an output), all three image identities hold. The reason is exactly the counterexample above: injectivity forbids two different sources sharing an output, so "found in both images" forces "found from one common source."

Rigid motions are injective (§3.5: no two inputs share an output) — a reflection cannot send two distinct points to the same place — so for a reflection $\sigma$:
$$\sigma(A\cap A') = \sigma(A)\cap\sigma(A').$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: for a rigid motion the two sides do agree, because a rigid motion never sends two points to the same place*

**This is why transformation proofs work.** When your course says "the image of the intersection of two figures is the intersection of their images," it is relying on a property that holds for rigid motions and fails for functions in general. Dilations preserve it too; projections do not, and that is why projecting a three-dimensional figure onto a plane loses information.

<Aside>
There is a name for this. A rule that carries overlap to overlap, pooling to pooling, and outside to outside is as well behaved as such a rule can be, and the theorem of this module is:

> For any $f: S\to T$, tracing back carries pooling to pooling, overlap to overlap, and outside to outside. Going forwards does not.

Note the direction: $f$ runs $S\to T$, and $f^{-1}$ runs $\mathcal{P}(T)\to\mathcal{P}(S)$ — backwards. Structure-preserving maps very often run backwards, and a great deal of mathematics is organized around that observation.

That asymmetry is the reason later courses state so many conditions by tracing back rather than going forwards: the traced-back version respects every operation, so proofs about it never need a special case.
</Aside>

## Using it

The identities let you rewrite conditions before working with them.

Suppose $T$ is a transformation of the plane and you want the set of points whose image lies inside the unit circle **and** above the $x$-axis. That set is
$$T^{-1}(D \cap H) = T^{-1}(D)\cap T^{-1}(H),$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: pulling back an overlap gives the overlap of the pull-backs — the same good behaviour, in the language of transformations*

so you may handle the two conditions separately and intersect at the end. The identity guarantees the two routes agree.

Attempting the same split with images would be an error, and the counterexample above is what you would run into.
