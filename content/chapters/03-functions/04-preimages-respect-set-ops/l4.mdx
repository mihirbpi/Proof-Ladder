Let $f : S \to T$, and let $U, V \subseteq T$, with complements taken in $T$ on the right of $f^{-1}$ and in $S$ on the left.

$$
f^{-1}(U \cup V) = f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V) \tag{P1}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with". So the line reads: **tracing back from "$U$ or $V$" gives the same inputs as tracing back from each and pooling the results.*

$$
f^{-1}(U \cap V) = f^{-1}(U) \cap f^{-1}(V) \tag{P2}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

$$
f^{-1}\big(\overline{U}\big) = \overline{f^{-1}(U)} \tag{P3}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

The pre-image operator commutes with every Boolean set operation. No hypotheses on $f$ are needed — not injectivity, not surjectivity, nothing.

## The pivot

All three proofs run on one equivalence, from module 3.3:
$$
x \in f^{-1}(U) \iff f(x) \in U. \tag{1}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when".*

(The notation $f(x)$ for the output on input $x$ is §3.1's.)

Everything else is the definitions of $\cup$, $\cap$, complement, and the corresponding logical facts. Nothing about $f$ is used beyond (1), which is why the theorem is so unconditional.

## Proof of (P1)

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">(1), and $y \in U \cup V \iff (y \in U) \vee (y \in V)$.</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion (module 2.6).</div>
  <div slot="how">Chase an arbitrary $x$. Apply (1) to move from $x$-statements to $f(x)$-statements, unfold the union, and apply (1) twice more to move back. Every step is a biconditional, so both inclusions come at once — but we will write both directions out, since the case structure is worth seeing.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Let $x \in f^{-1}(U \cup V)$ be arbitrary. By (1), $f(x) \in U \cup V$, so $f(x) \in U$ or $f(x) \in V$. That is a disjunction, so split into cases.

*Case 1: $f(x) \in U$.* Then by (1), $x \in f^{-1}(U)$, hence $x \in f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V)$.

*Case 2: $f(x) \in V$.* Then by (1), $x \in f^{-1}(V)$, hence again $x$ lies in the union.

The cases are exhaustive.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V)$, and split again.

*Case 1: $x \in f^{-1}(U)$.* Then $f(x) \in U \subseteq U \cup V$, so $f(x) \in U \cup V$, so $x \in f^{-1}(U \cup V)$ by (1).

*Case 2: $x \in f^{-1}(V)$.* Identically, with $V$ in place of $U$.

Both inclusions hold, so (P1) is proved.
</Proof>

(P2) is the same argument with $\wedge$ replacing $\vee$ and no case splits needed — conjunctions are cheaper to use than disjunctions (module 2.6).

## Proof of (P3)

<Proof>
Let $x \in S$ be arbitrary. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
x \in f^{-1}\big(\overline{U}\big)
&\iff f(x) \in \overline{U} &&\text{by (1)}\\
&\iff f(x) \notin U &&\text{definition of complement in } T\\
&\iff \neg\big(x \in f^{-1}(U)\big) &&\text{by (1)}\\
&\iff x \in \overline{f^{-1}(U)} &&\text{definition of complement in } S.
\end{aligned}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\neg$ is "not", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

Every step is a biconditional, so the sets are equal.
</Proof>

Note where the two complements are taken: the one inside is relative to $T$, the one outside relative to $S$. Module 2.5 insisted the universe be named, and this is why.

## Images do not behave

The forward direction fails, and it is worth knowing exactly how.

$$
f(A \cup B) = f(A) \cup f(B) \quad \text{always};
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with". So the line reads: going forwards respects pooling always, but respects overlap only one way — the image of an overlap can be smaller than the overlap of the images.*

$$
f(A \cap B) \subseteq f(A) \cap f(B), \quad \text{and the inclusion can be strict.}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

**Counterexample.** Let $f : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = x^2$, with $A = \{2\}$ and $B = \{-2\}$. Then $A \cap B = \emptyset$, so $f(A \cap B) = \emptyset$. But $f(A) = f(B) = \{4\}$, so $f(A) \cap f(B) = \{4\}$. Strict.

The reason is visible in the definitions. Membership in a pre-image is $f(x) \in U$ — no quantifier. Membership in an image is $\exists a \in A,\ f(a) = t$ — an existential. Existentials distribute over $\vee$ but not over $\wedge$, so images commute with union and not with intersection.

Complement fails even more badly: $f(\overline{A})$ and $\overline{f(A)}$ are unrelated in general.

<Warning title="Choose the direction that works">
When you have a choice, state conditions with pre-images. This is not stylistic advice. Later courses define several important properties this way — *tracing back always lands you somewhere well behaved* — precisely because tracing back is the operation that respects everything. Stating the same idea forwards gives a different and much rarer property.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Equality in $f(A \cap B) = f(A) \cap f(B)$ for all $A, B$ holds exactly when $f$ is injective — module 3.5's property, arriving early. All the defects of the image operator are defects of non-injectivity, and all of them vanish for bijections.
</Aside>
