Throughout, $f$ is a function from $S$ to $T$, and $U$ and $V$ are collections of targets. Outsides on the target side are taken inside $T$; outsides on the input side, inside $S$.

$$
f^{-1}(U \cup V) = f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V) \tag{P1}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with". So the line reads: **tracing back from "$U$ or $V$" gives the same inputs as tracing back from each and pooling the results.*

$$
f^{-1}(U \cap V) = f^{-1}(U) \cap f^{-1}(V) \tag{P2}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: tracing back from an overlap gives the overlap of the two traced-back sets.*

$$
f^{-1}\big(\overline{U}\big) = \overline{f^{-1}(U)} \tag{P3}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: the bar on top is "everything outside".*

Tracing back passes straight through every one of the set operations — pooling, overlapping, outsides. Nothing has to be true of $f$ for this to work — not injectivity, not surjectivity, nothing.

## The pivot

All three proofs run on a single sentence from module 3.3:
$$
x \in f^{-1}(U) \iff f(x) \in U. \tag{1}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when". So the line reads: a thing is in the traced-back set for $U$ exactly when $f$ sends it into $U$.*

(The notation $f(x)$ for the output on input $x$ is §3.1's.)

Everything else is the meaning of pooling, overlapping and outsides, plus the matching facts about *and*, *or* and *not*. **Nothing whatever about $f$ is used beyond (1)** — which is why these hold for every function there is, with no conditions attached.

## Proof of (P1)

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">Sentence (1), and what pooling means: landing in the pool is landing in one of them **or** the other.</div>
  <div slot="want">Double inclusion (module 2.6).</div>
  <div slot="how">Chase a single $x$, assuming nothing about it. Apply (1) to move from $x$-statements to $f(x)$-statements, unfold the union, and apply (1) twice more to move back. Every step is an if-and-only-if statement, so both inclusions come at once — but we will write both directions out, since the case structure is worth seeing.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
**($\subseteq$)** Take any $x \in f^{-1}(U \cup V)$. By (1), $f(x) \in U \cup V$, so $f(x) \in U$ or $f(x) \in V$. That is a disjunction, so split into cases.

*Case 1: $f(x) \in U$.* Then by (1), $x \in f^{-1}(U)$, so $x \in f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V)$.

*Case 2: $f(x) \in V$.* Then by (1), $x \in f^{-1}(V)$, so again $x$ lies in the union.

The cases are exhaustive.

**($\supseteq$)** Let $x \in f^{-1}(U) \cup f^{-1}(V)$, and split again.

*Case 1: $x \in f^{-1}(U)$.* Then $f(x) \in U \subseteq U \cup V$, so $f(x) \in U \cup V$, so $x \in f^{-1}(U \cup V)$ by (1).

*Case 2: $x \in f^{-1}(V)$.* Identically, with $V$ in place of $U$.

Both inclusions hold, so (P1) is proved.
</Proof>

(P2) is the same argument with *and* in place of *or*, and it needs no case split at all — an *and* hands you both halves, while an *or* makes you consider each in turn (module 2.6).

## Proof of (P3)

<Proof>
Take any $x \in S$. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
x \in f^{-1}\big(\overline{U}\big)
&\iff f(x) \in \overline{U} &&\text{by (1)}\\
&\iff f(x) \notin U &&\text{definition of complement in } T\\
&\iff \neg\big(x \in f^{-1}(U)\big) &&\text{by (1)}\\
&\iff x \in \overline{f^{-1}(U)} &&\text{definition of complement in } S.
\end{aligned}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\notin$ is "is not in", $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\neg$ is "not", the bar on top is "everything outside". So the line reads: a thing traces back from the outside of $U$ exactly when its output misses $U$ — which is exactly when it is **not** in the traced-back set for $U$.*

Every step is an if-and-only-if statement, so the sets are equal.
</Proof>

Note where the two complements are taken: the one inside is relative to $T$, the one outside relative to $S$. Module 2.5 insisted the universe be named, and this is why.

## Images do not behave

The forward direction fails, and it is worth knowing exactly how.

$$
f(A \cup B) = f(A) \cup f(B) \quad \text{always};
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with". So the line reads: going forwards respects pooling always, but respects overlap only one way — the image of an overlap can be smaller than the overlap of the images.*

$$
f(A \cap B) \subseteq f(A) \cap f(B), \quad \text{and the inclusion can be strict.}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\cap$ is "overlapped with". So the line reads: the image of an overlap sits inside the overlap of the images, and it can be strictly smaller.*

### A counterexample

Take squaring, with $A = \{2\}$ and $B = \{-2\}$.

Those two collections share nothing, so their overlap is empty, and the image of an empty collection is empty. But each of them has image $\{4\}$ — so the overlap of the images is $\{4\}$, not empty. The two sides genuinely differ.

The reason is visible in the two definitions. Being in a traced-back set says one thing about **your own** output — no searching involved. Being in an image says *there is some input in $A$ that lands here* — a there-is claim, and there-is claims survive an *or* but not an *and*, so images pass through union and not through intersection.

Complement fails even more badly: $f(\overline{A})$ and $\overline{f(A)}$ are unrelated in general.

<Warning title="Choose the direction that works">
When you have a choice, state conditions with pre-images. This is not stylistic advice. Later courses define several important properties this way — *tracing back always lands you somewhere well behaved* — exactly because tracing back is the operation that respects everything. Stating the same idea forwards gives a different and much rarer property.
</Warning>

<Aside>
The image of an overlap equals the overlap of the images, for every $A$ and $B$, exactly when $f$ is injective — module 3.5's property, arriving early. All the defects of the image operator are defects of non-injectivity, and all of them vanish for bijections.
</Aside>
