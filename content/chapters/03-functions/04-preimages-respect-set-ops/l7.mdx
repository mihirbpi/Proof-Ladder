> **Theorem.** Let $f : S \to T$. For all $U, V \subseteq T$ and every family $\{U_i\}_{i \in I}$ of subsets of $T$:
> $$
> f^{-1}\Big(U_1 \cup \cdots \cup U_n\Big) = f^{-1}(U_1) \cup \cdots \cup f^{-1}(U_n),
> \quad
> f^{-1}\Big(U_1 \cap \cdots \cap U_n\Big) = f^{-1}(U_1) \cap \cdots \cap f^{-1}(U_n),
> \quad
> f^{-1}\big(\overline U\big) = \overline{f^{-1}(U)}.
> $$

No hypotheses on $f$. The pre-image operator $f^{-1} : \mathcal{P}(T) \to \mathcal{P}(S)$ preserves every operation of the chapter — union, intersection, complement — and it also preserves $\emptyset$, $T \mapsto S$, and inclusions.

## The pivot

Every proof reduces to
$$
x \in f^{-1}(U) \iff f(x) \in U, \tag{1}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when".*

which is the definition. (1) contains **no quantifier**, and that is the entire reason the theorem holds unconditionally.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">(1); and that union, intersection and complement unfold into *there exists*, *for all* and *not*.</div>
  <div slot="want">Three set equalities.</div>
  <div slot="how">Chase an arbitrary $x \in S$ through a chain of if-and-only-if statements: apply (1), unfold the set operation into a logical one, then apply (1) pointwise to move back. Because every link is an equivalence, one chain proves both inclusions.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $x \in S$ be arbitrary.

*Unions.*
$$
x \in f^{-1}\Big(U_1 \cup \cdots \cup U_n\Big)
\iff f(x) \in U_1 \cup \cdots \cup U_n
\iff \exists i,\ f(x) \in U_i
\iff \exists i,\ x \in f^{-1}(U_i)
\iff x \in f^{-1}(U_1) \cup \cdots \cup f^{-1}(U_n).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\cup$ is "pooled with".*

*Intersections.* Identical with $\forall$ in place of $\exists$.

*Complements.*
$$
x \in f^{-1}(\overline U)
\iff f(x) \in \overline U
\iff \neg\big(f(x) \in U\big)
\iff \neg\big(x \in f^{-1}(U)\big)
\iff x \in \overline{f^{-1}(U)}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when", $\neg$ is "not", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

Each chain consists of if-and-only-if statements, so each proves both inclusions.
</Proof>

Two features worth noting. The proofs work for arbitrary index sets $I$, since the quantifiers $\exists i$ and $\forall i$ never had to be finite. And the complements are taken in different universes — $T$ inside, $S$ outside — which is exactly why §2.5 insisted the universe be named.

## The image operator, and its failures

$$
f\Big(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_n\Big) = f(A_1) \cup \cdots \cup f(A_n) \quad\text{always},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with".*

$$
f(A \cap B) \subseteq f(A) \cap f(B),\qquad
f\big(\overline A\big) \text{ and } \overline{f(A)} \text{ unrelated.}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\cap$ is "overlapped with", the bar on top is "everything outside".*

The mechanism is visible in the unfoldings. $t \in f(A) \iff \exists a \in A,\ f(a) = t$. *There exists* quantifiers commute with $\vee$ (so with unions) but not with $\wedge$: from $\exists a \in A,\ f(a)=t$ and $\exists b \in B,\ f(b)=t$ one cannot conclude that a *single* element of $A \cap B$ maps to $t$.

**Counterexample.** $f(x)=x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$, $A = \{2\}$, $B = \{-2\}$: $f(A \cap B) = \emptyset$ but $f(A) \cap f(B) = \{4\}$.

> **Proposition.** $f(A \cap B) = f(A) \cap f(B)$ for all $A, B \subseteq S$ if and only if $f$ is injective.

<Proof>
(backward) Suppose $f$ injective. The inclusion $\subseteq$ always holds. For $\supseteq$: let $t \in f(A) \cap f(B)$, so $t = f(a) = f(b)$ with $a \in A$, $b \in B$. Injectivity gives $a = b$, so this common element lies in $A \cap B$ and $t \in f(A \cap B)$.

(forward) Suppose $f$ is not injective, say $f(a) = f(b) = t$ with $a \neq b$. Take $A = \{a\}$, $B = \{b\}$. Then $f(A \cap B) = f(\emptyset) = \emptyset$ while $f(A)\cap f(B) = \{t\} \neq \emptyset$.
</Proof>

So every misbehavior of the image operator is a symptom of non-injectivity, and all of it disappears for bijections.

## The one inequality behind all of it

For all $A \subseteq S$, $U \subseteq T$:
$$
f(A) \subseteq U \iff A \subseteq f^{-1}(U).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when".*

Read left to right, it says an image is constrained *from above* by $U$; read right to left, a pre-image is constrained *from below* by $A$. The two operators are locked together by that one statement.

This single fact explains the whole module. The operator standing on the **left** of such a pairing always preserves unions and may fail on intersections — which is exactly the behavior of $f$. The operator standing on the **right** always preserves intersections. And $f^{-1}$ stands on the right here, while standing on the left in a second such pairing — so it preserves both. Being on both sides at once is a strong condition, and it is why $f^{-1}$ is the well-behaved operator.

<Warning title="State conditions with pre-images">
This is why continuity reads
$$
f \text{ continuous} \iff f^{-1}(\text{open}) \text{ is open},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when".*

not $f(\text{open})$ open. The latter defines *open maps*, a genuinely different class: $f(x) = x^2$ is continuous but not open, since $f\big((-1,1)\big) = [0,1)$.

The same choice recurs wherever a definition has to survive composition — pre-images compose cleanly, and that is exactly why the definition is stated with them.
</Warning>

*A reminder of the symbols in play: $\circ$ reads "after".*

<Aside>
Because $f^{-1}$ reverses direction — a map $S \to T$ induces $\mathcal{P}(T) \to \mathcal{P}(S)$ — the assignment reverses direction. That reversal is the whole story of this module. It is worth noticing how often the backwards direction is the better behaved one — tracing back from the outputs respects everything, while pushing forward from the inputs does not.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

The asymmetry proved here is the reason several later definitions are stated with $f^{-1}$ rather than $f$, and it is worth recognizing when you meet them.

**Solving systems with several conditions.** The set of $\mathbf{x}$ satisfying $A\mathbf{x}\in B$ **and** $A\mathbf{x}\in C$ is
$$A^{-1}(B\cap C) = A^{-1}(B)\cap A^{-1}(C),$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cap$ is "overlapped with".*

so the conditions may be handled separately and intersected. Attempting the same split on the output side fails, for the counterexample this module gives.

That identity is what licenses solving a system one equation at a time and intersecting the solution sets — a procedure so familiar it is easy to miss that it needs justifying.

**In calculus, the pre-image formulation is what makes domain computations composable.** The domain of the composite $g\circ f$ (§3.7) is $\{x\in\operatorname{dom} f : f(x)\in\operatorname{dom} g\} = \operatorname{dom} f \cap f^{-1}(\operatorname{dom} g)$, and computing it for a three-fold composition is a matter of intersecting pre-images — which works exactly because pre-images distribute.

**The general lesson:** when a definition could be stated with images or with pre-images, it will be stated with pre-images, and this module is why.

</WhereThisGoes>
