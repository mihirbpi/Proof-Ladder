You now have two chapters' worth of tools. Chapter 2 gave you pooling, overlapping, and outsides. Module 3.3 gave you pre-images. What happens when you use them together?

The answer is: they get along perfectly. That is this module.

## The rules

For a function $f : S \to T$ and sets $U$, $V$ inside $T$:

- Trace back from the pool of $U$ and $V$, and you get exactly what you get by tracing back from each and **uniting** the results.
- Trace back from the overlap of $U$ and $V$, and you get exactly what you get by tracing back from each and taking what is in **both**.
- Trace back from *everything outside $U$*, and you get exactly *everything outside* what you traced back from $U$.

In words: **you can trace back before or after doing the set operation, and you get the same answer.**

Take $U = \{1\}$ and $V = \{2\}$.

- Tracing back from $U$ gives $\{a\}$, and from $V$ gives $\{b, c\}$.
- the pool of $U$ and $V$ is $\{1, 2\}$, and tracing back from it gives $\{a, b, c\}$.
- And pooling the two traced-back sets gives $\{a\}$ together with $\{b,c\}$, which is $\{a,b,c\}$. ✓

Now the overlap. $U$ and $V$ have nothing in common, so tracing back from their overlap gives nothing. And taking what is in both traced-back sets gives nothing either. ✓

## Why it works

The reason is a single, small fact — and it is worth saying out loud, because every proof in this area comes down to it.

> Something is in the traced-back set for $U$ **exactly when** its output lands in $U$.

That is the whole engine. Watch it run on the union rule:

- Something is in the traced-back set for the pool when its output lands in the pool.
- Landing in the pool means landing in $U$ **or** landing in $V$.
- Landing in $U$ means being in the traced-back set for $U$; landing in $V$ means being in the one for $V$.
- So it is in one traced-back set **or** the other — which is exactly their union.

Every step was just applying the small fact, or the meaning of pooling. Nothing clever happened, which is exactly why the rule is so reliable.

## A worked example

Let $f$ send each letter to its position in the alphabet.

Take $U = \{1, 2, 3\}$ and $V = \{3, 4, 5\}$.

- Tracing back from $U$ gives $\{\text{a},\text{b},\text{c}\}$; from $V$ gives $\{\text{c},\text{d},\text{e}\}$.
- the overlap of $U$ and $V$ is $\{3\}$, so tracing back from it gives $\{\text{c}\}$.
- And what is in both traced-back sets is also $\{\text{c}\}$. ✓

<Warning title="Forwards is not so lucky">
None of this works as neatly in the forward direction. Take the rule *round to the nearest ten* and the sets $A = \{21\}$ and $B = \{24\}$. They have nothing in common, so their overlap is empty, and rounding an empty set gives an empty set.

But rounding $A$ gives $\{20\}$ and rounding $B$ also gives $\{20\}$ — so *their* overlap is $\{20\}$, not empty!

Going forwards, the overlap can grow. Going backwards, it never does.
</Warning>

<Aside>
This is why mathematicians reach for pre-images even when an image would seem more natural. In the definitions of continuity, of measurable functions, and of many other things, the condition is always stated in terms of tracing back — because tracing back respects all the set operations and $f$ does not.
</Aside>
