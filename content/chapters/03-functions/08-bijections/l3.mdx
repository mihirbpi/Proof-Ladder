Two modules ago you met one-to-one: no output is shared. One module ago you met onto: no output is left out. A function with both properties is called a **bijection**.

For a bijection, every element of the codomain has **exactly one** thing pointing at it. Not zero, not two. Exactly one.

Here is the table that ties the last three modules together:

| Property | Every element of $T$ has… |
|---|---|
| one-to-one | **at most** one arrow |
| onto | **at least** one arrow |
| bijection | **exactly** one arrow |

## Counting without counting

This is the idea that makes bijections important, and it is older than counting itself.

Suppose you want to know whether two piles are the same size. You could count both. Or you could pair them up — and if a perfect pairing exists, the piles are the same size.

That is not a trick. It is what "same size" *means*:

> Two sets have the same size exactly when there is a bijection between them.

For small piles the two methods agree, so nothing is gained. For piles you cannot count, pairing is the only method there is.

## The identity function

The simplest bijection of all sends everything to itself:
$$
\text{id}(s) = s.
$$
This is the **identity function**. It is one-to-one (if $s_1 = s_2$ then... well, they were equal already) and onto (given any $s$, feed in $s$). So every set has a bijection with itself, which is reassuring — a set had better be the same size as itself.

## A finite example

Let $f$ send each letter to its position, with codomain $\{1, 2, \ldots, 26\}$.

<Proof>
**One-to-one.** Different letters sit at different places in the alphabet, so no two letters share a position.

**Onto.** Given any number from $1$ to $26$, count that far into the alphabet and you land on a letter that maps to it.

Both hold, so $f$ is a bijection.
</Proof>

And so there are exactly as many letters as there are numbers from $1$ to $26$. Which we knew — but now we know it *without counting either side*.

## Now something strange

Let $f$ send each counting number to its double:
$$
1 \to 2, \quad 2 \to 4, \quad 3 \to 6, \quad 4 \to 8, \quad \ldots
$$
with the codomain being the **even** counting numbers.

<Proof>
**One-to-one.** If two numbers have the same double, divide by $2$ — they were the same number.

**Onto.** Take any even number. It is $2$ times something, and that something is a counting number, and it doubles to what we wanted.

So $f$ is a bijection.
</Proof>

Read what that says. There are **exactly as many even numbers as counting numbers.**

But the even numbers are *inside* the counting numbers, and they miss half of them! Every odd number is left out!

<Warning title="Infinite piles do not behave like finite ones">
For a finite pile, throwing things away always makes it smaller. For an infinite pile, it need not. You can remove infinitely many things from the counting numbers and be left with a pile the same size as the original.

This is not a mistake in the definition. It is a genuine discovery about infinity, and mathematicians spent a long time being uncomfortable with it before accepting that "same size" simply behaves differently up there.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Once you accept that bijections measure size, you can ask which infinite sets are the same size. Whole numbers and even numbers: same. Whole numbers and fractions: also the same, surprisingly. Whole numbers and all the numbers on the number line: **not** the same — there are strictly more of those, and the proof of that is one of the most famous arguments in mathematics.
</Aside>
