When you reflect a triangle, you do not reflect one point — you reflect the whole figure, and you call the result "the image of $\triangle ABC$."

Your course uses that word constantly and never defines it exactly. This module does, and then supplies the operation that runs the other way.

## Image

For $f : S \to T$ and a subset $A \subseteq S$, the **image** of $A$ is

$$
f(A) = \{\,f(a) : a \in A\,\} = \{\,t \in T : \exists a \in A,\ f(a) = t\,\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\exists$ is "there is some". So the line reads: the image of $A$ is everything $f$ produces as its input runs over $A$.*

Everything you get by applying $f$ to elements of $A$.

Let $\sigma$ be reflection in the $x$-axis and let $\triangle ABC$ have vertices $(1,2), (4,2), (1,6)$. Then $\sigma(\triangle ABC)$ is the triangle with vertices $(1,-2),(4,-2),(1,-6)$ — and *this is the image your worksheet calls $\triangle A'B'C'$*. The prime notation and the image notation name the same set.

The two membership conditions are worth separating:

$$t \in f(A) \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \exists a \in A \text{ with } f(a)=t.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when".*

**An existential.** To show something is in the image, produce a source. That is module 1.12's witness rule.

## The image of the whole domain

$f(S)$ is called **the image of $f$**, or its **range**. It need not be all of $T$:

- $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $f(x)=x^2$ has image $[0,\infty)$, not $\mathbb{R}$.
- The projection $(x,y)\mapsto(x,0)$ has image the $x$-axis, not the plane.
- A rotation of the plane has image the whole plane.

**Codomain and image are different.** The codomain is declared when you write $f : S\to T$; the image is computed, and may be smaller. Module 3.6 is about the functions where they coincide.

<Warning title="f(A) and f(a) are different operations">
$f(a)$ takes an **element** and returns an element. $f(A)$ takes a **subset** and returns a subset. The notation reuses the letter, and you must read the argument to know which is meant.

For the reflection $\sigma$: $\sigma\big((1,2)\big) = (1,-2)$ is a point, and $\sigma(\triangle ABC)$ is a triangle. Capital letters for sets and lowercase for elements is the convention that keeps this readable.
</Warning>

## Pre-image

For $B\subseteq T$, the **pre-image** is

$$
f^{-1}(B) = \{\,s \in S : f(s)\in B\,\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\in$ is "is in", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when". So the line reads: tracing back from $B$ collects every input whose output lands in $B$.*

Everything that lands in $B$. "Which points reflected onto this region?"

$$f(x)=x^2:\qquad f^{-1}(\{4\}) = \{2,-2\},\qquad f^{-1}(\{-1\}) = \emptyset,\qquad f^{-1}([0,4]) = [-2,2].$$

Three outcomes — two elements, none, an interval — and all three are legitimate. The membership condition is a plain conditional, not an existential:

$$s \in f^{-1}(B) \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad f(s)\in B.$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\emptyset$ is the empty collection, $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when". So the line reads: an input is in the traced-back collection exactly when its output is in the target.*

That difference — pre-image is a direct condition, image is an existential — is the reason the next module's asymmetry exists, and it is worth noticing now.

<Warning title="f⁻¹ here does not mean an inverse function">
$f^{-1}(B)$ is defined for **every** function, whether or not $f$ can be undone. $f(x)=x^2$ has no inverse function (§3.9: the rule that undoes it), and $f^{-1}([0,4]) = [-2,2]$ is perfectly well defined.

Two rules keep them apart:

- $f^{-1}$ applied to a **set** is always the pre-image, always defined.
- $f^{-1}$ applied to an **element** is the inverse function, and requires $f$ to be a bijection (module 3.9).

The notation is genuinely overloaded and mathematicians have not fixed it. Read the argument.
</Warning>

## Fixed points

A point with $f(P) = P$ is a **fixed point**, and the set of them is $\{P : f(P)=P\}$. Rigid motions are classified by their fixed sets, which is a fact your course states and this language makes precise:

| Motion | Fixed points |
|---|---|
| Identity | the whole plane |
| Reflection in $\ell$ | exactly the points of $\ell$ |
| Rotation about $C$ (not $360°$) | exactly $\{C\}$ |
| Translation (non-zero) | $\emptyset$ |

The fixed-point set is a pre-image in disguise, and reading the table as a statement about sets is what lets you prove classification theorems rather than say them.

## Two propositions

> **Proposition.** For any $f : S\to T$ and $A \subseteq S$: $A \subseteq f^{-1}(f(A))$.

<Proof>
Let $a$ be an arbitrary element of $A$. Then $f(a)\in f(A)$ by definition of image.

So $a$ is an element of $S$ whose output lies in $f(A)$ — which is exactly the condition for $a \in f^{-1}(f(A))$.

Since $a$ was arbitrary, $A \subseteq f^{-1}(f(A))$.
</Proof>

**The reverse containment can fail.** Take $f(x)=x^2$ and $A = \{2\}$. Then $f(A)=\{4\}$ and $f^{-1}(\{4\}) = \{2,-2\}$, which is strictly larger.

The pre-image picked up $-2$, which was never in $A$, because $f$ sent two inputs to the same output. **Equality holds exactly when $f$ is injective** — and that is module 3.5's subject, arriving here as a consequence rather than a definition.

> **Proposition.** For any $f: S\to T$ and $B \subseteq T$: $f(f^{-1}(B)) \subseteq B$.

<Proof>
Let $t \in f\big(f^{-1}(B)\big)$. By definition of image there is some $s \in f^{-1}(B)$ with $f(s) = t$.

Since $s \in f^{-1}(B)$, we have $f(s)\in B$. But $f(s)=t$, so $t \in B$.
</Proof>

This one can also be strict: with $f(x)=x^2$ and $B = \{-1, 4\}$, the pre-image is $\{2,-2\}$ and its image is $\{4\}$, missing $-1$. Nothing mapped to $-1$, so the round trip could not recover it. **Equality holds exactly when $B \subseteq f(S)$** — a surjectivity condition, module 3.6.

<Aside>
Notice how the two propositions failed in opposite ways.

$A \subseteq f^{-1}(f(A))$ can be strict because $f$ **merges** — two inputs, one output. $f(f^{-1}(B))\subseteq B$ can be strict because $f$ **misses** — some target has no source.

Merging and missing are the two ways a function fails to be undoable, and the next four modules are essentially a study of each one separately and then both together. When you meet injective, surjective (§3.6: every possible output is hit), and bijective, you will have already seen what each rules out.
</Aside>

## Rigid motions have neither problem

For a reflection $\sigma$: no two points share an image, and every point is the image of something. So $\sigma^{-1}(\sigma(A)) = A$ and $\sigma(\sigma^{-1}(B)) = B$ for **every** subset — the round trips are exact.

That is what "you can undo a reflection by reflecting again" means, stated as an equation about sets. It is also why congruence is symmetric in your course: if a rigid motion carries $F$ to $G$, the same motion run backwards carries $G$ to $F$, and module 3.9 proves that the backwards motion exists.
