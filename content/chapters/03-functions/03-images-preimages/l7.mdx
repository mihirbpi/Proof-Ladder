For $f : S \to T$ and $A \subseteq S$, $U \subseteq T$:

$$
f(A) = \{ f(s) \mid s \in A \} \subseteq T,
\qquad
f^{-1}(U) = \{ s \in S \mid f(s) \in U \} \subseteq S,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that". So the line reads: the image is everything actually produced; tracing back collects every input landing in the target.*
and $\operatorname{Im}(f) = f(S)$.

These are operators $\mathcal{P}(S) \to \mathcal{P}(T)$ and $\mathcal{P}(T) \to \mathcal{P}(S)$ respectively. The distinction matters: they act on *subsets*, not on elements, and the pre-image operator exists for every $f$ whatsoever.

Two unfoldings, of which the second is the one that does all the work:
$$
t \in f(A) \iff \exists s \in A,\ f(s) = t,
\qquad
s \in f^{-1}(U) \iff f(s) \in U. \tag{1}
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", $\exists$ is "there is some", $\Leftrightarrow$ is "exactly when".*

The asymmetry is already visible. Membership in an image carries an *there exists* quantifier; membership in a pre-image carries none. That single fact explains everything in §3.4 about why pre-images are better behaved.

## Image and codomain

$\operatorname{Im}(f) \subseteq T$, with equality iff $f$ is surjective (§3.6). The codomain is part of the data of $f$; the image is determined by $f$.

The word **range** is genuinely ambiguous — some authors mean image, others codomain — and should be avoided in careful writing.

Examples:
$$
f : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R},\ f(x)=x^2 \Rightarrow \operatorname{Im}(f) = [0,\infty);
$$
$$
\exp : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R} \Rightarrow \operatorname{Im}(\exp) = (0,\infty);
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

$$
\lfloor\cdot\rfloor : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{Z} \Rightarrow \operatorname{Im} = \mathbb{Z}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\mathbb{Z}$ is the integers, $\mathbb{R}$ is the real numbers, $\Rightarrow$ is "forces".*

## Fibres

The **fibre** of $f$ over $t \in T$ is $f^{-1}(\{t\})$. The fibres split the domain into non-overlapping pieces, one for each value the function actually takes, and together they use up all of $S$ — because each $s$ has exactly one value $f(s)$ — which is precisely well-definedness. So a function *is* a partition of its domain, labelled by elements of its codomain, and the entire remaining chapter is a study of fibre sizes:

| Property | Condition on fibres $f^{-1}(\{t\})$, $t \in T$ |
|---|---|
| injective | $\lvert f^{-1}(\{t\})\rvert \leq 1$ |
| surjective | $\lvert f^{-1}(\{t\})\rvert \geq 1$ |
| bijective | $\lvert f^{-1}(\{t\})\rvert = 1$ |

That $\operatorname{Im}(f)$ is exactly $\{t : f^{-1}(\{t\}) \neq \emptyset\}$ is immediate from (1).

## Worked computations

$f(x) = x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$:
$$
f^{-1}(\{3\}) = \{\pm\sqrt3\},\quad
f^{-1}(\{0\}) = \{0\},\quad
f^{-1}(\{-5\}) = \emptyset,\quad
f^{-1}\big((-2,6)\big) = (-\sqrt6,\sqrt6).
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\emptyset$ is the empty collection.*

In the last, the constraint $-2 < x^2$ is vacuous and only $x^2 < 6$ bites. Note that the pre-image of an open interval is an open interval here. That is not a coincidence: it is the property that continuity is eventually *defined* by, and it is stated with pre-images rather than images for exactly the reasons this module gives.

Floor: $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor^{-1}(\{n\}) = [n, n+1)$. Fibres are intervals; infinitely many inputs share each output.

## The two round trips

The two operators are not inverse but they are related, for all $A \subseteq S$, $U \subseteq T$:
$$
A \subseteq f^{-1}(f(A)),
\qquad
f(f^{-1}(U)) \subseteq U,
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside".*

with equality in the first iff $f$ is injective on the relevant part, and in the second iff $U \subseteq \operatorname{Im}(f)$.

<Proof>
For the first: let $a \in A$. Then $f(a) \in f(A)$, so by (1), $a \in f^{-1}(f(A))$.

For the second: let $t \in f(f^{-1}(U))$. Then $t = f(s)$ for some $s \in f^{-1}(U)$, and $s \in f^{-1}(U)$ means $f(s) \in U$; hence $t \in U$.
</Proof>

Strictness in the first: $f(x)=x^2$, $A = \{2\}$ gives $f^{-1}(f(A)) = \{\pm2\} \supsetneq A$. In the second: $U = \{-5\}$ gives $f(f^{-1}(U)) = \emptyset \subsetneq U$.

Both round trips can fail, and they fail for opposite reasons. $A \subseteq f^{-1}(f(A))$ is strict when $f$ **merges** — two inputs sharing an output, so tracing back picks up a companion that was never in $A$. $f(f^{-1}(U)) \subseteq U$ is strict when $f$ **misses** — some target has no source, so it cannot survive the round trip out and back.

Merging and missing are the only two ways a function fails to be undoable, and §3.5 and §3.6 study them one at a time. The single statement underneath both inclusions is
$$
f(A) \subseteq U \quad\text{exactly when}\quad A \subseteq f^{-1}(U),
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside".*

which is worth checking against the two proofs above — each is one direction of it.

<Warning title="Two meanings of f⁻¹">
$f^{-1}(U)$ for $U \subseteq T$ — the pre-image — exists for every function. $f^{-1} : T \to S$ — the inverse function — exists only for bijections (§3.9).

With $f(x) = x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$: $f^{-1}(\{9\}) = \{-3,3\}$ is defined; $f^{-1}(9)$ is not, since $f$ is not injective.

The notational clash is universal and permanent. Read the argument: a set means pre-image, an element means inverse function.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Images fail to commute with intersection: $f(A \cap B) \subseteq f(A) \cap f(B)$ always, and $f(x)=x^2$ with $A=\{2\}$, $B=\{-2\}$ gives $\emptyset \subsetneq \{4\}$. Equality for all $A, B$ holds exactly when $f$ is injective. Pre-images have no such defect, and §3.4 proves it.
</Aside>

<WhereThisGoes level="l7">

Pre-images are the objects both courses ahead are actually built on, and images are the ones that behave badly.

**In calculus the images are the interesting sets.** Two of the first theorems you meet are both statements about the image of a stretch under an unbroken curve. One says the curve actually reaches a highest and a lowest value on that stretch — its image includes its own endpoints. The other says the image has no gaps: every height in between is hit. Together they say the image of an unbroken stretch is itself an unbroken stretch, endpoints included.

**The two round-trip inequalities have calculus meaning.** $A \subseteq f^{-1}(f(A))$ is strict exactly when $f$ merges — which for $f(x)=x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is why $f^{-1}(f([1,2])) = [-2,-1]\cup[1,2]$, and why squaring both sides of an equation introduces extraneous roots. The extra solutions are the ones the pre-image picked up, and checking them is undoing the merge by hand.

$f(f^{-1}(B)) \subseteq B$ is strict exactly when $f$ misses, which is why $\sin(\sin^{-1} x) = x$ holds on $[-1,1]$ and $\sin^{-1}(\sin x) = x$ fails outside $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$. The two identities are not symmetric, and the asymmetry is exactly the one this module proved.

**Level sets are pre-images.** For $f(x,y)$, the curve $f = c$ is $f^{-1}(\{c\})$, and a contour map is a picture of a family of pre-images. So is a solution set of any equation — which means the objects you have been graphing since algebra are pre-images of single points.

</WhereThisGoes>
