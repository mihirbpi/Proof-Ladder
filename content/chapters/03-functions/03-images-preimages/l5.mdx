The codomain says where outputs are *allowed* to land. It does not say they all get used.

## The image

For $f : S \to T$, the **image** is the set of outputs actually produced:
$$
\operatorname{Im}(f) = \{\, f(s) \mid s \in S \,\} \subseteq T.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside", $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

Take $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$, $f(x) = x^2$. The codomain is all of $\mathbb{R}$, and the image is $[0,\infty)$ — no negative number is ever a square of a real.

$$
\operatorname{Im}(f) \subseteq T \ \text{ always}, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(f) = T \ \text{ sometimes}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\subseteq$ is "sits inside".*

The gap between those two is exactly the subject of module 3.6. For now, notice that the image is computed *from* the rule while the codomain is *declared* — which is why changing the codomain changes the function but never changes the image.

Your Algebra I course called the image the **range**, and unfortunately some books use "range" to mean the codomain instead. The word is ambiguous in the literature; *image* and *codomain* are not, and this course uses those.

## The pre-image

Running the other way, for a subset $U \subseteq T$:
$$
f^{-1}(U) = \{\, s \in S \mid f(s) \in U \,\}.
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

The **pre-image** of $U$ is everything that lands in $U$. With $f(x) = x^2$ again:

- $f^{-1}(\{4\}) = \{-2, 2\}$ — two inputs land on $4$.
- $f^{-1}(\{0\}) = \{0\}$ — one input.
- $f^{-1}(\{-1\}) = \emptyset$ — nothing squares to $-1$.
- $f^{-1}([0,4]) = [-2,2]$.

Three different sizes, from one function.

<Warning title="f⁻¹ here does not mean an inverse function">
$f(x) = x^2$ on $\mathbb{R}$ has no inverse function — module 3.9 explains why. Yet $f^{-1}([0,4])$ is perfectly well defined and equals $[-2,2]$.

**The pre-image exists for every function whatsoever.** No invertibility is required, none is being claimed, and the notation is an unfortunate collision rather than a hint.

Two clues keep them apart:

- The pre-image eats a **set** and returns a **set**: $f^{-1}(U) \subseteq S$.
- An inverse function eats an **element** and returns an **element**.

So $f^{-1}(\{4\}) = \{-2,2\}$ is a statement about pre-images, and it is not an equation about $\pm 2$ being "the inverse of 4". Watch the braces.
</Warning>

## An empty pre-image is not an error

$f^{-1}(\{-1\}) = \emptyset$ is a correct and complete answer. The question *what lands on $-1$?* has the answer *nothing*, and $\emptyset$ is how you write that.

This connects the two ideas:

> $f^{-1}(\{t\}) \neq \emptyset$ exactly when $t \in \operatorname{Im}(f)$.

Being in the image *means* having something land on you. Read left to right it says a nonempty pre-image witnesses membership in the image; right to left, that an element of the image must have a source.

## Images of subsets

The image construction also applies to a piece of the domain. For $A \subseteq S$:
$$
f(A) = \{\, f(a) \mid a \in A \,\},
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\in$ is "is in", the bar $\mid$ is "such that".*

so $\operatorname{Im}(f) = f(S)$, and $f(A)$ is called the image of $A$.

With $f(x) = x^2$: $f([1,2]) = [1,4]$, and $f([-2,2]) = [0,4]$ — the second is smaller than you might guess, because the negative half folds onto the positive half.

<Warning title="f(A) and f⁻¹(U) are not opposites">
It is tempting to expect $f^{-1}(f(A)) = A$. It is false in general.

With $f(x) = x^2$ and $A = [1,2]$:
$$
f(A) = [1,4], \qquad f^{-1}([1,4]) = [-2,-1] \cup [1,2].
$$

*Symbol by symbol: $\cup$ is "pooled with".*

Going out and coming back caught the negatives too. In general $A \subseteq f^{-1}(f(A))$, and the containment can be strict — which happens exactly when $f$ sends two inputs to one output, the condition module 3.5 studies.
</Warning>

## A first proof about pre-images

> **Proposition.** For $f : S \to T$ and $U \subseteq V \subseteq T$: $\ f^{-1}(U) \subseteq f^{-1}(V)$.

<Discussion>
  <div slot="know">$x \in f^{-1}(U)$ means $f(x) \in U$. That equivalence is the only fact about pre-images we have, so it must be the way in.</div>
  <div slot="want">A containment — so an arbitrary-element argument (module 2.3).</div>
  <div slot="how">Unfold membership, use $U \subseteq V$ on the output side, fold back up. Three lines.</div>
</Discussion>

<Proof>
Let $x \in f^{-1}(U)$ be arbitrary. By definition, $f(x) \in U$.

Since $U \subseteq V$ and $f(x) \in U$, we get $f(x) \in V$.

By definition again, $x \in f^{-1}(V)$.

As $x$ was arbitrary, $f^{-1}(U) \subseteq f^{-1}(V)$.
</Proof>

Look at what the proof did: **unfold, work on the output side, fold back.** The middle step was an ordinary fact about subsets, applied to $f(x)$ rather than to $x$.

That pattern is the whole of module 3.4, where the same three-line shape proves that pre-images respect unions, intersections, and complements.

<Aside>
Pre-images are better behaved than images, and this module has already shown the asymmetry: $f^{-1}$ respects containment cleanly, while $f$ can fold a set onto itself and lose information.

The reason is a matter of direction. Asking *what lands here?* has a definite answer for every target set, because you can always collect the sources — even when there are none. Asking *where does this go?* can produce collisions, and collisions destroy structure.

This is why the next module is about pre-images and not images, and it is a genuine piece of mathematical taste: when two constructions look symmetric, check which one behaves.
</Aside>
