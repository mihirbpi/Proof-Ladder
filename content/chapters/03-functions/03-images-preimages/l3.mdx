A machine runs one way: something goes in, something comes out. But you can ask questions in **both** directions, and the two questions have different names.

## The image: what actually comes out

The **image** is the set of answers the machine really produces.

Look at the picture and collect every arrowhead. The arrows land on $1$, on $2$, and on $4$. So the image is
$$
\{1,\ 2,\ 4\}.
$$

Nothing lands on $3$. It sits in the target set, and the machine never produces it, so $3$ is **not** in the image.

## The set you promised, and the set you get

These are two different things, and mixing them up is the usual mistake.

- The **target set** is what you *promised* when you set the machine up. Here that is $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$.
- The **image** is what you actually *get*. Here that is $\{1, 2, 4\}$.

You choose the target set. You do not choose the image — you work it out by looking.

The image is always part of the target set:
$$
\{1,\ 2,\ 4\} \subseteq \{1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4\}.
$$

## Working it out: the alphabet machine

Feed a letter in, and out comes its position in the alphabet. Call the machine $f$, so that $f(\text{a})$ means *what the machine gives back when you feed it* $\text{a}$.

| in | a | b | c | … | z |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| out | 1 | 2 | 3 | … | 26 |

The target set is all the counting numbers. But the image is only
$$
\{1,\ 2,\ 3,\ \ldots,\ 26\}.
$$

Nothing comes out as $27$. Nothing comes out as $100$, or a million. **Same machine, and the two sets are wildly different sizes.**

## The pre-image: tracing back

Now run your finger the other way. Pick something in the target set and ask: *what lands here?*

The answer is called the **pre-image**.

From the picture:

- Trace back from $2$. Both $a$ and $b$ point at it, so the pre-image of $2$ is $\{a,\ b\}$ — **two things**.
- Trace back from $1$. Only $c$ points at it, so the pre-image of $1$ is $\{c\}$ — one thing.
- Trace back from $3$. **Nothing at all points at it.**

That last one is the interesting case, and it is worth saying slowly. Tracing back from $3$ gives you nothing — not a mistake, not a broken machine. It is simply the honest answer, and there is even a set for it: the empty set, $\{\ \}$, from module 2.1.

### The alphabet machine again

For the alphabet machine:

- Trace back from $5$ and you get $\{\text{e}\}$.
- Trace back from $27$ and you get **nothing at all** — no letter sits in position $27$.
- Trace back from the whole set $\{1, 2, 3\}$ and you get $\{\text{a},\ \text{b},\ \text{c}\}$.

## The two directions are linked

Here is the connection, and it is worth remembering:

> Something is in the **image** exactly when tracing back from it gives you **something rather than nothing**.

Both statements say *"this one gets hit."* One says it going forwards, one says it going backwards.

<Warning title="Tracing back is not running the machine backwards">
Tracing back from $2$ gave $\{a,\ b\}$ — **two** answers. A machine has to give exactly one answer for each thing you feed it (module 3.1), so tracing back is not itself a machine.

Tracing back is a question you ask about a machine. It always has an answer, even when that answer is *nothing at all*, and even when the machine is a very messy one.

Module 3.9 sorts out exactly when a real backwards-machine exists.
</Warning>

<Aside>
Tracing back turns out to be much better behaved than going forwards, which is why mathematicians reach for it constantly.

Module 3.4 shows that tracing back gets along perfectly with unions and intersections — and that going forwards does not.
</Aside>
